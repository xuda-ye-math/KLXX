\begin{abstract}
Normalizing flow Boltzmann generators retain a tractable proposal density, but training with forward KL depends on target samples that may be biased or omit modes. A flow can then miss target mass while its observed importance weights give a high effective sample size. We introduce log-ratio variation, a pairwise regularizer of the exact target-to-proposal log-density ratio, and use it to define KLXX. Its three weighting measures address target fitting, candidate mode coverage, and proposal support control without changing the exact target minimizer. We derive an exponential Fisher--Rao bound and a fixed-surrogate error bound for forward KL with the target-weighted term, then place KLXX in an adaptive-staging Boltzmann generator with exact importance reweighting and sharpening of the regularized molecular potential. In the reported model and molecular tests, KLXX improves mode-coverage or stagewise diagnostics, and selected molecular observables agree with independent molecular simulations. Log-ratio variation thus supplies information that a sampled forward KL objective can omit.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec: intro}

Drawing samples from a target distribution $\mu \propto \exp(-U)$ on $\mathbb R^d$ is a recurring problem in molecular simulation, Bayesian inference, and statistical physics \citep{frenkel2002understanding,robert2004monte,landau2014guide}. When $\mu$ is high-dimensional and multimodal, its mass splits among basins of $U$ separated by barriers. Local sampling kernels, including Metropolis--Hastings \citep{metropolis1953equation,hastings1970monte}, Langevin dynamics \citep{langevin1908theorie,leimkuhler2015molecular}, Nos{\'e}--Hoover dynamics \citep{nose1984unified,hoover1985canonical}, and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo \citep{duane1987hybrid}, evolve from the current configuration and can therefore remain confined to one basin over accessible simulation times. When this occurs, finite-time estimates are biased toward the visited basin and give no direct account of the mass that was never reached.

Tempering and particle methods address this problem by connecting a tractable distribution to $\mu$ through intermediate distributions. Parallel tempering (PT) and replica exchange run copies of the system at several temperatures and exchange neighboring configurations, so a basin reached at high temperature can enter the low-temperature ensemble \citep{swendsen1986replica,geyer1991markov,hukushima1996exchange}. Annealed importance sampling (AIS) moves along a bridge of intermediate distributions while accumulating importance weights \citep{neal2001annealed}. Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) uses a similar bridge but resamples and rejuvenates a weighted particle set at each level \citep{doucet2001introduction,delmoral2006sequential}. For a prescribed chemical process, umbrella sampling biases selected reaction coordinates to cross the relevant barriers and reweights the biased samples to recover a free energy profile \citep{torrie1977umbrella,kastner2011umbrella}. Nested sampling instead rewrites the normalizing integral as a one-dimensional quadrature over a likelihood threshold \citep{skilling2006nested}.

Recent work improves PT through three distinct mechanisms. Nonreversible swap schedules and tuned temperature grids accelerate round trips \citep{syed2022nonreversible}; variational-reference methods adapt the reference endpoint and its connecting path \citep{surjanovic2022variational}; and neural transports improve communication between adjacent distributions with limited overlap \citep{zhang2025accelerated}. All three strengthen communication between neighboring distributions and can transmit a discovered basin efficiently, but none removes the dependence on local exploration: a basin that no chain has visited cannot enter the target ensemble \citep{woodard2009conditions,chehab2024provable}. A complementary strategy is to learn a global transport from an easily sampled reference to the target, thereby generating independent, nonlocal proposals.

Boltzmann generators implement this strategy with a learned transport map \citep{noe2019boltzmann}. Let $\mu_0\propto\exp(-U_0)$ be an easily sampled reference distribution, usually Gaussian, and let $G:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^d$ be an invertible normalizing flow \citep{rezende2015variational,papamakarios2017masked,kingma2018glow}. We use the target-to-source convention $G_{\#}\mu\approx\mu_0$. The inverse map then sends independent source draws toward the target, and their pushforward distribution is $\nu:=(G^{-1})_{\#}\mu_0$. Its density is
\begin{equation}
    \nu(y)=\mu_0(G(y))\left|\det J_G(y)\right|,
    \label{eq: pushforward-density}
\end{equation}
where $J_G(y)$ is the Jacobian matrix of $G$ at $y$. Importance weights proportional to $\mu/\nu$ are therefore available on pushforward samples without fitting another density or tracing a stochastic path. These weights enable importance-reweighted estimates and define the effective sample size (ESS), a diagnostic of density agreement on the support reached by $\nu$ \citep{liu2001monte,doucet2001introduction,martino2017rethinking}. The remaining challenge is that a high ESS certifies agreement only on the reached support: a target mode absent from the pushforward samples contributes no observed weight.

The choice of objective determines how this challenge enters flow training. The original Boltzmann generator \citep{noe2019boltzmann} and its variational-inference predecessor \citep{rezende2015variational} minimize the reverse Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence
\begin{equation}
    \text{(reverse)}\quad \KL(\nu\|\mu) = \int_{\mathbb R^d} \nu(x) \log \frac{\nu(x)}{\mu(x)} \D x,
    \label{eq: reverse KL}
\end{equation}
which needs samples only from $\nu$ and evaluations of $U$. Its empirical gradient is therefore accessible, but the divergence is mode-seeking \citep{wainwright2008graphical}: a mode absent from $\nu$ contributes no samples and hence no gradient. A flow can then collapse onto a subset of the target modes \citep{wu2020stochastic,felardos2023designing}. Existing approaches interleave Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) kernels with deterministic layers, encode target symmetries in the architecture, reduce gradient variance near the optimum, or anneal from a smoother potential \citep{wu2020stochastic,kohler2020equivariant,vaitl2022gradients,schopmans2025temperature}. These changes can improve exploration, but their success still depends on whether the resulting sampler reaches each relevant mode.

The opposing objective minimizes the forward KL,
\begin{equation}
    \text{(forward)}\quad \KL(\mu\|\nu) = \int_{\mathbb R^d} \mu(x) \log \frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} \D x,
    \label{eq: forward KL}
\end{equation}
which is mass-covering for exact distributions: it diverges if $\nu$ vanishes where $\mu$ has positive mass \citep{wainwright2008graphical}. Its empirical form, however, needs samples from the target distribution $\mu$ being learned. Existing work resolves this circularity in several ways. Maximum likelihood uses configurations from a reference thermodynamic state \citep{wirnsberger2020targeted}; adaptive flow samplers train on a running Markov chain and use the flow as a global proposal \citep{gabrie2022adaptive}; molecular flows can fit a molecular-dynamics trajectory \citep{klein2023equivariant}; and transferable architectures amortize coverage across related molecules \citep{klein2024transferable}. Persistent chains and coupled-target regression provide other approximations to the missing target expectation \citep{naesseth2020markovian,rehman2026regflow}. Because an empirical loss reflects only the regions represented by its sample source, the mass-covering property of the exact divergence does not prevent empirical mode omission. This tension motivates an objective that retains forward KL while using samples from more than one distribution.

The sampled forward KL leaves three distinct gaps. First, a mode absent from its target surrogate contributes no training points. If the flow also misses that mode, the observed weights can be nearly uniform on the reached support, so the ESS can be close to one even though target mass is absent; we call this a \emph{fake ESS} \citep{noe2019boltzmann,midgley2022flow}. Second, a finite or biased target surrogate distorts the sampled objective, and the fitted flow can inherit that error. Third, target samples give little information where the proposal places excess mass, allowing leakage between modes or into target-poor regions. Addressing one of these failures does not by itself resolve the other two.

To address these gaps, we propose KLXX, a forward KL loss augmented by log-ratio variations; the graphical abstract summarizes it, and Equation~\eqref{eq: KLXX-G-intro} gives its full form. KLXX preserves the tractable target-to-proposal density ratio and exact target minimizer while separating target fitting, candidate mode coverage, and proposal support control. The first two terms of \eqref{eq: KLXX-G-intro}, the sampled forward KL and the target-weighted variation $\X_\mu$, both draw on the target surrogate supplied by annealing. The new third term mixes samples from quench and temper (QT) with pushforward samples: QT contributes candidate modes missing from the surrogate, while pushforward samples expose proposal support and leakage. QT guides coverage rather than estimating physical mode weights or guaranteeing complete discovery.

The closest related method is Flow Annealed Importance Sampling Bootstrap (FAB), which couples AIS and flow training but replaces the forward KL by the $\alpha$-divergence ($\alpha=2$) \citep{midgley2022flow}. Its integrand is proportional to $\mu^2/\nu$, and its exact minimizer also minimizes importance-weight variance. FAB targets $\mu^2/\nu$ with AIS and reuses those samples through a prioritized replay buffer, which must be maintained across training. Related methods insert flows into SMC, anneal toward low-temperature targets, add an inference-time SMC correction, or connect disconnected regions \citep{arbel2021annealed,matthews2022craft,schopmans2025temperature,tan2025sequential,vonklitzing2026constrained}. KLXX instead retains the forward KL and adds nonnegative log-ratio variations. Its additional cost is the QT construction and the variation evaluations, including the extra mixture batch; it uses no replay buffer. FAB's $2$-divergence gives a stronger tail penalty, whereas KLXX obtains coverage information from QT. Section~\ref{subsec: annealing} compares their sampling mechanisms.

Centered log-ratio penalties also arise in log-variance objectives for learned diffusion samplers \citep{richter2024improved} and in log-dispersion regularization (LDR) objectives for Boltzmann generators \citep{schopmans2026ldr}. Although the present work was developed independently of LDR, these connections place our construction within a broader family of log-ratio regularizers. Our target-weighted term differs by pairing the forward KL integrand at two independent target points, which yields the double-integral variation in \eqref{eq: X}. KLXX further evaluates this variation under a mixture of QT and pushforward samples.

A separate line trains a stochastic process rather than a bijection. Controlled stochastic differential equations, denoising diffusions, and flow-matching models learn a drift or velocity that transports the reference toward $\mu$ \citep{zhang2022path,vargas2023denoising,berner2024optimal,nusken2021solving,richter2024improved}. Energy-only variants such as iterated denoising energy matching (iDEM) alternate a sampling loop with a score- or velocity-fitting loop and a replay buffer \citep{akhound2024iterated,woo2024iefm}. Learned nonequilibrium transport augments AIS with a trainable drift, and later work extends this approach to larger many-body systems \citep{albergo2024nets,havens2025adjoint,akhoundsadegh2025progressive}.

These stochastic transports can be more expressive than the invertible architectures used here. Their terminal density, however, often requires per-sample ordinary differential equation (ODE) integration \citep{he2025notrick}. We retain a bijection because the exact proposal density, importance ratio, ESS, and reweighting are central to the objective and final estimator. Table~\ref{tab: methods} summarizes these distinctions.

We now turn from these method-level comparisons to the mathematical definition of KLXX. Define the tractable log-density ratio $z(x):=\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)}$. For a nonnegative density $\omega$ on $\mathbb R^d$, define the \emph{log-ratio variation}
\begin{equation}
    \X_\omega(\mu\|\nu) = \iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d} \omega(x)\,\omega(y)
    \biggl|\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} - \log\frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)}\biggr| \D x\D y,
    \label{eq: X}
\end{equation}
which measures how far $z$ is from a constant on the region weighted by $\omega$. For $\omega=\mu$, $\X_\mu$ averages $|z(x)-z(y)|$ over independent target points, whereas LDR-L1 uses the centered penalty $\mathbb E_\mu|z-\mathbb E_\mu z|$. Jensen's inequality and the triangle inequality give $\mathbb E_\mu|z-\mathbb E_\mu z|\Le\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)\Le2\mathbb E_\mu|z-\mathbb E_\mu z|$. Thus, at the same coefficient, $\KL$+$\X_\mu$ and LDR-L1 are comparable up to a factor of two. This suggests similar training performance, although the objectives are not equivalent. $\KL$+$\X_\mu$ is also not equivalent to LDR-L2: its target-weighted variation is pairwise $L^1$, and its new mixture term uses QT and pushforward samples. Since $\X_\omega$ is nonnegative and vanishes at $\nu=\mu$, adding it to the exact forward KL preserves the target as a minimizer.

The target distribution $\mu$ supplies samples for both the forward KL and $\X_\mu$. The wide-coverage measure $\hat\mu$ supplies candidate-mode samples, while $\bar\nu$ is exactly the pushforward distribution $\nu$ of the current proposal, detached from the differentiation graph. In other words, $\bar\nu$ is a \emph{stop-gradient} copy of $\nu$: it supplies the same samples, but they are held fixed when the loss is differentiated. The combined loss is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}[\nu] = \KL(\mu\|\nu) + \lambda\, \X_\mu(\mu\|\nu) + \X_{\alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu}(\mu\|\nu),
    \label{eq: total-loss}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda,\alpha,\beta\Ge0$. For simplicity, we assume $\alpha+\beta=1$ in this section; Section~\ref{subsec: total-loss} allows arbitrary nonnegative mixture weights. Under this convention, the final term is the log-ratio variation under the mixture distribution $\alpha\hat\mu+\beta\bar\nu$. Its pairwise expectation includes pairs drawn within either distribution and pairs with one sample from each distribution.

To rewrite the loss \eqref{eq: total-loss} in a tractable map-level form, use $\nu=(G^{-1})_{\#}\mu_0$, which implies the exact identity $G_{\#}\nu=\mu_0$. Combining \eqref{eq: pushforward-density} with $\mu(y)=\exp(-U(y))$ and $\mu_0(x)=\exp(-U_0(x))$ gives the following expression for the log-density ratio defined above:
\begin{equation}
    z(y)=\log\frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)} = U_0(G(y)) - U(y) - \log|\det J_G(y)|.
    \label{eq: logratio-z}
\end{equation}
Substituting \eqref{eq: logratio-z} into \eqref{eq: total-loss} yields
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}[G]
    &= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \mu}\bigl[z(y)\bigr]}_{\KL(\mu\|\nu)}
     + \lambda \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \mu}\bigl[|z(y) - z(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)}
     + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu}\bigl[|z(y) - z(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_{\alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu}(\mu\|\nu)} .
    \label{eq: total-loss-G-intro}
\end{align}
KLXX is formally defined by the loss \eqref{eq: total-loss-G-intro} with the default choice $(\lambda,\alpha,\beta)=(1,\frac12,\frac12)$, and explicitly reads
\begin{align}
    \mathrm{KLXX}[G]
    &= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \mu}\bigl[z(y)\bigr]}_{\KL(\mu\|\nu)}
     + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \mu}\bigl[|z(y) - z(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)}
     + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim (\hat\mu + \bar\nu)/2}\bigl[|z(y) - z(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_{(\hat\mu + \bar\nu)/2}(\mu\|\nu)} .
    \label{eq: KLXX-G-intro}
\end{align}
With equal target and mixture batch sizes, KLXX evaluates about twice as many samples per step as forward KL and also requires the QT construction.

Our contributions are as follows. First, we introduce the KLXX objective, whose new component mixes QT and pushforward samples. Pairwise differences remove the unknown target normalizer, and the exact target loss retains $\nu=\mu$ as a minimizer. The target-weighted variation reuses the forward KL batch, while the mixture supplies candidate-mode and proposal support information absent from that batch.

Second, we analyze forward KL with $\X_\mu$ in Fisher--Rao geometry. The regularizer contributes a nonpositive term to the instantaneous KL dissipation in \eqref{eq: combined-rate}. For a fixed biased target surrogate and a regularization strength below the threshold of Theorem~\ref{thm: accuracy}, it also subtracts a nonnegative discrepancy term from the stationary-error bound. These results do not cover the full KLXX loss, the changing empirical surrogate used in training, or discrete neural-network optimization.

Third, we demonstrate that QT can reach target basins outside the regions covered by the source and annealed samples. In the Himmelblau and Sparse tests, the two objectives trained without QT miss remote wells, while both QT-based losses recover every mode; mixing QT and pushforward samples also reduces visible intermodal leakage (Figures~\ref{fig: himmelblau} and~\ref{fig: sparse}). We therefore report ESS together with coverage or a problem-specific mode diagnostic, since uniform weights on reached modes do not imply complete coverage. The clock comparison uses shared stage schedules, whereas the molecular stage schedules are method-specific and single-seed; the results support this mechanism without establishing universal or fixed-compute dominance over forward KL.

Fourth, we build an adaptive-staging Boltzmann generator without a replay buffer. Annealing and QT generate the required samples on the fly, and the MCMC transitions use only derivatives of $U$ without differentiating the flow density. Each stage compares its trained proposal with the identity on the validation set, retains the proposal with higher ESS, and shrinks rejected stage increments. For molecular potentials, an additional continuation sharpens a regularized interaction core toward the desired endpoint. We also bound the error of the sample set this construction returns: Theorem~\ref{thm: propagation} controls the strong error of the flow and resample scheme at rate $N^{-1/2}$ for every bounded observable, with a constant independent of $N$ that depends on the stage maps only through their weight suprema, so the scheme is asymptotically unbiased and its error is removed by enlarging the sample alone. Reading that constant stagewise defines the propagation factors, which quantify how the error accumulates across the accepted stages; the experiments report the computable factor $\hat F_\Sigma$, assembled from the ESS of each accepted stage.

Fifth, we develop the Python packages \texttt{jflows} and \texttt{jflows-md} for normalizing flow Boltzmann sampling with molecular potentials. With a JAX backend and OpenXLA compilation, the reported 22-atom alanine dipeptide run completes all ten accepted training stages in 72.7 minutes of total persisted stage time on one NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090, without an external target dataset or a prescribed conformational landscape.

\section{Fisher--Rao gradient flow analysis}
\label{sec: FR}

For simplicity, this section analyzes only forward KL with the target-weighted regularizer $\lambda\X_\mu$ as a density-space flow. Starting from the full loss \eqref{eq: total-loss}, we omit the mixture term weighted by $\hat\mu$ and $\bar\nu$ and consider
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal L[\nu] = \KL(\mu\|\nu) + \lambda\,\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu),
    \label{eq: loss KL X}
\end{equation}
with fixed $\lambda > 0$. This restriction isolates the nonlocal rank correlation induced by $\X_\mu$ from the additional mixture term built from QT and detached pushforward samples. It does not cover the full KLXX objective or its discrete neural-network optimization.

The metric determines the resulting density dynamics. In the Wasserstein-2 geometry the forward KL is not displacement convex, so the Otto--Villani \citep{otto2000generalization} and Bakry--{\'E}mery \citep{bakry2014analysis} arguments used for the reverse KL do not apply. We instead work in the Fisher--Rao (FR) geometry. We set $\operatorname{sgn}(0)=0$ and assume throughout that the densities are positive and sufficiently regular for the gradient calculations. Writing the $\nu$-mean as $\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle_\nu := \int (\cdot)\,\nu\,\D x$, the FR gradient of a smooth functional $\mathcal F$ and the gradient flow for $\mathcal L$ are
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal F(\nu) = \nu\Bigl(\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu} - \Bigl\langle\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu}\Bigr\rangle_\nu\Bigr),
    \qquad
    \partial_t\nu_t = -\,\mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal L(\nu_t),
    \label{eq: FR-gradient}
\end{equation}
and the mean-subtraction makes $\int \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal F = 0$, so the flow conserves probability mass.

For the loss \eqref{eq: loss KL X}, the gradient flow has the explicit form
\begin{equation}
    \partial_t\nu_t(x) = \mu(x) - \nu_t(x) + 2\lambda \mu(x) S_t(x), \quad
    \text{where~}
    S_t(x) := \mathbb E_{y\sim\mu}\biggl[\operatorname{sgn}\biggl(\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu_t(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu_t(y)}\biggr)\biggr].
    \label{eq: appendix-FR-L-flow}
\end{equation}
The local term $\mu(x)-\nu_t(x)$ is the contribution of forward KL to the FR gradient flow, while $S_t$ is the nonlocal rank correlation: the update at $x$ depends on how $\mu(x)/\nu_t(x)$ compares with the full distribution of importance weights under $\mu$. Since $\mathbb E_\mu[S_t]=0$, this term preserves total mass. Appendix~\ref{subsec: appendix-FR-grad-calc} derives \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-L-flow}.

Differentiating $\KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$ along this flow, and reducing the $\X_\mu$ term by a rank correlation identity, gives the dissipation
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\KL(\mu\|\nu_t) = -\chi^2(\mu\|\nu_t) - \lambda\, \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\!\left[ \left|\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu_t(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu_t(y)}\right| \right],
    \label{eq: combined-rate}
\end{equation}
where $\chi^2(\mu\|\nu):=\int(\mu/\nu-1)^2\nu\,\D x$ is the chi-squared divergence. The regularizer contributes a nonpositive term to the instantaneous KL dissipation. Since $\chi^2(\mu\|\nu_t) \Ge \KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$, Gr\"onwall's lemma yields
\begin{equation}
    \KL(\mu\|\nu_t) \Le e^{-t}\,\KL(\mu\|\nu_0), \qquad t \Ge 0,
    \label{eq: KL-exponential}
\end{equation}
without assuming log-concavity, a spectral gap, or a growth condition on $\mu$. This density-space statement does not imply that forward KL or KLXX trains faster with a discrete optimizer. It also excludes the cost of producing target samples. The added term in \eqref{eq: combined-rate} describes how $\X_\mu$ changes the density flow, not a universal transient speedup.

The convergence result assumes that the outer expectation is taken under the exact target. To isolate surrogate bias, fix a smooth density $\tilde\mu$ and replace $\mu$ by $\tilde\mu$ only in the outer integrals while retaining the exact pointwise log-ratio. This defines
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\mathcal L}[\nu]
=\mathbb E_{x\sim\tilde\mu}\!\left[\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)}\right]
+\lambda\,\mathbb E_{x,y\sim\tilde\mu}\!\left[\left|\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)}-\log\frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)}\right|\right].
\label{eq: surrogate-loss-main}
\end{equation}
We assume that the densities are positive on their common domain and that the displayed KL, chi-squared, and pair integrals are finite. At $\lambda=0$, the stationary density is $\nu^\star=\tilde\mu$, so the residual error is $\KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu)$. The next result shows when $\X_\mu$ improves this stationary-error bound. It is an idealization of one detached surrogate, not a theorem for the changing empirical batches used by the trainer.
\begin{theorem}
\label{thm: accuracy}
Assume $\mu$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $\tilde\mu$ and $\lambda \in [0, \tfrac12)$. Then every stationary density $\nu^\star$ of the gradient flow for the fixed-surrogate loss $\widetilde{\mathcal L}$ in \eqref{eq: surrogate-loss-main} satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \KL(\mu\|\nu^\star) \Le \KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu) - \lambda\!\iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\tilde\mu(y) \biggl|\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu^\star(y)}\biggr| \D x \D y.
    \label{eq: accuracy-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\noindent The subtracted term is nonnegative and is positive unless the displayed importance ratio is constant on the support of $\tilde\mu$. Within the theorem's assumptions, the rank correlation induced by the second term in \eqref{eq: surrogate-loss-main} raises $\nu^\star$ in comparatively underweighted regions and lowers it elsewhere. The experiments use $\lambda=1$, outside the sufficient range $\lambda<\tfrac12$, so the theorem does not certify their finite-training behavior. Appendix~\ref{sec: appendix-FR} gives the variation, rank correlation identity, and proofs.

\section{Flow training with log-ratio variation}
\label{sec: training}

This section turns the abstract loss of the introduction into a training algorithm for the flow map $G$. Let $U_0$ and $U$ be the source and target potentials on $\mathbb R^d$, so that $\mu_0 \propto \exp(-U_0)$ is the samplable reference and $\mu \propto \exp(-U)$ is the target. We parameterize $G:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^d$ so that $G_{\#}\mu \approx \mu_0$ and write $\nu := (G^{-1})_{\#}\mu_0$ for the induced approximation to $\mu$. The loss itself evaluates only the native target-to-source map $G$ and its log-Jacobian; it does not explicitly involve $G^{-1}$. The inverse map $G^{-1}$ nevertheless generates pushforward samples used both to initialize AIS and to evaluate the $\bar\nu$ term; these $G^{-1}$ evaluations are sampling-only, with the sampled coordinates detached from both the inverse-map path and their subsequent log-ratio evaluations differentiable through $G$. We first assemble the training loss and identify its sampling ingredients (Section~\ref{subsec: total-loss}), then supply them in turn: an AIS approximation of $\mu$ (Section~\ref{subsec: annealing}) and the quench and temper construction of the wide-coverage measure $\hat\mu$ (Section~\ref{subsec: QT}). Section~\ref{subsec: eval} gives the ESS and coverage diagnostics used to evaluate the trained flow.

\subsection{Training loss with log-ratio variation}
\label{subsec: total-loss}

By change of variables, the pushforward density is $\nu(y)=\mu_0(G(y))|\det J_G(y)|$, so every term is a functional of the scalar field $z(y)$ in \eqref{eq: logratio-z}. The forward KL is its mean under the target, $\KL(\mu\|\nu)=\mathbb E_{y\sim\mu}[z(y)]$. For the weighting measures used in training, we write $\omega(y)$ for the corresponding density; Equation~\eqref{eq: X} applies to any integrable nonnegative density, and empirical batches approximate its expectations by sample averages. The same formulas apply on a torus or a Euclidean--torus product with the corresponding standard reference volume. Set $s:=\alpha+\beta$. For $s>0$, let $\xi:=(\alpha\hat\mu+\beta\bar\nu)/s$ be the normalized mixture, with any zero-weight component omitted. Since $\alpha\hat\mu+\beta\bar\nu=s\xi$, the double integral in \eqref{eq: X} produces the factor $s^2$ below. The map-level loss is
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}[G]
    &= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \mu}\bigl[z(y)\bigr]}_{\KL(\mu\|\nu)}
     + \lambda \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \mu}\bigl[|z(y) - z(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)}
     + \underbrace{s^2\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \xi}\bigl[|z(y) - z(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_{\alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu}(\mu\|\nu)} .
    \label{eq: total-loss-G}
\end{align}
When $s=0$, the last term is omitted. Equation~\eqref{eq: total-loss-G} is the map form of \eqref{eq: total-loss} after removing the introductory restriction $s=1$; the factor $s^2$ follows from the double integral in \eqref{eq: X} and is required when $\alpha+\beta\ne1$. The default choice $\lambda=1$ and $\alpha=\beta=\frac12$ defines KLXX. The pushforward distribution $\nu=(G^{-1})_{\#}\mu_0$ depends on the trainable parameters of $G$. The barred measure $\bar\nu$ is the same distribution frozen at the current parameters: samples are drawn through $G^{-1}$ and detached from that sampling path, but $z$ at the sampled coordinates is still differentiated through the native map $G$.

The three sampling measures entering \eqref{eq: total-loss-G} address complementary failure modes of forward KL:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{$\mu$: target accuracy.} The term $\X_\mu$ penalizes pairwise log-ratio variation within the target batch. Theorem~\ref{thm: accuracy} gives its density-space bound for $\lambda<\frac12$; the finite-training effect at $\lambda=1$ is tested empirically.
    \item \emph{$\hat\mu$: candidate mode coverage.} QT provides log-ratio comparisons on basins represented in its wide-coverage set, including basins absent from the annealed batch. The melt scale $m_e$ in Algorithm~\ref{alg: QT} controls how broadly QT searches.
    \item \emph{$\bar\nu$: proposal support.} Pushforward samples evaluate the log-ratio where the current proposal places mass. The term is intended to reduce variation in target-poor regions; the experiments test whether this is accompanied by less visible leakage.
\end{itemize}
Adding these nonnegative terms to the exact target forward KL preserves $\nu=\mu$ as a minimizer. The terms change which samples enter the loss without changing that reference solution.

The forward KL and $\X_\mu$ share one target batch. Evaluate $\mathbf z = (z(y_1),\dots,z(y_B))$, use $\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf z)$ for the forward KL, and use $\mathrm{mean}(|\mathbf z - \mathbf z[\sigma]|)$ for $\X_\mu$, where $\sigma$ is a uniformly random batch permutation. This estimator introduces no additional flow evaluation or differentiation because it reuses the same $\mathbf z$ and the same flow differentiation graph; the only added operations are the random permutation and pairwise difference. When $s>0$, the last term evaluates one additional batch drawn from $\xi$, applies the same permuted-pair estimator, and multiplies it by $s^2$. At the KLXX setting, each sample in this additional batch is drawn from $\hat\mu$ or $\bar\nu$ with probability $1/2$.

The loss draws on three measures, but $\bar\nu$ requires no construction beyond source draws, so at most two must be built. The $\bar\nu$ batch consists of pushforward samples $\bar y_i = G^{-1}(x_i)$ generated from source samples $x_i \sim \mu_0$. The inverse-map sampling path is detached, while $z(\bar y_i)$ is then evaluated through the native map $G$ and differentiated normally. An approximation of the target $\mu$ for the forward KL and $\X_\mu$ terms is supplied by the AIS construction in Section~\ref{subsec: annealing}. When $\alpha>0$, a second construction supplies samples of the wide-coverage measure $\hat\mu$ by quench and temper (Section~\ref{subsec: QT}). Throughout loss differentiation, the sampled coordinates and all resampling, mixture, and permutation choices are treated as fixed; gradients pass only through evaluations of $z$ at the resulting coordinates. With equal target and mixture batch sizes, $\KL$+$\X_\mu$+$\X_{(\hat\mu+\bar\nu)/2}$ evaluates about twice as many samples per optimizer iteration as forward KL. This does not imply twice the total training time: constructing $\hat\mu$ also applies quench and temper to the validation set and adds a problem- and stage-dependent cost. Inference uses the same flow architecture for both methods.

Two recurring objectives are $\KL$+$\X_\mu$, with $\lambda=1$ and $\alpha=\beta=0$, and KLXX, with $\lambda=1$ and $\alpha=\beta=\frac12$. The first adds no sampling measure beyond the target batch, while the second draws the two components of its additional batch with equal probability. We report the full KLXX loss as $\KL$+$\X_\mu$+$\X_{(\hat\mu+\bar\nu)/2}$ when it must be distinguished from the other ablations.

We do not train with log-ratio variation alone, because empirical pairwise terms constrain relative log-ratios only on sampled regions and provide no global density-shaping constraint elsewhere. Appendix~D.2 of the LDR study \citep{schopmans2026ldr} shows the analogous failure: training with LDR-L1 or LDR-L2 alone, without a KL term, produces qualitatively incorrect distributions. Accordingly, every $\X$-regularized objective in this paper includes forward KL.

\subsection{Approximating $\mu$ by annealed importance sampling}
\label{subsec: annealing}

Evaluating the $\mu$-expectations in \eqref{eq: total-loss-G} requires samples from $\mu$, which are not directly available. We form a surrogate by drawing pushforward samples $y_1,\dots,y_B$ from $\nu=(G^{-1})_{\#}\mu_0$ and reweighting them. In the theory, the normalized importance ratio is $w=\mu/\nu$. Computation uses the proportional weight
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde w(y)
    := \exp\Big( U_0(G(y)) - U(y) - \log|\det J_G(y)| \Big)
    \propto \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)},
    \qquad y \in \mathbb R^d.
    \label{eq: weight}
\end{equation}
Below, $w_i$ denotes these proportional weights because resampling, normalized ESS, and log-ratio differences are scale invariant. In high dimensions, however, $\mu$ and $\nu$ can differ greatly, so the weights can collapse onto a few outliers. We temper the reweighting through $M+1$ bridge distributions
\begin{equation}
    \pi_m(y) \propto \nu(y)^{1-\frac{m}{M}} \mu(y)^{\frac{m}{M}} \propto ((G^{-1})_{\#}\mu_0)(y)\, \widetilde w(y)^{\frac{m}{M}}, \qquad m = 0,1,\dots,M,
    \label{eq: pi-m}
\end{equation}
so that $\pi_0 = \nu$ and $\pi_M = \mu$. Each level reweights by only $\widetilde w(y)^{1/M}$, reducing the degeneracy of direct importance sampling.
After each reweighting, we resample and diffuse the duplicates with the Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm (MALA) \citep{roberts1996exponential} before the next level.


\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\setstretch{1.2}
\caption{Annealed importance sampling (AIS) for a biased target surrogate}
\label{alg: annealing}
\KwInput{pushforward samples $y_1,\dots,y_B$ from $(G^{-1})_{\#}\mu_0$, target distribution $\mu$, ladder length $M$}
\KwOutput{biased target-surrogate batch $y_1,\dots,y_B$ approximating $\mu$}

\For{$m = 1,\dots,M$}{
    Compute the incremental weights $\widetilde w(y_i)^{1/M}=\exp(z(y_i)/M)$ on $y_1,\dots,y_B$\;
    \tcp{resample}
    Resample from the weighted set, $y_1',\dots,y_B' \gets \mathrm{resample}\bigl(y_{1:B}, (\widetilde w(y_i)^{1/M})_{i=1}^B\bigr)$\;
    \tcp{rejuvenate under the target}
    Diffuse the resampled duplicates by a short MALA chain on $\mu$, $y_{1:B} \gets \mathrm{MALA}(y_{1:B}', U)$\;
}
\end{algorithm}
\mbox{} \\
A standard SMC sampler targeting the path \eqref{eq: pi-m} would rejuvenate at level $m$ with a Markov kernel invariant for $\pi_m$. For $m<M$, a MALA kernel for $\pi_m$ would require $\nabla_y\log\nu(y)$ and hence positional derivatives of the flow density, including $\nabla_y\log|\det J_G(y)|$; at $m=M$, this term disappears because $\pi_M=\mu$. Algorithm~\ref{alg: annealing} instead applies MALA directly under $\mu$, using only $-\nabla U$ in its proposal. MALA preserves $\mu$, but not the intermediate $\pi_m$, so the output is a biased target surrogate. The construction trades intermediate invariance for a sampler that moves resampled samples under the final target. Section~\ref{sec: FR} studies a fixed smooth surrogate as a density-space idealization; it does not model the changing empirical output of Algorithm~\ref{alg: annealing}. Evaluation therefore uses fresh importance weights and coverage diagnostics.

The AIS construction in Algorithm~\ref{alg: annealing} differs from FAB \citep{midgley2022flow}. FAB places the flow inside the chains, and its MCMC transitions target the surrogate $\mu^2/\nu$, whose log-density gradient is
\begin{equation*}
    \nabla_y \log \frac{\mu^2(y)}{\nu(y)} = -2\nabla U(y) - \nabla_y \log\nu(y),
\end{equation*}
and therefore contains the positional gradient of the pushforward density at every MCMC step. FAB trains from a prioritized replay buffer of weighted AIS samples with a staleness correction. Reusing this buffer reduces repeated AIS generation but adds storage, prioritized sampling, and correction work. Here every MALA chain instead targets $\mu$ and uses only $-\nabla U$; the flow enters through the initial pushforward samples and evaluations of $z$. Each gradient step starts from a new source batch, so no replay buffer is kept.

The exploration costs also differ. Relative to forward KL, KLXX adds the QT construction and pairwise log-ratio variation evaluations: $\X_\mu$ reuses the target batch, while the mixture term evaluates an additional batch. The fixed $N$-sample QT set of Section~\ref{subsec: QT} supplies separate coverage information, whereas FAB relies on its AIS budget for exploration. FAB's $2$-divergence gives it a stronger tail-coverage incentive.

\subsection{Constructing \texorpdfstring{$\hat\mu$}{mu-hat} by quench and temper}
\label{subsec: QT}

The last term of the loss \eqref{eq: total-loss-G} uses the wide-coverage measure $\hat\mu$ to improve candidate mode coverage when $\alpha>0$. A finite source batch can omit remote target basins, and annealing initialized from that batch provides no log-ratio comparisons there unless its trajectories reach them.

QT addresses this limitation through three steps. The \emph{melt} scatters the input samples across a wider region, the \emph{quench} drives them toward mode centers with the limited-memory Broyden--Fletcher--Goldfarb--Shanno (L-BFGS) method \citep{liu1989limited}, and the \emph{temper} spreads them under the target around the discovered modes. Together, these steps construct a wide-coverage set that can include remote basins; $\X_{\hat\mu}$ then adds comparisons on basins absent from the annealed batch, strengthening mode discovery beyond annealing alone. Formally, QT is the map
\begin{equation*}
    \hat{\mathcal Y} = \mathrm{QT}(\mathcal Y,U)
\end{equation*}
from the validation set $\mathcal Y=\{x_1,\dots,x_N\}$ and target potential $U$ to the $N$ QT samples $\hat{\mathcal Y}=\{\hat x_1,\dots,\hat x_N\}$. Algorithm~\ref{alg: QT} applies this map once per stage attempt. The output $\hat{\mathcal Y}$ is held fixed as a reservoir during training. At each training step a mini-batch of $B$ samples is drawn uniformly from that reservoir and refreshed by a short MALA chain before entering the $\X_\omega$ computation. We use $\hat\mu$ for the law of this draw-and-refresh operation; its input is the uniform empirical measure on the fixed QT reservoir.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\setstretch{1.2}
\caption{Quench and temper (QT): wide-coverage construction}
\label{alg: QT}
\KwInput{validation set $\mathcal Y = \{x_1,\dots,x_N\}$, target potential $U$, melt scale $m_e$}
\KwOutput{wide-coverage set $\hat{\mathcal Y} = \{\hat x_1,\dots,\hat x_N\}$ forming $\hat\mu$, written $\hat{\mathcal Y} = \mathrm{QT}(\mathcal Y,U)$}
\tcp{melt}
Scatter the samples $x_i \gets x_i + m_e\zeta_i$ with $\zeta_i \sim \mathcal N(0, I_d)$ i.i.d. and melt scale $m_e > 0$\;
\tcp{quench}
Drive each sample toward a mode center of $\mu$ by L-BFGS on $U$, updating $x_1,\dots,x_N$\;
\tcp{temper}
Spread the samples around each mode, $\hat x_{1:N} \gets \mathrm{MALA}(x_{1:N}, U)$\;
\end{algorithm}
\mbox{}\\
On a torus or Euclidean--torus product, periodic coordinates are interpreted modulo their period after the melt and after each MALA proposal. If the QT set contains a basin absent from the annealed target batch, $\X_{\hat\mu}$ supplies pairwise log-ratio evaluations on that basin. QT does not certify complete mode discovery. A large melt scale $m_e$ can bias the QT set toward basins far from the input set. This is intentional: $\hat\mu$ is a coverage measure, not an estimate of target mode probabilities. Adding $\X_{\hat\mu}$ to the forward KL preserves $\nu=\mu$ as a minimizer, while empirical frequencies in the finite QT set affect the optimization path.

\subsection{Evaluation: ESS, coverage, and importance sampling}
\label{subsec: eval}

We first assess the quality of the pushforward samples through two complementary diagnostics. The ESS measures density agreement through the observed importance weights on support reached by the flow. Coverage instead checks whether the pushforward samples reach the regions represented by a fixed QT reference set. A high ESS can coexist with a missing mode, while high coverage does not establish the correct relative density within the reached regions. We therefore report the two diagnostics together.

At evaluation time the trained flow $G$ supplies the pushforward distribution $\nu=(G^{-1})_{\#}\mu_0$ and its tractable density $\nu(y)=\mu_0(G(y))|\det J_G(y)|$. The proportional importance weight $\widetilde w(y)$ in \eqref{eq: weight} can therefore be evaluated on pushforward samples without fitting another density model. Draw $\mathbf y=(y_1,\dots,y_N)$ with $y_i=G^{-1}(x_i)$ and $x_i\sim\mu_0$ independently, and write $\mathbf w=(w_1,\dots,w_N)$ for any common scaling of the corresponding positive weights.

The first diagnostic is the ESS, normalized to $[0,1]$ by dividing the classical ESS by the sample count $N$. For the weight vector $\mathbf w$, define
\begin{equation}
    \ESS(\mathbf w) = \frac{\big(\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i\big)^2}{N \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i^2} \in [0,1].
    \label{eq: ESS}
\end{equation}
The upper bound follows from Cauchy--Schwarz and is attained when all weights are equal. The factor $N$ distinguishes this normalized ESS from the classical count $(\sum_iw_i)^2/\sum_iw_i^2\in[1,N]$ \citep{martino2017rethinking}. A high ESS indicates uniform observed weights only on support reached by $\nu$; a target mode absent from the sample cannot lower it.

To detect such missing modes, we pair the ESS with the coverage diagnostic of \citet{naeem2020reliable}. Let $\hat{\mathbf y} = (\hat y_1,\dots,\hat y_N) \in \mathbb R^{N\times d}$ be the fixed QT reference set produced by Algorithm~\ref{alg: QT}. For each $1 \Le i \Le N$, the $\kappa$-nearest-neighbor radius of $\hat y_i$ (with $\kappa = 5$) is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{NND}_\kappa(\hat y_i;\hat{\mathbf y}) = \inf\Big\{ r \Ge 0 :
    \# \big\{1\Le j \Le N: 0 < |\hat y_i - \hat y_j| < r\big\} \Ge \kappa
    \Big\},
\end{equation}
i.e., the distance from $\hat y_i$ to its $\kappa$-th nearest neighbor in $\hat{\mathbf y}$. The coverage of $\hat{\mathbf y}$ by the pushforward samples $\mathbf y$ is then
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Coverage}_\kappa(\mathbf y;\hat{\mathbf y}) =
    \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf 1\Bigl\{
        \exists  1\Le j\Le N : |y_j - \hat y_i| \Le \mathrm{NND}_\kappa(\hat y_i;\hat{\mathbf y})
    \Bigr\},
\end{equation}
i.e., the fraction of points in $\hat{\mathbf y}$ whose $\kappa$-nearest-neighbor ball contains any of the pushforward samples. A coverage close to $1$ indicates that the finite QT reference set is well covered by the flow, while a coverage well below $1$ exposes missing regions that the ESS alone cannot see. Because the reference set is itself produced by QT, the diagnostic checks only modes found by that reference construction; it cannot reveal a mode absent from the reference set.

After these diagnostics assess the proposal, the same weights used for ESS define the resampling step. Normalize them as $p_i=w_i/\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_j$, resample $N$ times from the pushforward samples with probabilities $p_i$, and apply a short MALA chain under $\mu$ to rejuvenate the resulting duplicates. The resampled samples form an unweighted approximation of the target-weighted empirical measure, while MALA produces fresh configurations and retains $\mu$ as its invariant distribution.

Under the support condition $\mu\ll\nu$ and standard consistency conditions, the target-weighted empirical measure and its resampled particle approximation converge to $\mu$ as $N\to\infty$ \citep{liu2001monte,doucet2001introduction}. The $\mu$-invariant MALA refresh preserves this target limit. If the pushforward samples contain no sample from a mode, however, resampling cannot create one and a short local refresh may not reach it. Coverage therefore remains necessary alongside ESS.

\section{Adaptive-staging Boltzmann generator}
\label{sec: boltzmann}

A single flow can be inadequate when the source-to-target change is too large, so we divide the interpolation into stages. Each stage changes the potential by a controlled amount and trains one flow with the loss in \eqref{eq: total-loss-G}, using user-chosen parameters $\lambda,\alpha,\beta\Ge0$. The KLXX Boltzmann generator is the special case $(\lambda,\alpha,\beta)=(1,\frac12,\frac12)$. Sampling diagnostics determine the stage schedule.

\subsection{Why staging must be adaptive}

Two obstacles prevent a single flow from carrying $\mu_0$ to $\mu$ in one map.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Curse of dimensionality.} In low dimension a unimodal source can often be reshaped into a multimodal target by one map, but as $d$ grows the target landscape develops more basins, separated by higher barriers and occupying a smaller fraction of the source support. A normalizing flow of fixed depth and width can represent only a bounded change of shape, so the deformation it realizes in one training run is limited by its capacity rather than by the training budget. Requesting the whole source-to-target change from a single flow therefore leaves part of the target unfitted in exactly the high-dimensional, multimodal problems of interest. Splitting the interpolation into stages keeps each requested deformation within the capacity of one flow, and the number of stages needed to do so grows with dimension.

    \item \emph{Structural limitation of a diffeomorphism.} A trained flow is a diffeomorphism of $\mathbb R^d$, so the pushforward $\nu$ inherits the connected support of $\mu_0$: the map deforms that support continuously and cannot split it into disconnected components. Mass therefore remains on the paths joining the target basins even when the basins themselves are well fitted, an effect visible between the isolated modes of the Sparse target in Figure~\ref{fig: sparse}. Reweighting and MALA rejuvenation at the interpolating distributions remove the leaked samples at every stage, so the support changes shape gradually across the schedule.
\end{itemize}

The stage schedule cannot be fixed in advance, because we assume no external information about the target. Training uses no target dataset, no reference trajectory, and no prior knowledge of the number, location, or depth of the potential wells. The only available information is oracle access to the target potential $U(x)$ and its gradient $\nabla U(x)$: the interpolating potentials are formed from $U_0$ and $U$ alone, the loss evaluates $U_{t}$ through the log-ratio $z$, the annealing and rejuvenation kernels use $-\nabla U_{t}$ in their proposals, and the quench of Algorithm~\ref{alg: QT} descends on the same potential. Under this restriction the difficulty of a candidate stage cannot be read off the potential before attempting it. The algorithm therefore probes the interpolation parameter: it proposes a stage increment, trains the stage flow, accepts the increment only when the resulting proposal passes a sampling gate on the validation set, and otherwise shrinks the increment and retries. Adaptive staging is thus more than a convenience. It is the mechanism that turns oracle access alone into a stage schedule matched to the difficulty the target actually presents, and it lets easy regions advance in few large stages while hard regions receive as many small stages as they require.

\subsection{Construction of the adaptive stage schedule}

To interpolate from the source $\mu_0 \propto \exp(-U_0)$ to the target $\mu \propto \exp(-U)$, we seek an increasing sequence
\begin{equation*}
    0 = t_0 < t_1 < \cdots < t_K = 1
\end{equation*}
defining the interpolating potential and measure
\begin{equation*}
    U_t(x)=(1-t)U_0(x)+tU(x),
\qquad \mu_t(x)\propto\exp(-U_t(x)),
    \qquad t\in[0,1].
\end{equation*}
Here $t$ is a dimensionless interpolation parameter, not a thermodynamic temperature. The discrete values $t_k$ are stage points, and stage $k$ is the transition from $t_{k-1}$ to $t_k$. At the accepted stage points, write $\mu_k:=\mu_{t_k}$, so $\mu_0$ is the source and $\mu_K$ is the target. Stage $k$ trains a target-to-source map $G_k$, whose inverse induces the proposal $\nu_k=(G_k^{-1})_{\#}\mu_{k-1}$ for $\mu_k$. The number of stages $K$ is selected during the run. The central scheduling choice is the stage increment $\Delta t_k=t_k-t_{k-1}$: a large increment can be hard to fit, whereas a small increment adds stages.

Training $G_k$ requires the target surrogate for $\mu_k$ defined in Section~\ref{subsec: annealing}. Before training, an optional classical SMC calculation can screen the proposed stage increment $\Delta t_k$. Its smallest incremental ESS measures overlap along that candidate stage for the current sample set. This is a difficulty diagnostic, not a mode-coverage guarantee. The screen floor $\tau_{\mathrm{SMC}}$ lies in $[0,1)$, and setting it to zero disables the screen. Throughout this section, the validation set is the sample set carried across stages and used in full for training batches, QT, and stage selection; it is not a held-out test set, and neither QT nor the SMC screen first draws a separately sized subset of it.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
    \setstretch{1.2}
\caption{Adaptive selection of the stage increment $\Delta t_k$}
\label{alg: step}
\KwInput{potentials $U_0, U$, stage point $t_{k-1}$, validation set $\mathcal Y_{k-1} = \{y_1,\dots,y_N\}$ approximating $\mu_{k-1}$, ladder length $M$, SMC ESS floor $\tau_{\mathrm{SMC}} = 0$, shrink factor $\gamma=0.7$, initial guess $t_{\mathrm{init}}$}
\KwOutput{next stage point $t_{k}$, giving the stage increment $\Delta t_k = t_k - t_{k-1}$}

$U_{t_{k-1}} \gets (1-t_{k-1}) U_0 + t_{k-1} U$\;
\tcp{start from the supplied initial guess}
$t_k \gets t_{\mathrm{init}}$\;
\If{$\tau_{\mathrm{SMC}} = 0$}{
    \Return $t_k$\;
}
\While{true}{
    \tcp{run a classical SMC ladder across the candidate stage}
    Run an $M$-level SMC ladder on the set $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$, using the linear potential bridge from $U_{t_{k-1}}$ to $U_{t_k}$\;
    \tcp{evaluate the ladder by its worst per-level ESS}
    Record $\ESS_{\min}$, the smallest per-level ESS over the $M$ ladder levels\;
    \eIf{$\ESS_{\min} \Ge \tau_{\mathrm{SMC}}$}{
        \Return $t_k$\;
    }{
        \tcp{shrink the stage increment toward $t_{k-1}$}
        $t_k \gets t_{k-1} + \gamma\,(t_k - t_{k-1})$\;
    }
}
\end{algorithm}
\mbox{}\\
When enabled, these SMC ladders involve only the potentials $U_0$ and $U$. They use no QT samples, so their per-level ESS can overlook a mode the SMC samples never reach. We therefore use this ESS only to screen a candidate stage; it is a heuristic measure of stage difficulty, not a coverage certificate. The selected-proposal validation in step~(v) of Section~\ref{sec: boltzmann} below supplies the acceptance gate, while QT supplies the separate wide-coverage set when $\alpha>0$. By default $\tau_{\mathrm{SMC}} = 0$, so Algorithm~\ref{alg: step} returns $t_{\mathrm{init}}$ without running SMC. For a positive floor, it returns the first candidate stage along the shrink sequence whose estimated ESS clears that floor. The shrink factor is $\gamma = 0.7$. As $t_k$ approaches $t_{k-1}$, the stage becomes trivial and its ESS approaches $1$, so a floor below $1$ is attainable under repeated shrinking in exact arithmetic.


\subsection{The full training procedure}

Algorithm~\ref{alg: boltzmann} combines the stage-selection rule with the training procedure. It carries a fixed-size validation set $\mathcal Y_k\approx\mu_k$ across stages. Each candidate stage builds its QT set when $\alpha>0$, trains a flow with the selected loss weights, and compares that flow with the identity proposal. If neither proposal reaches the validation ESS floor $\tau_{\mathrm v}\in[0,1)$, the algorithm shrinks the stage increment and retries. The displayed loop ends when $t_K=1$. Practical implementations use an endpoint tolerance and finite caps on retries and stages; reaching a cap returns an incomplete stage schedule.

\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item \emph{Validation set.} Stage $k$ uses the \emph{validation set size} $N$ and the \emph{batch size} $B$, taken at least $2$ so that the permuted-pair estimator has a pair to form. The validation set $\mathcal Y_{k-1}=\{y_1,\dots,y_N\}\approx\mu_{k-1}$ is carried over from the previous stage. Training batches are drawn at run time, $B$ samples per gradient step, from $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$; no separate training set is built.

\item \emph{Adaptive stage selection.} Algorithm~\ref{alg: step} selects the next stage point $t_k$. The first stage starts from $t_{\mathrm{init}}=t_{\mathrm{safe}}=0.2$. Later stages extrapolate the last accepted stage increment, $t_{\mathrm{init}}=\min\bigl(1,t_{k-1}+\Gamma(t_{k-1}-t_{k-2})\bigr)$, with enlarge factor $\Gamma=1.5$. When a positive floor is supplied, the algorithm shrinks the stage increment until the classical SMC ESS on all $N$ samples clears that floor. The selected stage target is $\mu_k\propto\exp(-U_{t_k})$, with $U_{t_k}=(1-t_k)U_0+t_kU$.

\item \emph{Wide-coverage set.} When $\alpha>0$, quench and temper (Algorithm~\ref{alg: QT}) acts on the validation set $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$ under $U_{t_k}$. The resulting $N$ samples $\hat{\mathcal Y}_k=\mathrm{QT}(\mathcal Y_{k-1},U_{t_k})$ form the fixed stage reservoir. The measure $\hat\mu_k$ is the law obtained by drawing uniformly from this reservoir and applying the short MALA refresh used at each gradient step; it supplies the wide-coverage component of the last term in \eqref{eq: stage-loss}. When $\alpha=0$, that component vanishes and the QT construction is omitted.

\item \emph{Training loss.} Each attempt initializes $G_k$ to the identity map and trains it by the general loss \eqref{eq: total-loss-G} specialized to the stage $\mu_{k-1}\to\mu_k$. With the pushforward distribution $\nu_k = (G_k^{-1})_{\#}\mu_{k-1}$ and its frozen sampling measure $\bar\nu_k$, the stage log-ratio is
\begin{equation*}
    z_k(y) = U_{t_{k-1}}(G_k(y)) - U_{t_k}(y) - \log|\det J_{G_k}(y)|.
\end{equation*}
Let $s=\alpha+\beta$. For $s>0$, define the stage specialization of the normalized mixture $\xi$, with any zero-weight component omitted,
\begin{equation*}
    \xi_k:=\frac{\alpha\hat\mu_k+\beta\bar\nu_k}{s}.
\end{equation*}
The stage loss then reads
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}[G_k]
    &= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \mu_k}\bigl[z_k(y)\bigr]}_{\KL(\mu_k\|\nu_k)}
     + \lambda\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \mu_k}\bigl[|z_k(y) - z_k(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_{\mu_k}(\mu_k\|\nu_k)}
     + \underbrace{s^2\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \xi_k}\bigl[|z_k(y) - z_k(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_{\alpha\hat\mu_k+\beta\bar\nu_k}(\mu_k\|\nu_k)} .
    \label{eq: stage-loss}
\end{align}
When $s=0$, the last term is omitted. The $\mu_k$-expectations are estimated from the biased annealed surrogate: a batch $\mathcal X$ drawn from $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$ is mapped to the batch of pushforward samples $\bar{\mathcal X}_\nu = G_k^{-1}(\mathcal X)$, approximating $\nu_k$, and annealed toward $\mu_k$ by Algorithm~\ref{alg: annealing} with $G = G_k$. When $s>0$, a batch $\mathcal X_{\xi_k}$ from $\xi_k$ is formed by drawing from $\hat\mu_k$ with probability $\alpha/s$ when $\alpha>0$, and from $\bar\nu_k$ with probability $\beta/s$ when $\beta>0$; the permuted-pair estimate is then multiplied by $s^2$. This factor retains the general-weight definition in \eqref{eq: X} when $\alpha+\beta\ne1$. The choices $(\lambda,\alpha,\beta)=(0,0,0)$ and $(1,0,0)$ give forward KL and $\KL$+$\X_{\mu}$, respectively; $(1,\frac12,\frac12)$ gives the default KLXX loss.

\item \emph{Validation set update.} Once the candidate $G_k$ is trained, each $y \in \mathcal Y_{k-1}$ is pushed through its inverse to $\tilde y = G_k^{-1}(y)$. This gives the trained set of pushforward samples $\tilde{\mathcal Y}_k^{\mathrm{tr}} = G_k^{-1}(\mathcal Y_{k-1})$ from $\nu_k = (G_k^{-1})_{\#}\mu_{k-1}$, together with proportional importance weights represented by
\begin{equation*}
    w_k^{\mathrm{tr}}(\tilde y) := \exp\bigl(z_k(\tilde y)\bigr),
    \qquad \tilde y \in \tilde{\mathcal Y}_k^{\mathrm{tr}} .
\end{equation*}
The identity proposal keeps $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$ unchanged and has weights $w_k^{\mathrm{id}}(y)=\exp[U_{t_{k-1}}(y)-U_{t_k}(y)]$. We select the pair with the larger ESS over the validation set and denote it by $(\tilde{\mathcal Y}_k,w_k)$, taking the stage map to be $G_k$ or the identity accordingly. If $\ESS(w_k) < \tau_{\mathrm v}$, with default $\tau_{\mathrm v} = 0.6$, we discard the candidate, shrink to $t_k \gets t_{k-1} + \gamma\,(t_k - t_{k-1})$, and repeat steps (iii)--(v) at the reduced stage increment. Otherwise we advance the validation set to $\mathcal Y_k = \mathrm{MALA}\bigl(\mathrm{resample}(\tilde{\mathcal Y}_k, w_k;N),\, U_{t_k}\bigr) \approx \mu_k$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{algorithm}[p]
    \setstretch{1.1}
\caption{Adaptive-staging Boltzmann generator training}
\label{alg: boltzmann}
\small
\KwInput{potentials $U_0,U$; sizes $N,B\Ge2$; loss weights $\lambda,\alpha,\beta\Ge0$; ladder length $M$; ESS floors $\tau_{\mathrm{SMC}}=0$, $\tau_{\mathrm v}=0.6$; factors $\gamma=0.7$, $\Gamma=1.5$; safe start $t_{\mathrm{safe}}=0.2$}
\KwOutput{completed stage schedule $0=t_0<\cdots<t_K=1$ and selected stage maps $\{G_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$}

\tcp{validation set, $\mathcal Y_k \approx \mu_k$}
$\mathcal Y_0 \gets N$ samples from $\mu_0$, \quad $t_0 \gets 0$, \quad $k \gets 0$\;
\While{$t_k < 1$}{
    $k \gets k + 1$\;
    Set $U_{t_{k-1}} \gets (1-t_{k-1}) U_0 + t_{k-1} U$\;
    \tcp{(i) validation set}
    Use $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$ for stage selection, validation, and QT when $\alpha>0$\;
    \tcp{(ii) adaptive stage selection}
    \eIf{$k=1$}{
        Set $t_{\mathrm{init}} = t_{\mathrm{safe}}$\;
    }
    {
        Set $t_{\mathrm{init}} = \min\big(1, t_{k-1} + \Gamma(t_{k-1} - t_{k-2})\big)$\;
    }
    Choose $t_k$ by Algorithm~\ref{alg: step} on $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$ from $t_{\mathrm{init}}$\;
    Set $U_{t_k} \gets (1-t_k) U_0 + t_k U$\;
    \Repeat{$\ESS(w_k) \Ge \tau_{\mathrm v}$}{
        Initialize $G_k$ to the identity map\;
        \tcp{(iii) optional wide-coverage set}
        If $\alpha>0$, set $\hat{\mathcal Y}_k \gets \mathrm{QT}(\mathcal Y_{k-1},U_{t_k})$ by Algorithm~\ref{alg: QT}\;
        \tcp{(iv) general log-ratio-variation loss}
        \For{each gradient step}{
            Draw $B$ samples $\mathcal X$ from $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$\;
            Set the batch of pushforward samples $\bar{\mathcal X}_\nu \gets G_k^{-1}(\mathcal X)$ and detach its sampling path\;
            Anneal these pushforward samples toward $\mu_k$ to form the biased target batch $\mathcal X_\mu$ (Algorithm~\ref{alg: annealing})\;
            If $\alpha>0$, draw $\hat{\mathcal X}_\mu\subset\hat{\mathcal Y}_k$ and refresh it by MALA under $U_{t_k}$\;
            $s\gets\alpha+\beta$; if $s>0$, mix $\hat{\mathcal X}_\mu$ (when $\alpha>0$) and $\bar{\mathcal X}_\nu$ with probabilities $\alpha/s,\beta/s$ to form $\mathcal X_{\xi_k}$\;
            Update $G_k$ with the loss below using random batch permutations; omit its last term if $s=0$:
            \[
                \widehat{\mathcal L}[G_k]=\widehat{\mathbb E}_{y\in\mathcal X_\mu}\bigl[z_k(y)\bigr]+\lambda\widehat{\mathbb E}_{y,y'\in\mathcal X_\mu}\bigl[|z_k(y)-z_k(y')|\bigr]+s^2\widehat{\mathbb E}_{y,y'\in\mathcal X_{\xi_k}}\bigl[|z_k(y)-z_k(y')|\bigr].
            \]
        }
        \tcp{(v) validation set update}
        Form the trained and identity pairs as in step~(v)\;
        Keep the larger-ESS pair as $(\tilde{\mathcal Y}_k,w_k)$ and set $G_k$ to the corresponding map\;
        \eIf{$\ESS(w_k) < \tau_{\mathrm v}$}{
            \tcp{reject the stage and shrink toward $t_{k-1}$}
            $t_k \gets t_{k-1} + \gamma\,(t_k - t_{k-1})$, then update $U_{t_k} \gets (1-t_k) U_0 + t_k U$\;
        }{
            $\mathcal Y_k \gets \mathrm{MALA}\bigl(\mathrm{resample}(\tilde{\mathcal Y}_k,w_k;N),U_{t_k}\bigr)$\;
        }
    }
}
$K \gets k$\;
\Return $\{t_k\}_{k=0}^{K}$ and $\{G_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$\;
\end{algorithm}
\mbox{}\\
The accepted stage schedule defines a Boltzmann generator for $\mu$. The process starts with samples from $\mu_0$. At stage $k$, it applies the selected inverse map or identity, computes $w_k$, resamples, and rejuvenates under $U_{t_k}$. The resulting sample set supplies stage $k+1$.

\subsection{Propagation factor analysis}
\label{sec: propagation-analysis}

Algorithm~\ref{alg: boltzmann} returns a validation set of $N$ samples at the endpoint. This subsection bounds the Monte Carlo error of that validation set as an estimate of the target distribution $\mu$ and identifies how the bound grows along the stage schedule. We analyze the flow and resample part of the generator, omitting the MALA rejuvenation for simplicity. The stage maps are the diffeomorphisms fixed by an accepted schedule; no property of the training that produced them is used.

Assuming $\mu_k \ll \nu_k$, we take the stage weight in normalized form, $w_k := \D\mu_k/\D\nu_k$ with $\int w_k \, \D\nu_k = 1$, and assume $w_k > 0$ so that the self-normalization below is always defined. The scheme draws $X_0^1,\dots,X_0^N$ independently from $\mu_0$ and, for $k=1,\dots,K$, repeats three steps.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Propagation.} Apply the inverse stage map to each particle, $Y_k^i = G_k^{-1}(X_{k-1}^i)$.
    \item \emph{Reweighting.} Evaluate the stage weight at each propagated particle, $W_k^i = w_k(Y_k^i)$.
    \item \emph{Resampling.} Draw $X_k^{1:N}$ by multinomial resampling from $\sum_{i=1}^{N} \bigl(W_k^i/\sum_{l=1}^{N} W_k^l\bigr)\,\delta_{Y_k^i}$.
\end{itemize}
After stage $K$ the scheme returns the empirical measure $\mu_K^N = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N} \delta_{X_K^i}$ as the estimate of $\mu_K$. This is a simplified model of inference with a trained generator: every $G_k$ is fixed, the accepted schedule is replayed on a fresh particle set with no optimizer update, and the MALA rejuvenation that Algorithm~\ref{alg: boltzmann} applies after each resampling is dropped. No Markov kernel therefore acts between stages, so the error bounded here is the propagation error alone. For a bounded observable $\varphi$ write $\operatorname{osc}(\varphi) = \sup\varphi - \inf\varphi$, and for a function $h$ write $\lVert h \rVert_\infty$ for its essential supremum on $\mathbb R^d$, taken for $w_k$ with respect to $\nu_k$.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm: propagation}
Suppose $w_j>0$ and the $\nu_j$-essential supremum $\lVert w_j \rVert_\infty$ is finite, for every accepted stage $j$. Then for every bounded observable $\varphi$ and every $N \Ge 1$,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb E\Bigl[\bigl(\mu_K^N(\varphi) - \mu_K(\varphi)\bigr)^{2}\Bigr]
    \Le \frac{C}{N},
    \qquad
    C = \frac{\operatorname{osc}(\varphi)^{2}}{4}
    \left(\sum_{k=0}^{K}\ \prod_{j=k+1}^{K} 3\lVert w_j \rVert_\infty\right)^{\!2},
    \label{eq: propagation-bound}
\end{equation}
with the empty product equal to one.
\end{theorem}
\noindent
Appendix~\ref{sec: appendix-propagation} gives the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm: propagation}. The constant is explicit and independent of $N$, and depends on the stage maps only through the weight suprema. It is a product over stages, summed over the stage at which the error enters. Motivated by Theorem~\ref{thm: propagation}, we introduce two propagation factors read from that structure, which characterize how the strong error propagates across the stages.

The first is the square root of the constant, up to the observable,
\begin{equation}
    F_{\Sigma+}
    := \sum_{k=0}^{K}\ \prod_{j=k+1}^{K} 3\lVert w_j \rVert_\infty ,
    \label{eq: propagation-factor-plus}
\end{equation}
so that $C = \frac14 \operatorname{osc}(\varphi)^2 F_{\Sigma+}^2$, and \eqref{eq: propagation-bound} reads
\begin{equation*}
    \bigl\lVert \mu_K^N(\varphi) - \mu_K(\varphi) \bigr\rVert_2
    \Le \frac{\operatorname{osc}(\varphi)}{2}\, \frac{F_{\Sigma+}}{\sqrt N} .
\end{equation*}
The factor therefore acts on the strong error itself, not on its square, and by Theorem~\ref{thm: propagation} it is a valid upper bound on the accumulated error at rate $N^{-1/2}$.

The second keeps that form, drops the factors $3$, which arise from the recursion in the proof and carry no information about the problem, and replaces each supremum by the reciprocal square root of the stage ESS. For the stage weight, the ESS of \eqref{eq: ESS} reads
\begin{equation}
    \ESS(w_k) := \frac{1}{\int_{\mathbb R^d} w_k(x)^2 \, \nu_k(x) \, \D x} \in [0,1] ,
    \label{eq: propagation-ESS}
\end{equation}
which is the vector definition with the sample averages replaced by $\nu_k$-integrals, using $\int_{\mathbb R^d} w_k \, \D\nu_k = 1$. The second factor is then
\begin{equation}
    F_{\Sigma}
    := \sum_{k=0}^{K}\ \prod_{j=k+1}^{K} \frac{1}{\sqrt{\ESS(w_j)}} .
    \label{eq: propagation-factor-sigma}
\end{equation}
The substitution is ordered stagewise. Since $\lVert w_j \rVert_\infty \Ge \int w_j \, \D\nu_j = 1$,
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{1}{\ESS(w_j)} = \int_{\mathbb R^d} w_j^2 \, \D\nu_j \Le \lVert w_j \rVert_\infty \Le \lVert w_j \rVert_\infty^{2}
    \quad\text{for every accepted stage } j
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    F_\Sigma \Le F_{\Sigma+} .
\end{equation*}

Neither factor can be computed directly: $F_\Sigma$ requires the $\nu_j$-integral $\int w_j^2 \, \D\nu_j$, and $F_{\Sigma+}$ the $\nu_j$-essential supremum of $w_j$. A Boltzmann generator run already evaluates the importance weights of every stage, $\mathbf W_j = (W_j^1,\dots,W_j^N)$ with $W_j^i = w_j(Y_j^i)$, and the sample counterparts of both quantities are read off them at no further cost. We therefore propose the discrete approximations of the factors
\begin{equation}
    \hat F_{\Sigma+} := \sum_{k=0}^{K}\ \prod_{j=k+1}^{K}  \max_{1 \Le i \Le N} \frac{3N W_j^i}{\sum_{l=1}^{N} W_j^l} ,
    \qquad
    \hat F_{\Sigma} := \sum_{k=0}^{K}\ \prod_{j=k+1}^{K} \frac{1}{\sqrt{\ESS(\mathbf W_j)}} ,
    \label{eq: propagation-factor-hat}
\end{equation}
both invariant to the scale of the weights and therefore available when the target is known only up to a constant. They are ordered by the same three inequalities, with the largest rescaled weight in place of the supremum, so $\hat F_\Sigma \Le \hat F_{\Sigma+}$. Neither is a bound on its continuous counterpart at finite $N$: the particles need not visit the region where $w_j$ is largest, and the empirical normalization is itself random.

The experiments report $\hat F_\Sigma$ as the propagation factor, while the analysis stays with $F_{\Sigma+}$ and $F_\Sigma$. $F_\Sigma$ is not itself a bound on the error constant; it is a realistic and convenient surrogate for $F_{\Sigma+}$, built directly from the ESS of each accepted stage. Every summand is at least one, so $F_\Sigma \Ge K+1$, and the cost of resampling is charged even when every stage proposal equals its target.

The factor summarizes weight degeneracy along one accepted schedule, and does not measure the difficulty of the target. The sample size required by importance sampling grows exponentially in the KL divergence between target and proposal \citep{chatterjee2018sample}, and with the intrinsic dimension of the problem \citep{agapiou2017importance}; neither dependence appears in $F_\Sigma$.

Theorem~\ref{thm: propagation} also settles how the scheme behaves in $N$. The bound gives $\lVert \mu_K^N(\varphi) - \mu_K(\varphi) \rVert_2 \Le \sqrt{C/N}$ for every bounded observable, which also bounds the bias and vanishes as $N$ grows: the flow and resample inference is \emph{asymptotically unbiased}, and its error is removed by enlarging the sample alone. Classical Monte Carlo sampling of $\mu$ instead requires the simulation time to grow and, for unadjusted integrators, the time step to vanish; a Metropolis-adjusted kernel such as MALA removes the second requirement but not the first. The generator of Algorithm~\ref{alg: boltzmann} inherits the property, since its rejuvenation step is MALA and leaves each stage target invariant exactly \citep{delmoral2004feynman}.



% ===================== Section 5: model problems =====================
\section{Numerical experiments on model problems}
\label{sec: experiments}

We test forward KL against its $\X$-augmented losses $\KL$+$\X_\mu$, $\KL$+$\X_\mu$+$\X_{\hat\mu}$, and $\KL$+$\X_\mu$+$\X_{(\hat\mu+\bar\nu)/2}$, the last of which is KLXX. The target distributions vary in dimension, in the kind of barrier that separates their modes, and in how far those modes lie from the source. The 2D benchmarks separate finite-batch accuracy from mode discovery and make proposal leakage directly visible. The product multi-well raises the dimension to $256$, where the multimodality is a product of independent sign choices. The tilted $\phi^4$ model replaces isolated wells by a collective domain-wall barrier between two unequally weighted vacua. The clock model is a periodic $64$-dimensional target with six symmetry-related sectors, trained by the full adaptive-staging construction. Accordingly, ESS is always reported together with a mode-coverage diagnostic, since neither quantity is conclusive on its own.

\subsection{2D benchmark distributions}
\label{subsec: 2d-benchmark}

We consider four 2D targets: Two-Moon, Three-Well, Himmelblau, and Sparse. Two-Moon has two distorted crescent components and isolates finite-batch accuracy because both components lie within reach of the source. The remaining targets isolate mode collapse: Three-Well contains a low-probability basin, Himmelblau has four separated wells, and Sparse places two tight modes far into the source tails.

For every target, we initialize the same neural spline flow (NSF) \citep{durkan2019neural} at the identity and compare four objectives:
\begin{center}
$\KL$,\qquad
$\KL$+$\X_\mu$,\qquad
$\KL$+$\X_\mu$+$\X_{\hat\mu}$,\qquad
$\KL$+$\X_\mu$+$\X_{(\hat\mu+\bar\nu)/2}$.
\end{center}
The flow has $32$ spline bins, $6$ transforms, and conditioner widths $(128,128)$. Each training step constructs a target surrogate by one annealing update through the current flow. The rejuvenation kernel uses $100$ MALA steps of size $10^{-3}$ for Two-Moon and $50$ MALA steps of size $2\times10^{-3}$ for the other targets. The fixed validation sets contain $5\times10^4$ points for Two-Moon and Himmelblau and $8\times10^4$ for Three-Well and Sparse. For both objectives containing the wide-coverage term $\X_{\hat\mu}$, quench and temper acts on the fixed validation set, with melt scale $2$ and an Armijo L-BFGS quench using initial step $0.5$ and $200$ iterations. The final ESS is computed from flow importance weights on that validation set. Coverage, with $\kappa=5$ against an independently generated quench and temper reference set, measures the fraction of reference configurations represented by the pushforward samples through the nearest-neighbor test of Section~\ref{subsec: eval}. The reference sets contain $1000$, $4000$, $500$, and $4000$ points for Two-Moon, Three-Well, Himmelblau, and Sparse, respectively; their QT tempering uses $1000$ MALA steps at the target-specific step size above. Because coverage tests occupancy of the reference balls, a collapsed flow can have ESS near one but cannot have full coverage. Each objective--target pair is one fixed-seed training realization, so the numerical differences below describe these matched runs rather than variation across independent trainings.

\subsection{High-dimensional product multi-well}
\label{subsec: highd}

We next isolate accuracy scaling over the dimensions
$d=16,32,\ldots,256$. Letting $k=\lfloor\log_2 d\rfloor$, we use
\begin{equation}
    U(\mathbf{x})=\frac12\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2
    +12\sum_{i=1}^{k}\exp(-x_i^2).
    \label{eq: highd-potential}
\end{equation}
The first $k$ coordinates are symmetric double wells with minima at $\pm\sqrt{\ln 24}$ and barrier approximately $9.9$, while the remaining $d-k$ coordinates are standard Gaussian. The target therefore has exactly $2^k$ equal-weight sign-pattern modes, where $2^k\Le d<2^{k+1}$. Multimodality occupies only a $k$-dimensional subspace, but the log-weight error accumulates across all $d$ coordinates.

All four objectives use the same identity-initialized NSF on $[-4,4]^d$, with $16$ bins, $6$ transforms, and conditioner widths $(256,256)$. At every dimension, training uses batch size $250$, $2000$ Adam steps at learning rate $10^{-3}$, gradient clipping at $10^2$, and a fixed validation set of size $5000d$. The live target surrogate uses one annealing update followed by $50$ MALA steps of size $2\times10^{-3}$. This is a standalone one-flow ablation, so its separately reported QT set contains $1000d$ samples rather than reusing the validation set as the staged algorithm does. QT uses melt scale $2$ and an Armijo L-BFGS quench with initial step $0.5$ and $200$ iterations. Final validation ESS is evaluated on $\max(200d,80000)$ fresh source samples. Each objective--dimension pair is one fixed-seed training realization.

\begin{figure}[htb]

\subsection{Lattice \texorpdfstring{$\phi^4$}{phi4} field theory: ESS at a collective barrier}
\label{subsec: phi4}

The tilted scalar $\phi^4$ theory tests ESS at a collective barrier. On a periodic $L\times L$ lattice, with $d=L^2$ and magnetization $m(\boldsymbol\phi)=d^{-1}\sum_j\phi_j$, the action is
\begin{equation*}
U(\boldsymbol\phi)
=-0.8\sum_{\langle jl\rangle}\phi_j\phi_l
+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left[\phi_j^2+\frac12(\phi_j^2-1)^2\right]
+h\sum_{j=1}^{d}\phi_j.
\end{equation*}
We take $h=0.0257$ at $L=6$ and $h=0.0144$ at $L=8$. This size-dependent
field keeps the extensive tilt scale nearly fixed:
$hL^2=0.9252$ at $L=6$ and $hL^2=0.9216$ at $L=8$. Thus the two targets have
comparable phase imbalance despite their different volumes.
In the broken phase the field orders near two vacua $\pm v\mathbf 1$, where $v>0$ is the spontaneous field magnitude and $\mathbf 1\in\mathbb R^d$ is the all-ones vector. A collective domain-wall barrier separates the vacua. The positive linear term gives the two phases unequal probability, so the minority weight $p_+=\mathbb P(m>0)$ is not fixed by symmetry and must be learned.

PT gives the references $p_+=0.1251\pm0.0009$ and $0.1284\pm0.0014$, statistically consistent with occupancies $0.1260$ and $0.1268$ from an independent mirror-MALA construction. That construction samples the positive vacuum of the symmetric $h=0$ action, obtains the negative vacuum by the exact reflection $\boldsymbol\phi\mapsto-\boldsymbol\phi$, reweights the pair by the field term, and resamples one million configurations. Each loss is run at seeds $0,1,2$ using an identity-initialized NSF on $[-3,3]^d$, with $16$ bins, $6$ transforms, and conditioner widths $(256,256)$. The source is $\mathcal N(0,0.25I_d)$, centered on the barrier. Training uses a fixed validation set of $10^5$ source samples, batch size $500$, $2000$ Adam steps at learning rate $10^{-3}$, and gradient clipping at $10^3$. The live surrogate uses one annealing update followed by $50$ MALA steps of size $2\times10^{-3}$. For both objectives containing $\X_{\hat\mu}$, quench and temper acts on the validation set, with melt scale $2$ and an Armijo L-BFGS quench using initial step $0.1$ and $200$ iterations.

\subsection{Boltzmann generator on \texorpdfstring{$p$}{p}-state clock model}
\label{subsec: clock}

The preceding benchmarks use a single flow. We now test the full adaptive-staging construction on the $p=6$ clock model on a periodic $8\times8$ lattice,
\begin{equation}
U(\boldsymbol\theta)
=-\sum_{\langle jl\rangle}\cos(\theta_j-\theta_l)
-\frac12\sum_{j=1}^{d}\cos(6\theta_j),
\qquad \boldsymbol\theta\in[-\pi,\pi)^{d},\quad d=64,
\label{eq: clock-potential}
\end{equation}
where $\langle jl\rangle$ runs over nearest-neighbor pairs. The source is uniform on the torus and the interpolation is $U_t=tU$. At low temperature, the target separates into six symmetry-related clock sectors.

Each stage uses an identity-initialized neural circular spline flow with $16$ bins, $6$ autoregressive transforms, and conditioner widths $(256,256)$. The validation set contains $4\times10^5$ samples, and both SMC and quench and temper act directly on this set. Every stage uses $3000$ Adam steps at learning rate $10^{-3}$, a four-level annealed surrogate with $100$ MALA steps of size $10^{-3}$, energy clipping at $10^3$, and gradient clipping at $10^2$. For KLXX, QT uses wrapped-Gaussian melt scale $2\pi$ and an Armijo L-BFGS quench with initial step $10^{-2}$ and $100$ iterations. The clock stage schedule uses the nondefault settings $t_{\mathrm{safe}}=0.25$, enlarge factor $\Gamma=2$, and gates $\tau_{\mathrm{SMC}}=0.7$ and $\tau_{\mathrm v}=0.4$. We report batch sizes $B=2000,1000,500,250$. An additional completed run lies below this reporting cutoff and is excluded from the table and all aggregate clock claims.

The loss $\KL$+$\X_\mu$+$\X_{(\hat\mu+\bar\nu)/2}$ is then trained on the identical accepted values of $t$, without a second gate. \section{Boltzmann generators on molecular potential energies}
\label{sec: molecular}

The model problems above separate the target and QT terms on explicit targets and probe the term built from pushforward samples. Molecular systems add a singular nonbonded core, a mixed Euclidean--periodic domain, and more conformational degrees of freedom as molecular size grows. We first define the molecular measure and its regularized potential, then add sharpening to the staged generator and report the completed alkane family, three additional achiral molecules, and three chiral KLXX runs with fixed stereochemical configurations.

\subsection{Molecular potential models}
\label{sec: mol-models}

For Cartesian coordinates \(x\), let \(E(x)\) denote the complete molecular-mechanics energy
\begin{equation}
E(x)
=E_{\mathrm{bond}}+E_{\mathrm{angle}}+E_{\mathrm{torsion}}
 +E_{\mathrm{LJ}}+E_{\mathrm{Coul}}
 +E_{\mathrm{GB}}^{\mathrm{OBC1}}+E_{\mathrm{ACE}} .
\label{eq: molecular-energy}
\end{equation}
The bonded and vacuum nonbonded part has the Amber form \citep{cornell1995amber}
\begin{align}
E_{\mathrm{vac}}(x)
={}&\sum_{b} k_b(r_b-r_b^0)^2
 +\sum_{a} k_a(\theta_a-\theta_a^0)^2
 +\sum_{\tau}\frac{V_\tau}{2}
       \bigl[1+\cos(n_\tau\varphi_\tau-\gamma_\tau)\bigr] \notag\\
&+\sum_{\substack{i<j\\\mathrm{nonbonded}}}
 \left\lbrace 4\epsilon_{ij}\left[
 \left(\frac{\sigma_{ij}}{r_{ij}}\right)^{12}
 -\left(\frac{\sigma_{ij}}{r_{ij}}\right)^6\right]
 +k_{\mathrm e}\frac{q_iq_j}{r_{ij}}\right\rbrace ,
\label{eq: molecular-amber-energy}
\end{align}
Here $b$, $a$, and $\tau$ index bonds, angles, and proper or improper torsions, while $i,j$ index nonbonded atom pairs after topology-defined exclusions and scaling. The symbols $r_b^0$, $\theta_a^0$, $k_b$, $k_a$, $V_\tau$, $n_\tau$, and $\gamma_\tau$ are force-field equilibrium values and coefficients; $\sigma_{ij}$, $\epsilon_{ij}$, and $q_i$ are the nonbonded parameters. OBC1 supplies the generalized-Born electrostatic solvation term \citep{onufriev2004gb}, and ACE supplies the nonpolar term \citep{schaefer1996ace}. All terms in \eqref{eq: molecular-energy} belong to the sampled potential.

A nonlinear molecule with \(A\) atoms has \(d=3A-6\) internal degrees of freedom after rigid translation and rotation are removed. A fixed internal-coordinate tree reconstructs \(x=x(q)\) from whitened bond and angle coordinates and periodic torsions. Write $d_{\mathrm E}$ and $d_{\mathrm T}$ for the numbers of nonperiodic Euclidean coordinates and periodic torsions, respectively, and let $\mathbb T=\mathbb R/(2\pi\mathbb Z)$. Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal Q=\mathbb R^{d_{\mathrm E}}\times\mathbb T^{d_{\mathrm T}},
\qquad d_{\mathrm E}+d_{\mathrm T}=3A-6,
\label{eq: mixed-molecular-domain}
\end{equation}
and the physical reduced potential at inverse temperature \(\beta_{\mathrm T}=(k_BT)^{-1}\) is
\begin{equation}
U(q)=\beta_{\mathrm T} E(x(q))-\log J(q),
\label{eq: molecular-reduced-potential}
\end{equation}
where \(J(q)\) is the Cartesian volume Jacobian of the reconstruction, including the whitening maps. The source is Gaussian on the Euclidean block and uniform on the torus. The mixed spline flow uses ordinary rational-quadratic splines on the Euclidean coordinates and circular splines on the torsions. We abbreviate \(N\)-acetyl-\(\mathrm L\)-proline \(N\)-methylamide as Ac-Pro-NHMe.

The alkane family uses neutral all-atom bundles with GAFF2 parameters, AM1-BCC charges, graph-symmetry and neutrality projection, and \(\mathrm{mbondi2}\) radii. Ac-Pro-NHMe uses the corresponding 26-atom ACE--PRO--NME ff96/OBC1/ACE Hamiltonian.

The alkanes, glycerol, neutral diethanolamine, and NMA have no fixed configurational stereocenter under these bundle definitions. Neutral diethanolamine retains both signs of its nitrogen-pyramidal coordinate, and the NMA amide torsion remains periodic so that both cis and trans conformations belong to its target. The chiral targets retain only the named configurations: \(\mathrm L\)-alanine for alanine dipeptide, \((2R,3R)\) for 2,3-butanediol, and \(\mathrm L\)-proline for Ac-Pro-NHMe. All conformational basins within each selected configuration remain part of the corresponding target. These support conventions are part of the target rather than an after-the-fact filter.

The Lennard--Jones core in \eqref{eq: molecular-amber-energy} grows as \(r^{-12}\), so early flow proposals can place a few samples at energies and gradients far beyond the scale of the thermally occupied region. We therefore use a two-parameter molecular surrogate. Let \(x_\star\) be the bundle reference geometry. After flooring only the regular and exception Coulomb/Lennard--Jones pair distances at \(r\), denote the complete force-field energy by \(E_r(x)\), its reference value by \(E_r^\star:=E_r(x_\star)\), and its reference-relative value by \(\Delta E_r(x):=E_r(x)-E_r^\star\). For an energy threshold \(e\), define the soft cap
\begin{equation}
\mathcal C_e(\Delta E)=
\begin{cases}
\Delta E, & \Delta E\Le e,\\[2pt]
e\bigl[1+\log(\Delta E/e)\bigr], & \Delta E>e,
\end{cases}
\label{eq: molecular-regularizer}
\end{equation}
and collect the two regularization parameters in \(\rho=(e,r)\). The corresponding reduced potential is
\begin{equation}
U^{\{\rho\}}(q)
=\beta_{\mathrm T}\!\left[E_r^\star
+\mathcal C_e\bigl(\Delta E_r(x(q))\bigr)\right]-\log J(q).
\label{eq: regularized-molecular-potential}
\end{equation}
The threshold \(e\) is measured in \(\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), and the pair floor \(r\) is measured in \(\mathrm{nm}\). The component formulas of the bonded and OBC1/ACE terms are unchanged before the scalar cap is applied to the complete energy. The coordinate Jacobian and domain are not capped. The physical potential \(U\) in \eqref{eq: molecular-reduced-potential} is recovered in the formal limit \(e\to\infty\), \(r\to0\). Thus \(U^{\{\rho\}}\) is a regularized sampling potential, not optimizer clipping or a replacement for the physical energy used in the observables.

\subsection{Boltzmann generators with sharpening}
\label{sec: mol-sharpening}

A softer molecular potential can make early stages easier in a given adaptive-staging run. After each flow stage, \emph{sharpening} reweights the validation set toward a stricter regularization setting, resamples it, and applies MALA under the stricter potential. This is a stochastic potential update, not an additional flow-training loss. To extend the source-to-target interpolation of Section~\ref{sec: boltzmann}, let $\rho_0=(e_0,r_0)$ and $\rho_1=(e_1,r_1)$ be the soft and final settings and interpolate them by
\begin{equation}
\rho_s=(1-s)\rho_0+s\rho_1,
\qquad s\in[0,1].
\label{eq: molecular-sharpening-path}
\end{equation}
The alkane and NMA experiments use the finite endpoint \(\rho_1=(100,0.15)\), while glycerol and neutral diethanolamine use \(\rho_1=(100,0.10)\). The residual difference from \(U\) is audited separately. A fixed-regularization run has \(\rho_0=\rho_1\). Extending the interpolation of Section~\ref{sec: boltzmann} by the single index \(s\), set
\begin{equation}
U_{t,s}(q)=(1-t)U_0(q)+tU^{\{\rho_s\}}(q),
\qquad
\mu_{t,s}\propto\exp(-U_{t,s}).
\label{eq: molecular-two-coordinate-bridge}
\end{equation}
Thus \(t\) remains the source-to-target interpolation parameter defined in Section~\ref{sec: boltzmann}, while \(s\) changes only the regularization setting. If \(s\) is fixed, suppressing the second index recovers the notation \(U_t,\mu_t\) of Section~\ref{sec: boltzmann}.

Accepted sample sets lie on the diagonal $s=t$, but one stage advances the two coordinates in sequence:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal Y_{k-1}\approx\mu_{t_{k-1},t_{k-1}}
\ \xrightarrow[
    \text{selected }(\tilde{\mathcal Y}_k,w_k)
]{\ \text{flow or identity}\ }\
\mathcal Y_k^-\approx\mu_{t_k,t_{k-1}}
\ \xrightarrow[
    w_k^{\mathrm{sharp}}
]{\ \text{sharpen}\ }\
\mathcal Y_k\approx\mu_{t_k,t_k}.
\label{eq: molecular-stage-arrows}
\end{equation}
The first arrow holds $s=t_{k-1}$ fixed and advances $t$ from $t_{k-1}$ to $t_k$. Potential-space SMC and mixed-domain MALA carry a copy of all $N$ particles along this bridge. Here mixed-domain MALA is the MALA kernel of Section~\ref{subsec: annealing} applied on the mixed domain \eqref{eq: mixed-molecular-domain}: the Langevin drift and noise act on all coordinates, each proposal is wrapped back into $\mathcal Q$, and the acceptance ratio uses the wrapped normal proposal density on the periodic block. The resulting sample set is held fixed and subsampled during flow training; its smallest incremental ESS also screens the proposed $t_k$. In KLXX, a second sample set is built by applying QT under $U_{t_k,t_{k-1}}$ to $N$ fresh source draws. Thus the molecular implementation uses a fixed SMC target set and a fresh source QT set, rather than the per-stage target surrogate and validation-seeded QT in Section~\ref{sec: boltzmann}.

After training, the candidate flow and identity proposals are evaluated on the complete carried set $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$. The pair with larger ESS over the validation set is denoted by $(\tilde{\mathcal Y}_k,w_k)$; ties retain the identity. Reweighting, resampling, and MALA under $U_{t_k,t_{k-1}}$ produce $\mathcal Y_k^-$. The second arrow holds $t=t_k$ fixed and advances the regularization coordinate from $t_{k-1}$ to $t_k$. Its exact log weight is
\begin{equation}
\log w_k^{\mathrm{sharp}}(q)
=U_{t_k,t_{k-1}}(q)-U_{t_k,t_k}(q)
=t_k\!\left[U^{\{\rho_{t_{k-1}}\}}(q)
-U^{\{\rho_{t_k}\}}(q)\right].
\label{eq: molecular-sharpening-weight}
\end{equation}
Sharpening is accepted only when its ESS over the validation set clears the stage threshold. The samples are then resampled and rejuvenated by MALA under $U_{t_k,t_k}$. A failed proposal or sharpening gate shrinks $t_k-t_{k-1}$ and retries. The selected map advances the source-to-target interpolation; the stochastic sharpening step changes the molecular potential.

Temperature-annealed Boltzmann generators (TA-BG) \citep{schopmans2025temperature} address the same barrier problem through a different continuation coordinate. TA-BG first trains a flow by reverse KL at an elevated temperature, where metastable basins are more connected, and then lowers the temperature through reweighting, resampling, and forward KL updates until the physical target temperature is reached. Sharpening instead keeps the thermodynamic temperature fixed and anneals the surrogate interaction core: the energy threshold $e$ and pair-distance floor $r$ in $U^{\{\rho_s\}}$ move from a softer setting toward the reported endpoint, with exact incremental reweighting and MALA between settings. As a continuation mechanism, this is a stronger intervention at the singular core than temperature scaling alone: changing temperature rescales the physical energy but preserves its divergent short-range form, whereas $\rho_s$ directly changes the regular and exception Coulomb/Lennard--Jones pair floor and the high-energy tail. TA-BG likewise uses an energy regularizer for stable training, but its annealed variable is temperature rather than the regularization setting. This stronger intervention also has a drawback: before the endpoint, the sharpening states are algorithmic surrogate ensembles rather than physical molecular systems because their interaction laws have been modified. By contrast, the TA-BG intermediates remain canonical distributions of the same physical potential at explicitly defined elevated temperatures and can therefore serve as physically interpretable high-temperature reference states. The molecular results below support sharpening as an effective continuation mechanism, but we do not yet have a matched numerical comparison with TA-BG under common targets, force fields, architectures, and energy-evaluation budgets. We leave that comparison to future work.

We compare three generators. ID performs both stochastic arrows without training a flow. KL trains the forward KL and then selects the trained or identity proposal. KLXX uses the same selection rule with log-ratio variation. All three methods retain the same SMC selection, reweighting, resampling, and MALA steps.
The molecular runs use the same propagation factor $\hat F_\Sigma$, evaluated on the reweighting steps the generator actually performs. Every accepted stage reweights and resamples twice: first on the selected flow or identity proposal, then on the sharpening weight of \eqref{eq: molecular-sharpening-weight}. We take the validation ESS of both steps at every accepted stage and concatenate them in the order performed, so a run of $K$ accepted stages gives a sequence of $2K$ ESS values, beginning with the flow ESS of the first stage followed by its sharpening ESS, and ending with the two ESS values of the stage that reaches the endpoint. The factor is accumulated over that sequence: each entry contributes its reciprocal square root, these are multiplied over every suffix of the sequence, and $\hat F_\Sigma$ is the sum of those products, with the empty suffix contributing one. On a fixed regularization path the sharpening weight is identically one and its ESS is one; the step still enters the sequence, because the generator resamples there whether or not the regularization path moves, and it then contributes a summand without enlarging any product. Every summand is at least one, so $\hat F_\Sigma \Ge 2K+1$. As in Section~\ref{sec: propagation-analysis}, $\hat F_\Sigma$ summarizes stagewise weight degeneracy; it is not a fresh source-to-endpoint ESS or an endpoint error estimator.

w_i^{\mathrm{phys}}

The other two are checked against a published trajectory set and against a PT run, both of which sample the target directly; here neither is available, and even the reference needs a move constructed for this one bond before it can equilibrate the two sectors at all. The residual gap is what one expects from a finite sample rather than from a systematic error in the method. By Theorem~\ref{thm: propagation} the flow and resample inference is asymptotically unbiased: its error is bounded at rate \(N^{-1/2}\) for every bounded observable and is removed by enlarging the sample alone, so a discrepancy that survives at fixed \(N\) is the finite-sample term and should shrink as \(N\) grows. It is tempting to read the propagation factors of Table~\ref{tab: mol-chiral-summary} as a prediction of how large that term should be, since \(\hat F_\Sigma\) is \(176.7\) for Ac-Pro-NHMe against \(68.08\) for alanine dipeptide, a ratio of about \(2.6\). That reading does not follow. Theorem~\ref{thm: propagation} bounds a fixed bounded observable, with the observable entering separately through its oscillation, whereas the quantities compared here are basin populations of different molecules at different dimensions, and the measured discrepancy may retain an implicit dependence on dimension that the factor does not carry. A factor larger by \(2.6\) therefore does not predict a bias larger by \(2.6\).

Enlarging the inference set beyond \(1.5\times10^7\) is the direct test, and it is the one we do not run. The replay cost grows in proportion to the sample count, this target is already the most expensive of the three, and pushing \(N\) far enough to resolve a difference of \(0.02\) in a population of \(0.14\) is beyond the budget of this study. We leave the gap open rather than close it by tuning. Taken as a whole, the Ac-Pro-NHMe result is the method working on its hardest case: with no external data and no prior knowledge of where the basins lie, the generator recovers the qualitatively correct conformational picture, and disagrees with an independent reference only in the quantitative value of the minority population.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec: conclusions}

Forward KL is mass-covering for exact distributions, but its sampled loss sees only the target regions supplied to it. This gap can produce a fake ESS, transfer surrogate error to the flow, and leave target-poor proposal regions weakly constrained. Log-ratio variation addresses these failures by comparing the exact log-density ratio at pairs of samples. The weighting measure determines which regions are compared: target samples measure density variation on reached target regions, QT samples add candidate modes, and pushforward samples expose regions occupied by the proposal. KLXX combines these comparisons while retaining the exact target as a minimizer and the flow as a tractable importance proposal.

The theory establishes a narrower statement. For the FR gradient flow of forward KL with $\X_\mu$, the regularizer adds a nonpositive term to the instantaneous KL dissipation. For a fixed smooth surrogate and $\lambda<\frac12$, it also subtracts a nonnegative discrepancy term from the stationary-error bound. This does not prove finite-step behavior for full KLXX, Adam, or the experimental value $\lambda=1$. Theorem~\ref{thm: propagation} is separate and complete: it bounds the strong error of the flow and resample scheme at rate $N^{-1/2}$ for every bounded observable, with a constant independent of $N$, so the scheme is asymptotically unbiased. The propagation factors read from that constant summarize stagewise weight degeneracy, and the reported $\hat F_\Sigma$ is a computable surrogate rather than a bound on the error constant.

The numerical results provide the remaining evidence. The 2D and $\phi^4$ ablations are consistent with the roles of the target, QT, and pushforward samples; the product test shows higher validation ESS for every tested objective with log-ratio variation at all sixteen tested dimensions. On the shared clock stage schedules, KLXX has higher validation ESS on all $28$ shared stages and a smaller propagation factor at every reported batch size. In the single-seed alkane runs, KLXX has the smallest factor in all ten matched settings, while forward KL approaches the identity baseline as molecule size grows. The sharpening paths have lower KLXX factors for the larger alkanes. For NMA, glycerol, and neutral diethanolamine, KLXX also has a smaller factor than $\KL$+$\X_\mu$ and reaches the endpoint in fewer accepted stages. The three chiral KLXX runs reach \(t=1\), retain their specified stereochemical components, and populate multiple torsional basins in the reported conformational diagnostics. For alanine dipeptide, the final sample set matches the published equilibrium reference of \citet{midgley2022flow} at \(10^7\) samples per side: the three backbone basin populations differ by at most \(0.0018\), including the \(0.3\%\) \(\alpha_{\mathrm L}\) basin, and the mean free energies agree to \(0.03\,k_{\mathrm B}T\) in every band. For Ac-Pro-NHMe, the hardest target and the one reached with no external data and no conformational prior, the generator populates both peptide sectors and orders them correctly, and its cis population differs from an independent reference of our own by \(0.0244\). Selected alkane observables and achiral dihedral marginals agree with independent physical-potential simulations on the reported diagnostics. These findings are consistent with an explanation based on additional support information; they are not a theorem of universal dominance or a fixed-compute comparison.

The clock and molecular configurations require additional training seeds to measure run-to-run variation. The finite alkane endpoints have nearly uniform endpoint-to-physical weights on the reported sample sets, but a continuation to $U$ would remove the remaining regularization. The additional achiral figures report selected one-dimensional marginals and do not determine joint conformational weights. Among the chiral results, alanine dipeptide is checked against published ground truth, and 2,3-butanediol and Ac-Pro-NHMe against references of our own. The Ac-Pro-NHMe comparison agrees on the qualitative picture and leaves a quantitative gap in the cis population; the propagation bound makes that gap a finite-sample term, but resolving it would require an inference set beyond the budget of this study, so we report it open. No alternative-loss run was made for any of the three. Larger molecules and explicit solvent remain outside the completed results.

\section{Fisher--Rao gradient flow with log-ratio variation}
\label{sec: appendix-FR}

Section~\ref{sec: FR} states the main gradient flow results used in the paper. This appendix supplies their derivations and proofs on the space of densities. We view $\nu$ as a generic probability density evolving along the gradient flow of the loss under an appropriate Riemannian metric on the space of densities \citep{ambrosio2008gradient,villani2009optimal} and ask how fast $\nu_t$ converges to the target $\mu$ in $\KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$.

At ties we set $\operatorname{sgn}(0)=0$. Throughout this appendix, the densities are assumed positive and sufficiently regular for the displayed gradient calculations.

In this appendix we restrict to forward KL with the target-weighted regularizer $\X_\mu$, the log-ratio variation at weighting density $\omega = \mu$,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal L[\nu] = \KL(\mu\|\nu) + \lambda  \X_\mu(\mu\|\nu) = \int_{\mathbb R^d} \mu(x) \log \frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} \D x +
    \lambda\iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}
     \mu(x)\mu(y) \biggl|\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} - \log\frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)}\biggr| \D x \D y,
    \label{eq: appendix-loss-KL-X}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda>0$ is fixed. The calculations below hold along a positive solution of this gradient flow; when $\lambda\Le\tfrac12$, the birth term in \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-L-flow} is nonnegative and preserves positivity.
Throughout this appendix we abbreviate the importance weight by
\begin{equation*}
    w(x) := \frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)},
\end{equation*}
so that the log-ratio is $z(x) = \log w(x) = \log\mu(x) - \log\nu(x)$, the field $z$ of \eqref{eq: logratio-z}. The importance weight $w$ is distinct from the weighting density $\omega$ in $\X_\omega$.

The choice of Riemannian metric matters. In the Wasserstein-2 geometry, induced by optimal transport \citep{jordan1998variational,otto2001geometry}, the forward KL fails to be displacement convex along Wasserstein-2 geodesics, and the standard Otto--Villani/Bakry--{\'E}mery argument \citep{otto2000generalization,bakry2014analysis} used for the reverse KL does not apply. We therefore work in the FR geometry \citep{amari2016information,bauer2016uniqueness}. Along the positive flow, the forward KL has an exponential bound and $\X_\mu$ contributes a nonlocal birth--death term \citep{lu2019accelerating}: the update at $x$ depends on how its importance weight ranks among weights under $\mu$.

Denoting the $\nu$-mean by $\langle \cdot \rangle_\nu := \int( \cdot ) \nu \D x$, the FR gradient of a functional $\mathcal F[\nu]$ and the associated dissipation identity along the FR gradient flow $\partial_t\nu_t = -\mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal F(\nu_t)$ are
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal F(\nu) = \nu\biggl(\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu} - \Big\langle\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu}\Big\rangle_\nu\biggr),
    \qquad
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\mathcal F[\nu_t] = -\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\biggl(\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu} - \Big\langle\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu}\Big\rangle_{\nu_t}\biggr)^{2}\nu_t \D x.
    \label{eq: appendix-FR-gradient}
\end{equation}
The mean-subtraction enforces $\int\mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal F = 0$, so the FR gradient flow conserves probability mass.

\subsection{Derivation of \texorpdfstring{$\X_\mu$}{X-mu}-regularized Fisher--Rao gradient flow}
\label{subsec: appendix-FR-grad-calc}

The first variation of the forward KL with respect to $\nu$ is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\delta \KL(\mu\|\nu)}{\delta \nu}(x) = -\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} = -w(x),
    \label{eq: appendix-var-KL}
\end{equation}
so by \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-gradient}, using $\langle w \rangle_\nu = \int_{\mathbb R^d} \mu \,\D x = 1$, the FR gradient of the forward KL is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\big[\KL(\mu\|\nu)\big](x) = \nu(x) - \mu(x).
    \label{eq: appendix-FR-grad-KL}
\end{equation}

For $\X_\mu$, the variation uses $\operatorname{sgn}(0)=0$ at ties. At an evaluation point $r$, the variation of the inner log-ratio difference is
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\delta}{\delta\nu(r)}
    \bigl[\log w(x)-\log w(y)\bigr]
    =-\frac{\delta(r-x)}{\nu(x)}
    +\frac{\delta(r-y)}{\nu(y)}.
\end{equation*}
Here $\delta$ is the Dirac distribution, so integration against $\delta(r-x)$ evaluates the remaining integrand at $x=r$.
Applying this sign convention and then exchanging $x$ and $y$ in the second term gives
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\delta \X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)}{\delta \nu}(r)
    & = \iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}
    \mu(x)\mu(y)\operatorname{sgn}\bigl(w(x)-w(y)\bigr)
    \left[-\frac{\delta(r-x)}{\nu(x)}
    +\frac{\delta(r-y)}{\nu(y)}\right]\D x\D y \\
    & = -2w(r)\int_{\mathbb R^d}
    \mu(y)\operatorname{sgn}\bigl(w(r)-w(y)\bigr)\D y.
\end{align*}
Hence the variation is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\delta \X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)}{\delta \nu}(x) = - 2w(x)S(x),
    \qquad
    S(x) := \mathbb E_{y\sim\mu}\bigl[\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr].
    \label{eq: appendix-var-X}
\end{equation}
We call $S$ the \emph{rank correlation} of the importance weight under $\mu$: $S(x) > 0$ indicates that $w(x)$ is comparatively large within the distribution of weights, and hence that $\nu$ pointwise undercovers $\mu$ relative to that distribution; $S(x) < 0$ has the opposite interpretation, and $S \equiv 0$ at $\nu = \mu$. By antisymmetry,
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbb E_\mu[S] = \mathbb E_{x,y\sim \mu}\bigl[\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr] = 0,
\end{equation*}
and the FR gradient of $\X_\mu$ with respect to $\nu$ is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\big[\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)\big](x) = -2\mu(x) S(x).
    \label{eq: appendix-FR-grad-X}
\end{equation}

The FR gradient of $\mathcal L[\nu]=\KL(\mu\|\nu)+\lambda\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)$ is the sum of \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-grad-KL} and \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-grad-X}:
\begin{equation*}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}[\mathcal L](x) = \nu(x) - \mu(x) - 2\lambda \mu(x) S(x).
\end{equation*}
This yields the FR gradient flow \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-L-flow} stated in Section~\ref{sec: FR}. It is a nonlocal partial differential equation: its right-hand side at $x$ depends on $S_t(x)$, which in turn requires the entire profile of the importance weight $\mu/\nu_t$ over the support of $\mu$. Mass is conserved along the flow because
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\nu_t(x) \D x = 2\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\mu(x) S_t(x) \D x = 0,
\end{equation*}
and the flow is stationary at $\nu_t = \mu$, where $\mu(x)/\nu_t(x) \equiv 1$ and hence $S_t\equiv 0$. When $\lambda = 0$, \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-L-flow} reduces to the FR gradient flow of forward KL.

\subsection{Convergence analysis of the \texorpdfstring{$\X_\mu$}{X-mu}-regularized flow}
\label{subsec: appendix-FR-convergence}

To analyze the convergence rate of the regularized flow \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-L-flow}, we take the forward KL $\KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$ as the error metric and differentiate it along the flow:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\KL(\mu\|\nu_t) = -\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\frac{\mu^2}{\nu_t}(1 + 2\lambda S_t) \D x + \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\mu \D x = -\chi^2(\mu\|\nu_t) - 2\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\frac{\mu^2}{\nu_t} S_t \D x,
\end{equation*}
where the chi-squared divergence is
\begin{equation*}
    \chi^2(\mu\|\nu) := \int_{\mathbb R^d}(w-1)^2\nu\D x = \int_{\mathbb R^d} w^2\nu\D x - 1.
\end{equation*}
The sign of the second integral is settled by the following rank correlation identity.

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem: appendix-rankcorrelation}
Let $\mu$ and $\nu$ be positive probability densities for which the displayed integral is finite. Then
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu(x)^2}{\nu(x)}S(x)\D x = \frac{1}{2} \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\bigl[|w(x) - w(y)|\bigr] \Ge 0,
\end{equation*}
with equality if and only if $w$ is $\mu$-almost-everywhere constant.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $\mu^2/\nu = \mu w$, the definition of $S$ gives
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu^2}{\nu}S\D x = \mathbb E_\mu\bigl[w(x)S(x)\bigr] = \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\bigl[w(x)\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr].
\end{equation*}
Symmetrizing in $x\leftrightarrow y$ gives
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu^2}{\nu}S\D x = \frac{1}{2} \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\bigl[(w(x) - w(y))\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr] = \frac{1}{2} \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\bigl[ |w(x) - w(y)| \bigr].
\end{equation*}
\end{proof}

Applying Lemma~\ref{lem: appendix-rankcorrelation} with $\nu = \nu_t$ and $S = S_t$, the dissipation can be rewritten as
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\KL(\mu\|\nu_t) = -\chi^2(\mu\|\nu_t) - \lambda \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\!\left[ \left|\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu_t(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu_t(y)}\right| \right].
    \label{eq: appendix-combined-rate}
\end{equation}
The elementary inequality $w\log w \Le w^2 - w$ applied to $w = \mu/\nu_t$ gives $\chi^2(\mu\|\nu_t) \Ge \KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$, hence $\frac{\D}{\D t}\KL(\mu\|\nu_t) \Le -\KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$, and Gr\"onwall's lemma yields the exponential decay
\begin{equation}
    \KL(\mu\|\nu_t) \Le e^{-t} \KL(\mu\|\nu_0),\qquad t \Ge 0,
    \label{eq: appendix-KL-exponential}
\end{equation}
with no structural assumption on $\mu$: neither log-concavity, nor a spectral gap, nor any growth condition. This contrasts with the Wasserstein-2 geometry, where the forward KL is not displacement convex and an analogous exponential rate is unavailable without further assumptions.

The uniform convergence rate \eqref{eq: appendix-KL-exponential} should \emph{not} be read as a blanket statement that minimizing the forward KL is intrinsically faster or more efficient than minimizing the reverse KL. The FR gradient flow is a continuous-time idealization on the space of densities and does not account for sampling cost. In practice, evaluating the forward KL and its gradient requires samples from $\mu$, and producing representative samples in high dimensions is itself difficult. The empirical KLXX objective therefore also evaluates the exact log-ratio on the quench and temper measure $\hat\mu$, which is intended to supply support information that may be absent from the target-sample measure. The bound above quantifies convergence given exact target expectations; it does not establish the behavior of this additional empirical term or remove the cost of constructing its samples.

A naive comparison of dissipation rates between forward KL and $\KL$+$\lambda\X_\mu$ would also be misleading, since scaling the loss by a constant is mere time reparametrization with no qualitative gain. The substantive contribution of $\X_\mu$ is structural rather than rate-based: the term from forward KL depends locally on the value of $\mu(x)/\nu_t(x)$, whereas the rank correlation $S_t$ compares that value with the complete distribution of importance weights under $\mu$. The regularized flow \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-L-flow} therefore couples the update at $x$ to this rank correlation, which the term from forward KL does not contain.

Two further cautions concern the discrete realization of the flow. First, the time-reparametrization argument cuts in both directions once the optimizer enters. Training follows Adam steps, not the FR gradient flow: the per-coordinate second-moment normalization absorbs a global rescaling of the loss, so a larger gradient buys no speedup by itself, while a regularizer that reshapes the relative magnitudes of the gradient components does change the optimizer's effective time scale. Part of any observed difference in training speed between forward KL and $\KL$+$\lambda\X_\mu$ may therefore be optimizer scaling rather than information geometry, and we claim no universal transient speedup on this basis. Second, the dissipation \eqref{eq: appendix-combined-rate} is a density-space statement, while each gradient step uses a finite, generally biased sample estimate. The staged clock experiment examines this finite-batch setting with a schedule-matched comparison: its quantitative claims use the selected-proposal ESS over the validation set at each stage, not a composed endpoint estimate or a diagnostic computed on a training mini-batch.

Despite these cautions, the density-space flow retains a further monotonicity property: $\X_\mu$ decreases along the regularized flow. Pairing the variation $\delta\X_\mu/\delta\nu = -2wS$ \eqref{eq: appendix-var-X} against the flow \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-L-flow} gives
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu_t) = -2\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu^2}{\nu_t} S_t \D x - 4\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu^2}{\nu_t} S_t^2 \D x  \Le  0.
\end{equation*}
The first term is nonpositive by Lemma~\ref{lem: appendix-rankcorrelation} and vanishes only when $\mu/\nu_t$ is $\mu$-almost-everywhere constant. Hence the inequality is strict away from that case, and $\X_\mu$ is driven to zero along the flow.

\subsection{Asymptotic accuracy for a fixed biased surrogate}
\label{subsec: appendix-accuracy}

The convergence analysis of Section~\ref{subsec: appendix-FR-convergence} takes the outer expectation in $\KL(\mu\|\nu)+\lambda\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)$ under the exact target. To isolate surrogate bias, fix a smooth density $\tilde\mu$ that does not vary with $\nu$ and define
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{\mathcal L}[\nu]
    = \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\log w(x)\D x
    + \lambda\!\iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\tilde\mu(y)\bigl|\log w(x) - \log w(y)\bigr|\D x\D y,
    \label{eq: appendix-surrogate-loss}
\end{equation}
where the pointwise ratio $w(x)=\mu(x)/\nu(x)$ is still evaluated against the exact target. Repeating the gradient calculation of Section~\ref{subsec: appendix-FR-grad-calc} with $\mu$ replaced by $\tilde\mu$ only in the outer integrals gives
\begin{equation*}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\bigl[\widetilde{\mathcal L}\bigr](x) = \nu(x) - \tilde\mu(x)\bigl(1 + 2\lambda \widetilde S(x)\bigr),
    \qquad
    \widetilde S(x) := \mathbb E_{y\sim\tilde\mu}\bigl[\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr].
\end{equation*}
A stationary density $\nu^\star$ of this gradient flow therefore satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \nu^\star(x) = \tilde\mu(x)\biggl[1 + 2\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\tilde\mu(y)\operatorname{sgn}\biggl(\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu^\star(y)}\biggr)\D y\biggr].
    \label{eq: appendix-surrogate-stationary}
\end{equation}
At $\lambda=0$, the equation reduces to $\nu^\star=\tilde\mu$ and the residual error is $\KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu)$. For $\lambda>0$, the rank correlation $\widetilde S^\star$ enters through $1+2\lambda\widetilde S^\star$, raising $\nu^\star$ relative to $\tilde\mu$ where $\mu/\nu^\star$ is comparatively high and lowering it where the ratio is low. For $\lambda<\frac12$, \eqref{eq: appendix-surrogate-stationary} is positive because $\widetilde S^\star\in[-1,1]$. Antisymmetry of the sign also gives
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\mathbb R^d} \tilde\mu(x)\, \widetilde S^\star(x)\, \D x
    = \mathbb E_{x, y \sim \tilde\mu}\biggl[\operatorname{sgn}\biggl(\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu^\star(y)}\biggr)\biggr] = 0,
\end{equation*}
hence every stationary point $\nu^\star = \tilde\mu(1 + 2\lambda \widetilde S^\star)$ is a probability density.

We now prove Theorem~\ref{thm: accuracy} of Section~\ref{sec: FR}, whose fixed-surrogate loss \eqref{eq: surrogate-loss-main} is the loss \eqref{eq: appendix-surrogate-loss} derived above.

\begin{proof}
Substituting the stationary identity $\nu^\star = \tilde\mu(1+2\lambda\widetilde S^\star)$ into the difference of forward KLs and using $\mu = \nu^\star \cdot (\mu/\nu^\star) = \tilde\mu(1+2\lambda\widetilde S^\star)(\mu/\nu^\star)$,
\begin{equation*}
    \KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu) - \KL(\mu\|\nu^\star)
    = \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\mu(x)\log\bigl(1 + 2\lambda\widetilde S^\star(x)\bigr)\D x
    = \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x) \frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)}u(x)\log u(x)\D x,
\end{equation*}
where $u(x) := 1 + 2\lambda\widetilde S^\star(x) \Ge 1 - 2\lambda > 0$, since $\widetilde S^\star \in [-1, 1]$ (a $\tilde\mu$-average of signs) and $\lambda < \frac12$. The elementary inequality $u\log u \Ge u - 1$ for $u > 0$ then yields
\begin{equation*}
    \KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu) - \KL(\mu\|\nu^\star)
    \Ge 2\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x) \frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} \widetilde S^\star(x)\D x.
\end{equation*}
Applying the rank correlation identity of Lemma~\ref{lem: appendix-rankcorrelation} verbatim with $\mu$ replaced by $\tilde\mu$ in the outer measure,
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)}\widetilde S^\star(x)\D x = \frac{1}{2}\!\iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\tilde\mu(y) \biggl|\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu^\star(y)}\biggr|\D x\D y,
\end{equation*}
which gives \eqref{eq: accuracy-bound}.
\end{proof}

At $\lambda=0$, the bound is attained by $\nu^\star=\tilde\mu$. For $0<\lambda<\frac12$, the subtracted discrepancy is nonnegative and is positive unless $\mu/\nu^\star$ is constant on the support of $\tilde\mu$. Thus, within the theorem's assumptions, $\X_\mu$ improves the stationary-error bound for a fixed biased surrogate.

The threshold $\lambda<\frac12$ is a sufficient condition for strict positivity of $\nu^\star$. The experiments use $\lambda=1$ and do not extend the theorem beyond its stated range. The live surrogate also changes with the current flow and is represented by finite batches, so it is not the fixed smooth density assumed here.

\section{Proof of the propagation factor analysis}
\label{sec: appendix-propagation}

Section~\ref{sec: propagation-analysis} states the error bound and the two propagation factors read from it. This appendix proves Theorem~\ref{thm: propagation} and identifies the asymptotic variance of the flow and resample scheme. Throughout, $\mu_k$ are the accepted stage targets, $\nu_k=(G_k^{-1})_{\#}\mu_{k-1}$ the stage proposals, $w_k=\D\mu_k/\D\nu_k$ the stage importance weights with $w_k>0$, and $\mu_K^N$ the equally weighted empirical measure of the validation set returned at stage $K$. We write $\lVert\cdot\rVert_2$ for the $L^2$ norm over all the randomness of the algorithm. The scheme evaluates $w_k$ at particles rather than integrating it, so a pointwise defined weight is part of its specification. We therefore fix each $w_k$ once as a version that is everywhere positive and everywhere bounded by $\lVert w_k\rVert_\infty$. Since $w_k>0$ and $w_k\Le\lVert w_k\rVert_\infty$ both hold $\nu_k$-almost everywhere, the two constraints remove only $\nu_k$-null sets, so $\mu_k$ is unchanged; and the law of every $Y_k^i$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $\nu_k$, so the realized weights are almost surely unaffected by the choice. The last claim is an induction: $Y_1^i$ has law exactly $\nu_1$; resampling only copies the $Y_k^j$, so $\operatorname{law}(X_k^i)\ll\nu_k\ll\mu_k$, the second relation because $w_k>0$; and applying $G_{k+1}^{-1}$ gives $\operatorname{law}(Y_{k+1}^i)\ll\nu_{k+1}$.

The proof has three steps. A change of variables carries every particle back through the composed stage maps, after which the propagation step is the identity and only reweighting and resampling remain. An estimate for a single accepted stage then bounds its error by the oscillation of the observable and the supremum of the stage weight. Iterating that estimate across the accepted stages gives a recursion whose solution is the constant of Theorem~\ref{thm: propagation}.

\subsection{Reduction by change of variables}
\label{subsec: appendix-pullback}

The stage maps move the particles but leave the weights attached to them, so they can be removed from the analysis by expressing every accepted stage in the coordinates of the source. Write $\Theta_k := G_k^{-1}\circ\cdots\circ G_1^{-1}$, with $\Theta_0 := \mathrm{id}$, for the composition of the maps applied through stage $k$, so that $\Theta_k$ is what a particle has undergone by the end of stage $k$ and is obtained from $\Theta_{k-1}$ by appending $G_k^{-1}$ on the left.

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem: appendix-pullback}
Set $\varrho_k := (\Theta_k^{-1})_{\#}\mu_k$ and $r_k := w_k\circ\Theta_k$, so that $0 < r_k \Le \lVert w_k\rVert_\infty$ everywhere. Then $\varrho_0=\mu_0$, $(\Theta_k^{-1})_{\#}\nu_k = \varrho_{k-1}$, and $r_k$ is a version of $\D\varrho_k/\D\varrho_{k-1}$. In these coordinates the propagation step is the identity, so the scheme is reweighting and resampling on a fixed space, with the maps entering only through the sequence $\{\varrho_k\}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
From $\Theta_k = G_k^{-1}\circ\Theta_{k-1}$ we get $\Theta_k^{-1} = \Theta_{k-1}^{-1}\circ G_k$ and hence $\Theta_k^{-1}\circ G_k^{-1} = \Theta_{k-1}^{-1}$, so
\begin{equation*}
    (\Theta_k^{-1})_{\#}\nu_k = (\Theta_k^{-1}\circ G_k^{-1})_{\#}\mu_{k-1} = (\Theta_{k-1}^{-1})_{\#}\mu_{k-1} = \varrho_{k-1}.
\end{equation*}
The remaining identity is the only analytic ingredient. Pushing numerator and denominator forward by the same bi-measurable bijection $\phi$ leaves the density ratio unchanged, up to relabeling the point at which it is evaluated: for $\alpha\ll\beta$,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\D\phi_{\#}\alpha}{\D\phi_{\#}\beta}\circ\phi = \frac{\D\alpha}{\D\beta} .
    \label{eq: appendix-cov}
\end{equation}
Each $\Theta_k$ is such a map, since the stage maps are diffeomorphisms. With $\phi=\Theta_k^{-1}$, $\alpha=\mu_k$ and $\beta=\nu_k$ this identifies $r_k$ as a version of $\D\varrho_k/\D\varrho_{k-1}$. Write $\tilde X_k^i := \Theta_k^{-1}(X_k^i)$ for the pulled-back particles. Since $\Theta_k^{-1}(Y_k^i) = \Theta_{k-1}^{-1}(X_{k-1}^i)$, the pulled-back particle does not move at the propagation step, and the stage weights agree,
\begin{equation*}
    W_k^i = w_k(Y_k^i) = r_k\bigl(\tilde X_{k-1}^i\bigr).
\end{equation*}
Two particle systems are in play: the original one, and the system that starts from $\tilde X_0^{1:N} = X_0^{1:N}$ and, at each accepted stage, weights its current particles by $r_k$ and resamples. The displays above show that the second sees the same weight vector as the first. Multinomial resampling draws indices from that vector and copies the corresponding values, so driving both systems with the same index draws preserves $\tilde X_k^i = \Theta_k^{-1}(X_k^i)$ for every $i$, and the identification propagates by induction on $k$. In particular every $\tilde X_k^i$ is one of the initial draws $\tilde X_0^{1:N}$ from $\varrho_0$.
\end{proof}

This is a change of variables, not a change of scheme: the resampling steps are retained in full. Write $\eta_k^N := \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\tilde X_k^i}$ for the equally weighted empirical measure of the pulled-back particles after resampling at stage $k$, with $\eta_0^N$ the empirical measure of the $N$ initial draws, $\check\eta_k^N$ for the weighted measure before resampling, and $\Phi_k(\eta)(f) := \eta(r_kf)/\eta(r_k)$, so that $\varrho_k = \Phi_k(\varrho_{k-1})$ and $\check\eta_k^N = \Phi_k(\eta_{k-1}^N)$. Thus $\Phi_k$ is the reweighting operator of one accepted stage, acting on the exact sequence and on its particle approximation in the same way, which is what allows the error after stage $k$ to be compared with the error after stage $k-1$.

The next definition is used only in Section~\ref{subsec: appendix-CLT}. For statements back on the original space put $\bar\mu_k := (\Theta_K\circ\Theta_k^{-1})_{\#}\mu_k$, the stage target carried through the remaining maps, so that $\bar\mu_K=\mu_K$, $\bar\mu_0 = (\Theta_K)_{\#}\mu_0$, and
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\D\varrho_K}{\D\varrho_k}\circ\Theta_K^{-1} = \frac{\D\mu_K}{\D\bar\mu_k}.
    \label{eq: appendix-carried}
\end{equation}
This is \eqref{eq: appendix-cov} with $\phi=\Theta_K$: it converts a density ratio between pulled-back measures into the corresponding ratio on the original space.

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm: propagation}}
\label{subsec: appendix-proof}

Two facts are used repeatedly. First $\varrho_{k-1}(r_k)=1$, because $r_k$ is a version of $\D\varrho_k/\D\varrho_{k-1}$ and $\varrho_k$ is a probability measure. Second $0<r_k\Le\lVert w_k\rVert_\infty$ everywhere, by the version convention and Lemma~\ref{lem: appendix-pullback}, so $\eta(r_k)\in(0,\lVert w_k\rVert_\infty]$ for every probability measure $\eta$ and no normalization can vanish or diverge. All suprema and oscillations below are therefore pointwise; for $r_k$ the symbol $\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty$ denotes that pointwise supremum, shown below to equal $\lVert w_k\rVert_\infty$, and no null-set qualification is needed.

A single accepted stage perturbs the estimate through the weighted numerator and through the random normalization. The next lemma separates the two and charges each to a fixed function, $g$ for the numerator and $r_k$ for the normalization. Charging them to fixed functions is what makes the lemma usable, since the induction below controls the particle error only on functions that do not depend on the particles.

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem: appendix-onestep}
Write $\lVert r_k\rVert_\infty$ for the supremum of $r_k$, finite by the convention above together with the hypothesis $\lVert w_k\rVert_\infty<\infty$ of Theorem~\ref{thm: propagation}. Let $\eta$ be a probability measure and $f$ bounded and measurable, and put $g := r_k(f-\varrho_k(f))$. Then $\varrho_{k-1}(g)=0$, $\operatorname{osc}(g)\Le 2\lVert r_k\rVert_\infty\operatorname{osc}(f)$, $\operatorname{osc}(r_k)\Le\lVert r_k\rVert_\infty$, and
\begin{equation*}
    \bigl|\Phi_k(\eta)(f)-\varrho_k(f)\bigr| \Le \bigl|\eta(g)-\varrho_{k-1}(g)\bigr| + \operatorname{osc}(f)\bigl|\eta(r_k)-\varrho_{k-1}(r_k)\bigr|.
\end{equation*}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Using $\varrho_{k-1}(r_kh)=\varrho_k(h)$ and $\varrho_{k-1}(r_k)=1$ gives $\varrho_{k-1}(g)=\varrho_k(f)-\varrho_k(f)=0$. Write $D:=\Phi_k(\eta)(f)-\varrho_k(f)$ and $\delta:=\eta(r_k)-1$. Substituting $\eta(r_kf)=\eta(r_k)\Phi_k(\eta)(f)$,
\begin{equation*}
    \eta(g) = \eta(r_kf)-\varrho_k(f)\eta(r_k) = \eta(r_k)\bigl[\Phi_k(\eta)(f)-\varrho_k(f)\bigr] = D(1+\delta),
\end{equation*}
hence
\begin{equation*}
    D = \eta(g) - D\delta ,
    \qquad
    |D| \Le |\eta(g)| + |D|\,|\delta| .
\end{equation*}
Both $\Phi_k(\eta)(f)$ and $\varrho_k(f)$ are weighted averages of $f$ and lie in $[\inf f,\sup f]$, so $|D|\Le\operatorname{osc}(f)$; applying that bound to the second $|D|$ only, and using $\varrho_{k-1}(g)=0$, gives the display. Finally $\varrho_k(f)\in[\inf f,\sup f]$ gives $\lVert g\rVert_\infty\Le\lVert r_k\rVert_\infty\operatorname{osc}(f)$, and $r_k\Ge0$ bounds the oscillation of $r_k$ by its supremum, so
\begin{equation*}
    \operatorname{osc}(g) \Le 2\lVert g\rVert_\infty \Le 2\lVert r_k\rVert_\infty\operatorname{osc}(f) ,
    \qquad
    \operatorname{osc}(r_k) \Le \lVert r_k\rVert_\infty .
\end{equation*}
\end{proof}

We now prove Theorem~\ref{thm: propagation} of Section~\ref{sec: propagation-analysis}.

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem: appendix-pullback}, $r_j=w_j\circ\Theta_j$ with $\Theta_j$ a bijection, so $\sup r_j=\sup w_j$; the convention gives $\sup w_j\Le\lVert w_j\rVert_\infty$ and an essential supremum never exceeds a supremum, so $\lVert r_j\rVert_\infty=\lVert w_j\rVert_\infty$. The same lemma gives $\mu_K^N(\varphi)=\eta_K^N(f)$ and $\mu_K(\varphi)=\varrho_K(f)$ for $f:=\varphi\circ\Theta_K$, with $\operatorname{osc}(f)=\operatorname{osc}(\varphi)$, so it suffices to bound $\lVert\eta_K^N(f)-\varrho_K(f)\rVert_2$. The recursion is carried by one scalar per accepted stage, the worst-case $L^2$ error over observables of unit oscillation. Set
\begin{equation*}
    \varepsilon_k := \sup\bigl\{\lVert\eta_k^N(h)-\varrho_k(h)\rVert_2 : h \text{ bounded measurable},\ \operatorname{osc}(h)\Le1\bigr\}.
\end{equation*}
This supremum is finite, indeed $\varepsilon_k\Le1$, since $\eta_k^N(h)$ and $\varrho_k(h)$ both lie in $[\inf h,\sup h]$. The quantity $\eta_k^N(h)-\varrho_k(h)$ is unchanged when a constant is added to $h$ and is positively homogeneous in $h$, so applying the definition to $h/\operatorname{osc}(h)$ gives, with both sides vanishing when $\operatorname{osc}(h)=0$,
\begin{equation}
    \lVert\eta_k^N(h)-\varrho_k(h)\rVert_2 \Le \operatorname{osc}(h)\,\varepsilon_k
    \qquad\text{for every bounded } h.
    \label{eq: appendix-eps}
\end{equation}

Since $\eta_0^N$ is the empirical measure of $N$ independent draws from $\varrho_0$, we have $\mathbb E[(\eta_0^N(h)-\varrho_0(h))^2]=\operatorname{Var}_{\varrho_0}(h)/N$, and Popoviciu's inequality bounds a variance by a quarter of the squared range, so $\varepsilon_0\Le 1/(2\sqrt N)$.

The inductive step separates the two sources of error introduced at one accepted stage: the sampling noise added by resampling, and the error inherited from the previous stage and carried through $\Phi_k$. With $\check\eta_k^N=\Phi_k(\eta_{k-1}^N)$, the identity
\begin{equation*}
    \eta_k^N(f)-\varrho_k(f) = \bigl[\eta_k^N(f)-\check\eta_k^N(f)\bigr] + \bigl[\Phi_k(\eta_{k-1}^N)(f)-\varrho_k(f)\bigr]
\end{equation*}
is exact, the two brackets cancelling $\check\eta_k^N(f)$. Conditionally on $\check\eta_k^N$ the stage-$k$ particles are $N$ independent draws from it, so the first bracket has conditional mean zero and conditional second moment $\operatorname{Var}_{\check\eta_k^N}(f)/N\Le\operatorname{osc}(f)^2/(4N)$, again by Popoviciu; the bound is uniform in $\check\eta_k^N$, so its $L^2$ norm is at most $\operatorname{osc}(f)/(2\sqrt N)$. For the second bracket, Lemma~\ref{lem: appendix-onestep} with $\eta=\eta_{k-1}^N$, the triangle inequality, and \eqref{eq: appendix-eps} applied to the fixed bounded functions $g$ and $r_k$ give
\begin{equation*}
    \bigl\lVert\Phi_k(\eta_{k-1}^N)(f)-\varrho_k(f)\bigr\rVert_2
    \Le \bigl[\operatorname{osc}(g)+\operatorname{osc}(f)\operatorname{osc}(r_k)\bigr]\varepsilon_{k-1}
    \Le 3\lVert r_k\rVert_\infty\operatorname{osc}(f)\,\varepsilon_{k-1}.
\end{equation*}
Adding the two by Minkowski's inequality and taking the supremum over $\operatorname{osc}(f)\Le1$ yields
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_k \Le \frac{1}{2\sqrt N} + 3\lVert r_k\rVert_\infty\,\varepsilon_{k-1}.
    \label{eq: appendix-recursion}
\end{equation}
Write $a:=1/(2\sqrt N)$ and $b_j:=3\lVert r_j\rVert_\infty$. We claim $\varepsilon_k\Le a\sum_{i=0}^{k}\prod_{j=i+1}^{k}b_j$, the empty product being one. At $k=0$ the right side is $a$. Assuming it at $k-1$, \eqref{eq: appendix-recursion} gives
\begin{equation*}
    \varepsilon_k \Le a + b_k\,a\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\prod_{j=i+1}^{k-1}b_j
    = a\left[1+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\prod_{j=i+1}^{k}b_j\right]
    = a\sum_{i=0}^{k}\prod_{j=i+1}^{k}b_j,
\end{equation*}
the last equality because the $i=k$ term is the empty product. Taking $k=K$ and applying \eqref{eq: appendix-eps} with $h=f$,
\begin{equation*}
    \lVert\mu_K^N(\varphi)-\mu_K(\varphi)\rVert_2 \Le \operatorname{osc}(\varphi)\,\varepsilon_K
    \Le \frac{\operatorname{osc}(\varphi)}{2\sqrt N}\sum_{i=0}^{K}\prod_{j=i+1}^{K}3\lVert w_j\rVert_\infty ,
\end{equation*}
which is the strong error bound of Section~\ref{sec: propagation-analysis}. Squaring gives the constant $C$ of \eqref{eq: propagation-bound}.
\end{proof}

The bound holds for every $N\Ge1$, with no asymptotics. Its dependence on $K$ is pessimistic, and the following case shows that this is an artifact of the recursion rather than a property of the scheme. Taking $\mu_k\equiv\mu$ and $G_k=\mathrm{id}$ makes every stage weight one, so the scheme is independent sampling followed by $K$ rounds of uniform resampling, and $F_{\Sigma+}=(3^{K+1}-1)/2$ makes $C$ grow like $9^{K}$. The asymptotic variance of Section~\ref{subsec: appendix-CLT} below is $(K+1)\operatorname{Var}_\mu(\varphi)$ in the same case, linear in $K$. Theorem~\ref{thm: propagation} should therefore be read as establishing the rate and the finiteness of the constant, not its size.

\subsection{Central limit theorem and the asymptotic variance}
\label{subsec: appendix-CLT}

The finite-$N$ bound is a worst case over observables. The asymptotic variance identifies instead what each accepted stage costs.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm: appendix-CLT}
If $\chi^2(\bar\mu_m\|\bar\mu_j)<\infty$ for all $0\Le j\Le m\Le K$ and $\varphi$ is bounded, then $\sqrt N(\mu_K^N(\varphi)-\mu_K(\varphi))$ converges in distribution to $\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2(\varphi))$ with
\begin{equation}
    \sigma^2(\varphi) = \sum_{k=0}^{K}\mathbb E_{\bar\mu_k}
    \left[\left(\frac{\D\mu_K}{\D\bar\mu_k}\right)^{2}\bigl(\varphi-\mu_K(\varphi)\bigr)^{2}\right].
    \label{eq: appendix-CLT}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

By Lemma~\ref{lem: appendix-pullback} the scheme is, in pulled-back coordinates, a particle system with the identity mutation kernel and the resampling steps intact. This is the case \citet{chopin2004central} singles out: when particles are not mutated, the generic particle filter reduces to sequential importance resampling. His Theorem~1 gives the central limit theorem for multinomial resampling through a variance recursion whose mutation term vanishes under the identity kernel. Write $V_k(h)$ for the asymptotic variance of $\sqrt N(\eta_k^N(h)-\varrho_k(h))$, and put $R_k := \D\varrho_K/\D\varrho_k = \prod_{j=k+1}^{K} r_j$ and $\bar f := f - \varrho_K(f)$, with $f = \varphi\circ\Theta_K$ as in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm: propagation}. That recursion reads $V_k(h) = \operatorname{Var}_{\varrho_k}(h) + V_{k-1}(r_k h)$ on $\varrho_k$-centered $h$, with $V_0(h) = \operatorname{Var}_{\varrho_0}(h)$, and unrolls to
\begin{equation}
    V_K(\bar f) = \sum_{k=0}^{K} \mathbb E_{\varrho_k}\bigl[R_k^{2}\,\bar f^{2}\bigr],
    \label{eq: appendix-unrolled}
\end{equation}
each summand being a second moment because $\varrho_k(R_k\bar f) = \varrho_K(\bar f) = 0$. Transporting \eqref{eq: appendix-unrolled} by $\Theta_K$ through \eqref{eq: appendix-carried} gives \eqref{eq: appendix-CLT}: the accumulated ratio $R_k$ becomes $\D\mu_K/\D\bar\mu_k$ on the original space, and $\bar f$ becomes $\varphi-\mu_K(\varphi)$. The hypothesis is a family because the recursion visits every pair of stages. Its members with $m=j+1$ are the per-stage conditions $\chi^2_k<\infty$ for $1\Le k\Le K$, writing $\chi^2_k := \chi^2(\mu_k\|\nu_k)$, which make each single reweighting square integrable; its intermediate members control the fluctuation of the random normalization $\eta_{m-1}^N(r_m)$, whose own variance $V_{m-1}(r_m-1)$ involves the accumulated ratios $\D\varrho_{m-1}/\D\varrho_j$ for every $j<m-1$; and its $m=K$ members, with $\varphi$ bounded, are what make $\sigma^2(\varphi)$ finite. The family is closed under the products the recursion forms, since $(\D\varrho_{m-1}/\D\varrho_j)\,r_m = \D\varrho_m/\D\varrho_j$. Theorem~\ref{thm: propagation} and Theorem~\ref{thm: appendix-CLT} are consistent: the former is a finite-$N$ bound obtained from a worst-case recursion, the latter the exact asymptotic variance, and \eqref{eq: appendix-CLT} carries no factor $3^{K-k}$.
