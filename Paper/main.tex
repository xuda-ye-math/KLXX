\begin{abstract}
Training normalizing flow Boltzmann generators with the forward KL is fragile in three complementary ways: it is silent about modes the flow misses (the effective sample size can stay near one while a large fraction of the target mass is absent), it cannot see off-support leakage in regions the target excludes, and it oscillates transversally on thin low-dimensional manifold supports. We address all three by augmenting the forward KL with a family of cross-regularization functionals $\X_\omega$ that penalize the pairwise variation of $\log(\mu/\nu)$ under a non-negative weight $\omega$. Three concrete instances are studied, $\X_\mu$ for in-mode shape control, $\X_{\hat\mu}$ for stable mode discovery against a wide-coverage measure $\hat\mu$, and $\X_{\bar\nu}$ for off-support leakage suppression, together with a balanced mixture that combines $\hat\mu$ and $\bar\nu$ inside a single pairwise integral. A Fisher--Rao analysis shows that the forward KL augmented with $\X_\mu$ decays exponentially with no structural assumption on $\mu$, and that the asymptotic accuracy floor under biased AIS samples is strictly improved. The framework is conservative on low-dimensional manifold targets with roughly uniform along-manifold density. Heavy-tailed manifold targets remain hard: the quench-and-temper mode-finder that supplies $\hat\mu$ tends to miss the thin outer tails, and the regularizer inherits the blind spot.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Drawing samples from a target distribution $\mu \propto \exp(-U)$ on $\mathbb R^d$ known only up to its normalization constant arises across molecular simulation, Bayesian inference, and statistical physics, and is widely tackled with Boltzmann generators built on normalizing flows \citep{rezende2015variational,papamakarios2017masked,kingma2018glow,noe2019boltzmann,wu2020stochastic}. A flow is an invertible parametric map $G:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^d$ trained so that the pushforward $\nu := G^{-1}_{\#}\mu_0$ of an easily samplable reference $\mu_0$ (typically a Gaussian) approximates $\mu$. The trained flow returns independent samples from $\nu$ along with an exact, tractable density, and importance reweighting against $\mu$ then converts those into Monte Carlo estimates with controllable finite-sample error.

Two training objectives dominate the literature. The original Boltzmann generator \citep{noe2019boltzmann} and its variational-inference predecessor \citep{rezende2015variational} minimize the reverse KL
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{KL}(\nu\|\mu) = \int_{\mathbb R^d} \nu(x) \log \frac{\nu(x)}{\mu(x)} \D x,
    \label{eq: reverse KL}
\end{equation}
which needs only samples from $\nu$ and is therefore cheap to estimate. It is, however, mode-seeking: on multimodal $\mu$ the flow tends to collapse onto a subset of the modes from which it cannot recover \citep{noe2019boltzmann,wu2020stochastic}. The opposing family minimizes the forward KL \citep{gabrie2022adaptive,wirnsberger2020targeted},
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\nu) = \int_{\mathbb R^d} \mu(x) \log \frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} \D x,
    \label{eq: forward KL}
\end{equation}
which is mode-covering but needs samples from $\mu$. Such samples are produced by annealed importance sampling (AIS) \citep{neal2001annealed}, which transports $\nu$ toward $\mu$ through a ladder of intermediate distributions while accumulating importance weights. The flow-AIS bootstrap (FAB) of \citet{midgley2022flow} couples AIS to flow training and replaces the target by the mode-sensitive surrogate $\mu^2/\nu$ to encourage exploration.

A separate line of work drops the explicit-density requirement and trains a diffusion- or SDE-based sampler directly: the path-integral sampler \citep{zhang2022path}, denoising diffusion samplers \citep{vargas2023denoising}, and the more recent iterated denoising / energy-matching family \citep{akhound2024iterated,woo2024iefm} learn a stochastic process whose terminal distribution matches $\mu$. These samplers are expressive and scale well, but they do not deliver an exact tractable density of their output, so importance reweighting against $\mu$ is no longer available. We commit to flow-based methods for that reason: the invertibility of $G$ gives an explicit $\nu$ and therefore an exact, finite-sample importance-sampling pipeline, in which the effective sample size (ESS) \citep{liu2001monte,doucet2001introduction,martino2017rethinking} computed from the flow's own weights is a standard and convenient diagnostic of training quality, with known blind spots that motivate the failure-mode analysis below.

Forward KL training has three complementary failure modes that we use to organize the rest of the paper. The first is silent missed modes, the fake ESS pitfall: ESS is gold-standard only when $\mu$ and $\nu$ are evaluated on the same support, so if $\nu$ misses modes of $\mu$, the AIS chain initialized at $\nu$ rarely escapes the basin $\nu$ already covers \citep{noe2019boltzmann,midgley2022flow} and the resulting ESS can be close to $1$ while a large fraction of the mass of $\mu$ is missing. The second is off-support leakage. Where $\mu$ vanishes, the forward KL integrand $\mu \log(\mu/\nu)$ also vanishes, so the gradient is blind to mass that $\nu$ places outside the support of $\mu$. The third is transversal oscillation on low-dimensional manifolds. When $\mu$ concentrates on a thin manifold, the forward KL controls only the in-manifold marginal of $\log(\mu/\nu)$, and the off-manifold profile of $\nu$ is left free to oscillate. All three blind spots persist no matter how accurately AIS samples the modes $\nu$ already reaches.

Our remedy is to augment the forward KL with a family of cross-regularization functionals that penalize variation of the log-ratio $\log(\mu/\nu)$ directly. Let
\begin{equation}
    \X_\omega(\mu\|\nu) = \iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d} \omega(x,y)
    \biggl|\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} - \log\frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)}\biggr| \D x \D y,
    \label{eq: X}
\end{equation}
where $\omega$ is a non-negative measure on $\mathbb R^{2d}$. The motivation is direct: matching $\nu$ to $\mu$ is the same as flattening $\log(\mu/\nu)$, and the pairwise difference cancels the unknown normalization of $\mu$. Suitably localized choices of $\omega$ provide the three safeguards corresponding to the three failure modes above: stable mode discovery (against silent missed modes), off-support leakage suppression (against gradient blindness off the support of $\mu$), and shape accuracy on low-dimensional manifolds (against transversal oscillation). As a loss ingredient $\X_\omega$ has three properties that we use throughout. First, it gives pointwise control: the forward KL constrains $\nu \approx \mu$ only in a global Jensen-averaged sense, whereas $\X_\omega$ penalizes log-ratio variation wherever $\omega$ places mass and localizes the constraint to the support of $\omega$. Second, it is robust to the sampling of $\omega$: $\X_\omega$ vanishes whenever $\log(\mu/\nu)$ is constant on the support of $\omega$, so its minimizer is insensitive to the precise shape of $\omega$, and even hand-injected mode locations can be added to $\omega$ without corrupting it. Third, it is compatible with the forward KL: for $\omega = \mu \otimes \mu$ the integrand of $\X_\omega$ coincides with that of the forward KL up to a sign, so the same autograd graph and the same $\mu$-samples are reused at negligible extra cost, the scales match, and no extra hyperparameter is needed.

Each choice of $\omega$ localizes the constraint to a region of $\mathbb R^d$; we use three choices with complementary roles. With $\omega = \mu \otimes \mu$,
\begin{equation}
    \X_\mu(\mu\|\nu) = \iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}
    \mu(x) \mu(y) \biggl|\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} - \log\frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)}\biggr| \D x \D y,
    \label{eq: X-mu}
\end{equation}
which is essentially free by the forward KL compatibility above. The forward KL controls the $\mu$-mean of $\log(\mu/\nu)$ while $\X_\mu$ controls its $\mu$-spread, tightening the in-mode density match once $\nu$ has reached the modes of $\mu$ and reducing the residual bias inherited from imperfect AIS samples.

With $\omega = \hat\mu \otimes \hat\mu$,
\begin{equation}
    \X_{\hat\mu}(\mu\|\nu) = \iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}
    \hat\mu(x) \hat\mu(y) \biggl|\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} - \log\frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)}\biggr| \D x \D y,
    \label{eq: X-hat-mu}
\end{equation}
where $\hat\mu$ is a coarse, deliberately over-spread approximation of $\mu$. By sample robustness the minimizer of $\X_{\hat\mu}$ is insensitive to the precise shape of $\hat\mu$, so the sole purpose of $\hat\mu$ is to place mass on modes and tails that the current $\nu$ has not yet covered; the gradient then pulls the flow toward those regions. We build $\hat\mu$ once, before training, with a quench and temper (QT) algorithm that diffuses an initial cloud through Brownian noise to bridge basins, runs a deterministic mode-finder to collapse it onto mode centers, and rejuvenates with a short Langevin chain. QT errs on the side of broad coverage and works well on targets with disconnected modes and on thin low-dimensional manifolds whose density along the manifold is roughly uniform. It has a known weakness on heavy-tailed targets: the thin, low-mass outer tail is easily missed because the diffusion step rarely scatters particles close enough, and the deterministic mode-finder pulls any near miss back into the wider basins of the body.

With $\omega = \bar\nu \otimes \bar\nu$,
\begin{equation}
    \X_{\bar\nu}(\mu\|\nu) = \iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}
    \bar\nu(x) \bar\nu(y) \biggl|\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} - \log\frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)}\biggr| \D x \D y,
    \label{eq: X-nu}
\end{equation}
where $\bar\nu$ denotes a stop-gradient copy of the current pushforward $\nu$. Sampling from $\bar\nu$ is free, since the training loop already pushes a batch from $\mu_0$ through $G^{-1}$ at every step; the detached gradient keeps the loss numerically stable on sharp targets where a true reverse KL gradient explodes. $\X_{\bar\nu}$ plays the reverse KL role without its pathologies: it penalizes mass that $\nu$ places outside the support of $\mu$, suppressing the mode-overfitting leakage that $\X_\mu$ and $\X_{\hat\mu}$ alone cannot see.

The three weights are complementary: $\X_\mu$ refines what AIS already reaches, $\X_{\hat\mu}$ pulls $\nu$ toward modes it has missed, and $\X_{\bar\nu}$ pushes $\nu$ away from regions outside the support of $\mu$. Our preferred training loss combines all three with the forward KL,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}[\nu] = \KL(\mu\|\nu) + \lambda \X_\mu(\mu\|\nu) + \X_{(\alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu)\otimes(\alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu)}(\mu\|\nu),
    \label{eq: total-loss}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda,\alpha,\beta \Ge 0$ are tunable weights and the third term is $\X_\omega$ with mixture weight $\omega = \alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu$. This mixture form is strictly stronger than the sum $\alpha^2 \X_{\hat\mu} + \beta^2 \X_{\bar\nu}$: the pairwise integral also generates cross contributions $\bigl|\log(\mu/\nu)(x) - \log(\mu/\nu)(y)\bigr|$ with $x \sim \hat\mu$ and $y \sim \bar\nu$, which anchor the in-support and out-of-support log-ratios to each other. We confirm this empirically: across our 2D benchmarks the mixture variant attains the best full-coverage ESS while always reaching complete mode coverage.

Equation~\eqref{eq: total-loss} is written in the abstract density $\nu$, but the trainable object is the flow map $G$. By the change of variables $\nu(y) = \mu_0(G(y))|\det J_G(y)|$, and writing $U_0 = -\log\mu_0$ for the source potential, the log-ratio along the flow is the single field
\begin{equation*}
    \log\frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)} = U_0(G(y)) - U(y) - \log|\det J_G(y)|  =:  z(y),
\end{equation*}
up to an additive constant. Substituting into \eqref{eq: total-loss} expresses the preferred loss directly as an objective in $G$,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}[G]
    = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \mu}\bigl[z(y)\bigr]}_{\KL(\mu\|\nu)}
    + \lambda \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \mu}\bigl[|z(y) - z(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)}
    + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu}\bigl[|z(y) - z(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_{\alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu}(\mu\|\nu)} ,
    \label{eq: total-loss-G-intro}
\end{equation}
which is the loss we minimize by stochastic gradient descent and the central object of this paper. Every term is read off a single forward pass of $G$ on a batch -- the forward KL is the batch mean of $z$, and each X term the mean absolute pairwise difference of $z$ over the batch -- so the regularizers cost essentially nothing beyond a vanilla forward KL step. Section~\ref{sec: FR} analyzes the Fisher--Rao gradient flow of this loss, and Section~\ref{sec: training} details the annealed importance sampling and quench-and-temper sampling that supply its three expectations.

We pair ESS with the coverage metric of \citet{naeem2020reliable} for evaluation, which counts the fraction of QT-discovered $\hat\mu$ samples whose $k$-nearest-neighbor ball is reached by the trained $\nu$. Coverage exposes the modes that a fake ESS hides, so the two metrics together certify both density calibration and mode coverage.

This paper makes three contributions. We introduce the cross-regularization framework $\X_\omega$, study three concrete instances $\X_\mu$, $\X_{\hat\mu}$, $\X_{\bar\nu}$, and identify the mixture variant $\X_{(\alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu)\otimes(\alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu)}$ that combines mode discovery and leakage suppression inside a single pairwise integral. We carry out a Fisher--Rao convergence analysis showing that the forward KL augmented with $\X_\mu$ decays exponentially with no structural assumption on $\mu$, and that the asymptotic accuracy floor under biased AIS samples is strictly improved by the $\X_\mu$ term. We report an empirical study on five 2D benchmarks (Threewell, Himmelblau, Annulus, Python, Chessboard), where, among the four losses we compare, the mixture variant is the only one that both matches or beats the best full-coverage ESS and reaches full mode coverage on every target, together with a high-dimensional product multi-well family up to $d = 128$, where the gap between the X-augmented losses and the bare forward KL widens with the dimension.

\section{Fisher--Rao gradient flow with X functionals}
\label{sec: FR}

Before turning to the training algorithm, we study the gradient-flow properties of the loss on the space of densities; the convergence rate of this continuous-time idealization is a clean proxy for how trainable the corresponding discrete loss is in practice. We view $\nu$ as a generic probability density evolving along the gradient flow of the loss under an appropriate Riemannian metric on the space of densities \citep{ambrosio2008gradient,villani2009optimal} and ask how fast $\nu_t$ converges to the target $\mu$ in $\KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$.

In this section we restrict to the loss with only the forward KL and the raw-target regularizer $\X_\mu$,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal L[\nu] = \KL(\mu\|\nu) + \lambda  \X_\mu(\mu\|\nu) = \int_{\mathbb R^d} \mu(x) \log \frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} \D x +
    \lambda\iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}
     \mu(x)\mu(y) \biggl|\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} - \log\frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)}\biggr| \D x \D y,
    \label{eq: loss KL X}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda>0$ is a fixed constant.
Throughout this section we abbreviate the importance weight by
\begin{equation*}
    w(x) := \frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)},
\end{equation*}
so that the log-ratio is $\log w(x) = \log\mu(x) - \log\nu(x)$.

The choice of Riemannian metric matters. In the Wasserstein-2 geometry, induced by optimal transport \citep{jordan1998variational,otto2001geometry}, the forward KL fails to be displacement convex along Wasserstein-2 geodesics, and the standard Otto--Villani / Bakry--{\'E}mery machinery \citep{otto2000generalization,bakry2014analysis} that yields exponential convergence for the reverse KL does not apply. We therefore work in the Fisher--Rao geometry \citep{amari2016information,bauer2016uniqueness}, in which the forward KL admits an unconditional exponential decay rate and the regularizer $\X_\mu$ contributes a qualitatively new, nonlocal birth--death modulation \citep{lu2019accelerating}: the local update at $x$ is driven by how the importance weight at $x$ ranks against the population of weights under $\mu$.

Denoting the $\nu$-mean by $\langle \cdot \rangle_\nu := \int( \cdot ) \nu \D x$, the Fisher--Rao gradient of a functional $\mathcal F[\nu]$ and the associated dissipation identity along the FR gradient flow $\partial_t\nu_t = -\mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal F(\nu_t)$ are
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal F(\nu) = \nu\biggl(\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu} - \Big\langle\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu}\Big\rangle_\nu\biggr),
    \qquad
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\mathcal F[\nu_t] = -\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\biggl(\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu} - \Big\langle\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu}\Big\rangle_{\nu_t}\biggr)^{2}\nu_t \D x.
    \label{eq: FR-gradient}
\end{equation}
The mean-subtraction enforces $\int\mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal F = 0$, so the FR flow conserves probability mass automatically.

\subsection{Derivation of $\X_\mu$-augmented Fisher--Rao gradient flow}
\label{subsec: FR-grad-calc}

The first variation of the forward KL with respect to $\nu$ admits the simple form
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\delta \KL(\mu\|\nu)}{\delta \nu}(x) = -\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} = -w(x),
    \label{eq: var-KL}
\end{equation}
so by \eqref{eq: FR-gradient} the FR gradient of the forward KL is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\big[\KL(\mu\|\nu)\big](x) = \nu(x) - \mu(x).
    \label{eq: FR-grad-KL}
\end{equation}

For the $\X_\mu$ functional, the inner log-ratio difference has first variation
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\delta\bigl(\log w(x) - \log w(y)\bigr)}{\delta\nu(x)} = \frac{1}{\nu(y)}\delta(x-y) - \frac{1}{\nu(x)}.
\end{equation*}
By the symmetry of $\X_\mu$ in $x$ and $y$,
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\delta \X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)}{\delta \nu}(x) & =
    2 \int_{\mathbb R^d} \mu(x) \mu(y) \operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y)) \frac{\delta (\log w(x) - \log w(y))}{\delta\nu(x)} \D y \\
    & = 2 \int_{\mathbb R^d} \mu(x) \mu(y) \operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))
    \bigg(\frac1{\nu(y)}\delta(x-y) - \frac1{\nu(x)}\bigg) \D y \\
    & = -2\int_{\mathbb R^d} w(x) \operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))  \D y,
\end{align*}
where the contribution of $\delta(x-y)$ vanishes because $\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y)) = 0$ at $y = x$. Hence
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\delta \X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)}{\delta \nu}(x) = - 2w(x)S(x),
    \qquad
    S(x) := \mathbb E_{y\sim\mu}\bigl[\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr].
    \label{eq: var-X}
\end{equation}
We call $S$ the \emph{rank field} of the importance weight under $\mu$: $S(x) > 0$ where $w(x)$ exceeds the $\mu$-median of $w$, i.e.\ where $\nu$ undercovers a mode of $\mu$; $S(x) < 0$ where $\nu$ overcovers; and $S \equiv 0$ at $\nu = \mu$. By antisymmetry,
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbb E_\mu[S] = \mathbb E_{x,y\sim \mu}\bigl[\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr] = 0,
\end{equation*}
and the FR gradient of $\X_\mu$ with respect to $\nu$ is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\big[\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)\big](x) = -2\mu(x) S(x).
    \label{eq: FR-grad-X}
\end{equation}

Since the FR gradient is linear in the functional, the FR gradient of $\mathcal L[\nu] = \KL(\mu\|\nu) + \lambda \X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)$ is the direct sum of \eqref{eq: FR-grad-KL} and \eqref{eq: FR-grad-X}:
\begin{equation*}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}[\mathcal L](x) = \mu(x) - \nu(x) + 2\lambda \mu(x) S(x).
\end{equation*}
The augmented Fisher--Rao gradient flow $\partial_t\nu_t = -\mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}[\mathcal L]$ therefore reads
\begin{equation}
    \partial_t\nu_t(x) = \mu(x) - \nu(x) + 2\lambda \mu(x) S_t(x), \quad 
    \text{where~}
    S_t(x) := \mathbb E_{y\sim\mu}\biggl[\operatorname{sgn}\biggl(\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu_t(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu_t(y)}\biggr)\biggr].
    \label{eq: FR-L-flow}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq: FR-L-flow} is a \emph{nonlocal} PDE: its right-hand side at $x$ depends on $S_t(x)$, which in turn requires the entire profile of the importance weight $\mu/\nu_t$ over the support of $\mu$. Mass is conserved along the flow because
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\nu_t(x) \D x = 2\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\mu(x) S_t(x) \D x = 0,
\end{equation*}
and the flow is stationary at $\nu_t = \mu$, where $\mu(x)/\nu_t(x) \equiv 1$ and hence $S_t\equiv 0$. When $\lambda = 0$, \eqref{eq: FR-L-flow} reduces to the pure Fisher--Rao gradient flow of the forward KL.

\subsection{Convergence analysis of the $\X_\mu$-augmented flow}
\label{subsec: FR-convergence}

To analyze the convergence rate of the augmented flow \eqref{eq: FR-L-flow}, we take the forward KL $\KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$ as the error metric and differentiate it along the flow:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\KL(\mu\|\nu_t) = -\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\frac{\mu^2}{\nu_t}(1 + 2\lambda S_t) \D x + \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\mu \D x = -\chi^2(\mu\|\nu_t) - 2\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\frac{\mu^2}{\nu_t} S_t \D x,
\end{equation*}
where the chi-squared divergence is
\begin{equation*}
    \chi^2(\mu\|\nu) := \int_{\mathbb R^d}(w-1)^2\nu\D x = \int_{\mathbb R^d} w^2\nu\D x - 1.
\end{equation*}
The sign of the second integral is settled by the following rank-correlation identity.

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem: rank-correlation}
For any probability density $\nu$ on $\mathbb R^d$,
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu(x)^2}{\nu(x)}S(x)\D x = \frac{1}{2} \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\bigl[|w(x) - w(y)|\bigr] \Ge 0,
\end{equation*}
with equality if and only if $w$ is $\mu$-almost-everywhere constant, i.e.\ $\nu = \mu$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By the definition of $S$,
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu^2}{\nu}S\D x = \mathbb E_\mu\bigl[w(x)S(x)\bigr] = \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\bigl[w(x)\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr].
\end{equation*}
Symmetrizing in $x\leftrightarrow y$ and averaging the two equal copies gives
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu^2}{\nu}S\D x = \frac{1}{2} \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\bigl[(w(x) - w(y))\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr] = \frac{1}{2} \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\bigl[ |w(x) - w(y)| \bigr].
\end{equation*}
\end{proof}

Applying Lemma~\ref{lem: rank-correlation} with $\nu = \nu_t$ and $S = S_t$, the dissipation rewrites as
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\KL(\mu\|\nu_t) = -\chi^2(\mu\|\nu_t) - \lambda \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\!\left[ \left|\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu_t(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu_t(y)}\right| \right].
    \label{eq: combined-rate}
\end{equation}
The elementary inequality $w\log w \Le w^2 - w$ applied to $w = \mu/\nu_t$ gives $\chi^2(\mu\|\nu_t) \Ge \KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$, hence $\frac{\D}{\D t}\KL(\mu\|\nu_t) \Le -\KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$, and Gr\"onwall's lemma yields the exponential decay
\begin{equation}
    \KL(\mu\|\nu_t) \Le e^{-t} \KL(\mu\|\nu_0),\qquad t \Ge 0,
    \label{eq: KL-exponential}
\end{equation}
with no structural assumption on $\mu$: neither log-concavity, nor a spectral gap, nor any growth condition. This contrasts sharply with the Wasserstein-2 geometry, where the forward KL is not displacement convex and an analogous exponential rate is unavailable without further assumptions.

The uniform convergence rate \eqref{eq: KL-exponential} should \emph{not} be read as a blanket statement that minimizing the forward KL is intrinsically faster or more efficient than minimizing the reverse KL. The FR flow is a continuous-time idealization on the space of densities and is agnostic to the sampling cost. In practice, the difficulty of training has been partially transported to the AIS step: evaluating the forward KL and its gradient requires accurate samples of $\mu$, and producing such samples in high dimensions is itself a hard problem. This is the very reason that AIS is needed, and that the $\X_{\hat\mu}$ regularizer must compensate for modes that AIS alone fails to reach. The exponential rate \eqref{eq: KL-exponential} quantifies how fast the flow \emph{can} converge given accurate target samples; it does not eliminate the burden of producing them.

A naive comparison of dissipation rates between $\KL$-only and $\KL + \lambda \X_\mu$ would also be misleading, since scaling the loss by a constant is mere time reparametrization with no qualitative gain. The substantive contribution of $\X_\mu$ is structural rather than rate-based: while the pure-KL FR gradient is local in $x$, the rank field $S_t$ is global, so the augmented flow \eqref{eq: FR-L-flow} couples the update at $x$ to the entire distribution of importance weights under $\mu$. This nonlocal coupling amplifies the birth rate on undercovered regions of $\mu$ and suppresses it on overcovered ones, a global comparison that pure forward KL (which knows only whether $\mu(x)/\nu_t(x)$ exceeds or falls below $1$ pointwise) cannot produce.

\begin{remark}[Monotone decay of $\X_\mu$]
The regularizer $\X_\mu$ also decreases monotonically along the augmented flow: a direct computation along \eqref{eq: FR-L-flow} gives
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu_t) = -2\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu^2}{\nu_t} S_t \D x - 4\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu^2}{\nu_t} S_t^2 \D x  \Le  0,
\end{equation*}
with strict inequality whenever $\nu_t \ne \mu$. Thus $\X_\mu$ is itself driven to zero in the long-time limit.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Asymptotic accuracy of biased forward KL training}
\label{subsec: accuracy}

The convergence analysis of Section~\ref{subsec: FR-convergence} assumes that the outer expectation in $\KL(\mu\|\nu) + \lambda \X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)$ is taken under the exact target $\mu$. In practice, the algorithm only has access to an approximation $\tilde\mu$ of $\mu$ supplied by AIS, and the realized loss reads
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{\mathcal L}[\nu]
    = \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\log w(x)\D x
    + \lambda\!\iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\tilde\mu(y)\bigl|\log w(x) - \log w(y)\bigr|\D x\D y,
    \label{eq: biased-loss}
\end{equation}
where the pointwise ratio $w(x) = \mu(x)/\nu(x)$ is still evaluated against the exact target, since it is computed per sample from the target potential $U$ and the flow's own density. Repeating the derivation of Section~\ref{subsec: FR-grad-calc} with $\mu$ replaced by $\tilde\mu$ in the outer integrands gives the Fisher--Rao gradient
\begin{equation*}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\bigl[\widetilde{\mathcal L}\bigr](x) = \nu(x) - \tilde\mu(x)\bigl(1 + 2\lambda \widetilde S(x)\bigr),
    \qquad
    \widetilde S(x) := \mathbb E_{y\sim\tilde\mu}\bigl[\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr].
\end{equation*}
A stationary point $\nu^\star$ of \eqref{eq: biased-loss} therefore satisfies the self-consistent equation
\begin{equation}
    \nu^\star(x) = \tilde\mu(x)\biggl[1 + 2\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\tilde\mu(y)\operatorname{sgn}\biggl(\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu^\star(y)}\biggr)\D y\biggr].
    \label{eq: biased-stationary}
\end{equation}
At $\lambda = 0$ the equation collapses to $\nu^\star = \tilde\mu$: pure forward KL training inherits the bias of the AIS distribution verbatim, and the residual accuracy is exactly $\KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu)$. For $\lambda > 0$, however, the rank-based correction $1 + 2\lambda\widetilde S^\star$ inflates $\nu^\star$ above $\tilde\mu$ where the importance weight $\mu/\nu^\star$ is high (i.e.\ where $\nu^\star$ still undercovers $\mu$) and deflates it where the weight is low, providing a global feedback that pulls $\nu^\star$ from $\tilde\mu$ back toward $\mu$. This is quantified in the following theorem.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm: accuracy}
Assume $\mu$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $\tilde\mu$ and $\lambda \in [0, 1/2]$, so that the bracket in \eqref{eq: biased-stationary} is non-negative and $\nu^\star$ is a probability density. Any stationary point $\nu^\star$ of the biased loss \eqref{eq: biased-loss} satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \KL(\mu\|\nu^\star) \Le \KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu) - \lambda\!\iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\tilde\mu(y) \biggl|\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu^\star(y)}\biggr| \D x \D y.
    \label{eq: accuracy-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Substituting the stationary identity $\nu^\star = \tilde\mu(1+2\lambda\widetilde S^\star)$ into the difference of forward KLs and using $\mu = \nu^\star \cdot (\mu/\nu^\star) = \tilde\mu(1+2\lambda\widetilde S^\star)(\mu/\nu^\star)$,
\begin{equation*}
    \KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu) - \KL(\mu\|\nu^\star)
    = \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\mu(x)\log\bigl(1 + 2\lambda\widetilde S^\star(x)\bigr)\D x
    = \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x) \frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)}u(x)\log u(x)\D x,
\end{equation*}
where $u(x) := 1 + 2\lambda\widetilde S^\star(x) \Ge 1 - 2\lambda \Ge 0$ since $\lambda \Le 1/2$. The elementary inequality $u\log u \Ge u - 1$ for $u \Ge 0$ then yields
\begin{equation*}
    \KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu) - \KL(\mu\|\nu^\star)
    \Ge 2\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x) \frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} \widetilde S^\star(x)\D x.
\end{equation*}
Applying the rank-correlation argument of Lemma~\ref{lem: rank-correlation} verbatim with $\mu$ replaced by $\tilde\mu$ in the outer measure,
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)}\widetilde S^\star(x)\D x = \frac{1}{2}\!\iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\tilde\mu(y) \biggl|\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu^\star(y)}\biggr|\D x\D y,
\end{equation*}
which gives \eqref{eq: accuracy-bound}.
\end{proof}

The right-hand side of \eqref{eq: accuracy-bound} contrasts the two roles of $\X_\mu$ training. At $\lambda = 0$ the bound is tight at $\nu^\star = \tilde\mu$ and the residual KL is fixed at $\KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu)$; the AIS bias is a hard floor. Switching on the regularizer with $\lambda \Le 1/2$ strictly lowers this floor by an amount controlled by the discrepancy of the importance weights $\mu/\nu^\star$ across the support of $\tilde\mu$: the larger this discrepancy, the more the rank-based feedback pulls $\nu^\star$ toward $\mu$. The $\X_\mu$ regularizer is therefore not merely a numerical stabilizer for the forward KL flow: it directly improves the asymptotic accuracy of the trained density when the target samples are biased, which is the practically relevant regime.

The threshold $\lambda \Le 1/2$ is a worst-case condition enforcing positivity of $\nu^\star$ uniformly in $x$; in practice the accuracy improvement persists well beyond it. The practical takeaway is that even without the wide-coverage regularizer $\X_{\hat\mu}$, adding $\X_\mu$ alone to the forward KL loss already reduces the bias floor inherited from inaccurate AIS samples.

\section{Flow training with X functionals}
\label{sec: training}

This section turns the abstract loss of the introduction into a concrete training algorithm for the flow map $G$. Let $U_0$ and $U$ be the source and target potentials on $\mathbb R^d$, so that $\mu_0 \propto \exp(-U_0)$ is the easily samplable reference and $\mu \propto \exp(-U)$ is the target. We parameterize $G:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^d$ so that $G_{\#}\mu \approx \mu_0$ -- mapping target to source, so that no inversion is required during training -- and write $\nu := G^{-1}_{\#}\mu_0$ for the induced approximation to $\mu$. We first assemble the training loss and read off the two sampling ingredients it requires (Section~\ref{subsec: total-loss}), then supply them in turn: annealed importance sampling of $\mu$ (Section~\ref{subsec: ais}), the quench and temper construction of the wide-coverage measure $\hat\mu$ (Section~\ref{subsec: QT}), and finally the ESS and coverage diagnostics used to evaluate the trained flow (Section~\ref{subsec: eval}).

\subsection{The cross-regularized training loss}
\label{subsec: total-loss}

By the change-of-variables formula the pullback density of $\mu_0$ under $G$ is $\nu(y) = \mu_0(G(y))|\det J_G(y)|$, so the log-ratio between the target and the model along the flow is the single scalar field
\begin{equation}
    z(y)  :=  \log\frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)}  =  U_0(G(y)) - U(y) - \log|\det J_G(y)| + \mathrm{const}, \qquad y \in \mathbb R^d.
    \label{eq: logratio-z}
\end{equation}
Both ingredients of the loss are elementary functionals of $z$. The forward KL is its mean under the target, $\KL(\mu\|\nu) = \mathbb E_{y\sim\mu}[z(y)] + \mathrm{const}$ (cf.\ \eqref{eq: forward KL}), and each cross-regularizer $\X_\omega$ \eqref{eq: X} is the mean absolute pairwise variation of $z$ under a non-negative weight $\omega$, $\X_\omega(\mu\|\nu) = \mathbb E_{y, y'\sim\omega}\bigl|z(y) - z(y')\bigr|$. Combining the shape term $\omega = \mu$ with the balanced mixture $\omega = \alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu$ that handles mode discovery and leakage suppression, the total training loss in terms of the flow map $G$ is
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}[G]
    &= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \mu}\bigl[z(y)\bigr]}_{\KL(\mu\|\nu)}
     + \lambda \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \mu}\bigl[|z(y) - z(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)}
     + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu}\bigl[|z(y) - z(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_{\alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu}(\mu\|\nu)} .
    \label{eq: total-loss-G}
\end{align}
We stress the two pushforward measures appearing in \eqref{eq: total-loss-G}. The model distribution $\nu = G^{-1}_{\#}\mu_0$, with density $\nu(y) = \mu_0(G(y))|\det J_G(y)|$, is the flow's live approximation to the target $\mu$ and depends on the trainable parameters of $G$. The barred measure $\bar\nu$ is the \emph{same} pushforward frozen at the current parameters -- a stop-gradient copy of $\nu$ -- which enters the mixture term only as a sampling weight and is never differentiated through.

All three terms are computed in a single forward pass: evaluate $\mathbf z = (z(y_1),\dots,z(y_B))$ once on a batch, take $\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf z)$ for the forward KL, and $\mathrm{mean}(|\mathbf z - \mathbf z[\sigma]|)$ for a random permutation $\sigma$ of the batch for each X term. Backpropagation passes through $\mathbf z$ exactly once, identical to a vanilla forward KL step, so the X functionals add essentially no extra cost.

The loss draws on three measures, of which only two must be built. The $\bar\nu$ batch is free: it is the raw pushforward $\bar y_i = G^{-1}(x_i)$ of source samples $x_i \sim \mu_0$, taken under \texttt{torch.no\_grad}; this detached sampler keeps the loss numerically stable on sharp targets, where differentiating through the sampling chain (as a true reverse-KL gradient does) would explode. The other two require construction: samples of the target $\mu$ for the forward KL and $\X_\mu$ terms, supplied by annealed importance sampling (Section~\ref{subsec: ais}), and samples of the wide-coverage measure $\hat\mu$ for the mixture term, supplied by quench and temper (Section~\ref{subsec: QT}).

Although $(\lambda, \alpha, \beta)$ are three free parameters in principle, the X functional is by construction on the same scale as the forward KL, so any choice of order $1$ works in practice. Two regimes are worth highlighting. The simple regime sets $\lambda = 1$ and $\alpha = \beta = 0$, adding only $\X_\mu$ on top of the forward KL; this term reuses the AIS samples and autograd graph of the forward KL, costing only an extra permutation and elementwise difference per batch, and it suffices whenever the source $\mu_0$ already overlaps every mode of $\mu$. The mode-discovery regime sets $\lambda = 1$ and $\alpha = \beta = 1/2$, turning on the mixture term with a balanced half-half draw from $\hat\mu$ and $\bar\nu$: the $\hat\mu$ half pulls $\nu$ toward QT-discovered modes that AIS alone cannot reach, while the $\bar\nu$ half restrains the mass the flow places outside the support of $\mu$. We recommend the mode-discovery regime as the default whenever $\mu_0$ may not cover all modes of $\mu$, and use it throughout the experiments; re-tuning $(\lambda, \alpha, \beta)$ away from these defaults did not improve results on any of our benchmarks.

\subsection{Sampling $\mu$ by annealed importance sampling}
\label{subsec: ais}

Evaluating the $\mu$-expectations in \eqref{eq: total-loss-G} requires samples from $\mu$, which are not directly available. The natural surrogate draws $y_1,\dots,y_B$ from the pushforward $\nu$ and reweights by
\begin{equation}
    w(y) = \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)} = \exp\bigl(z(y)\bigr) = \exp\Big( U_0(G(y)) - U(y) - \log|\det J_G(y)| \Big), \qquad y \in \mathbb R^d,
    \label{eq: weight}
\end{equation}
followed by a resampling step and a short Langevin simulation to diffuse the duplicates. In high dimensions, however, $\mu$ and $\nu$ typically differ greatly, so the weights $w(y_i)$ collapse onto a few outliers and the resulting samples are of very low quality. Annealed importance sampling (AIS) bridges this gap by inserting $M+1$ intermediate distributions
\begin{equation}
    \pi_k = \nu^{1-\frac{k}{M}} (\mu)^{\frac{k}{M}} = \nu \cdot w(y)^{\frac{k}{M}}, \qquad k = 0,1,\dots,M,
    \label{eq: pi-k}
\end{equation}
so that $\pi_0 = \nu$ and $\pi_M = \mu$. Each rung of the ladder reweights by only $w(y)^{\frac{1}{M}}$, keeping the weight ratio close to $1$ and avoiding the degeneracy of direct importance sampling:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\setstretch{1.2}
\caption{Annealed importance sampling from $\nu$ to $\mu$}
\KwInput{samples $y_1,\dots,y_B$ from $\nu$, ladder length $M$}
\KwOutput{samples $y_1,\dots,y_B$ approximately distributed as $\mu$}

\For{$k = 1,\dots,M$}{
    Compute the per-step weights $w(y_i)^{\frac{1}{M}}$ on $y_1,\dots,y_B$\;
    Resample $y_1',\dots,y_B'$ from the weighted empirical distribution\;
    \tcp{The resampled set has duplicates; Langevin diffuses them}
    Apply a short Langevin simulation to $y_1',\dots,y_B'$, returning fresh samples $y_1,\dots,y_B \sim \pi_k$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{remark}[Choosing the ladder length $M$]
\label{rem: ladder-length}
For simple targets close to the source, $M = 1$ already suffices; in high dimensions or under large deformations, $M = d$ is a good default. For instance, annealing $\mu_0 \propto e^{-|x|^2}$ to $\mu \propto e^{-2|x|^2}$ on $\mathbb R^d$, a ladder of $M = d$ steps keeps the weighted empirical distribution at a finite ESS that does not degrade with $d$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Constructing $\hat\mu$ by quench and temper}
\label{subsec: QT}

The mixture term needs samples from the wide-coverage measure $\hat\mu$. These are prepared once, before training, as a fixed pool of $P$ points $x_1,\dots,x_P$ distributed as $\hat\mu$; at each training step a mini-batch is drawn from the pool and refreshed by a short Langevin chain before entering the $\X_\omega$ computation of Section~\ref{subsec: total-loss} with $\mu$ replaced by $\hat\mu$. The pool is produced by quench and temper (QT), which proceeds in three stages: diffusion (melt), optimization (quench), and rejuvenation (temper).

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\label{alg: QT}
\setstretch{1.2}
\caption{QT: initiative mode discovery}
\KwInput{samples $x_1,\dots,x_P$ from $\mu_0$}
\KwOutput{samples $x_1,\dots,x_P$ forming $\hat\mu$}
\tcp{Diffusion (melt): scatter the samples to a wider region of $\mathbb R^d$}
Choose a large $\sigma > 0$ and update $x_i \leftarrow x_i + \sigma\xi_i$ with $\xi_i \sim \mathcal N(0, I_d)$ i.i.d.\;
\tcp{Optimization (quench): drive each sample toward a mode center of $\mu$}
Run LBFGS on $U$ for a fixed number of steps to update $x_1,\dots,x_P$\;
\tcp{Rejuvenation (temper): spread the samples around each mode}
Run a short Langevin simulation targeting $\mu$ on $x_1,\dots,x_P$\;
\end{algorithm}
\mbox{}\\
The output samples concentrate near the mode centers of $\mu$, and the initial Brownian diffusion ensures that even modes far from the support of the input are reachable. The $\X_{\hat\mu}$ regularizer therefore penalizes any mode of $\mu$ on which the current pushforward $\nu$ places too little mass, driving the flow to discover modes that AIS alone could never reach.

A large diffusion $\sigma$ biases the QT output toward modes that lie far from the bulk of $\mu_0$. This is desirable rather than problematic: the modes the current pushforward fails to cover are precisely those far from $\mu_0$, and the bias is also harmless because the minimizer of $\X_{\hat\mu}$ is insensitive to the precise shape of $\hat\mu$.

\begin{remark}[QT failure mode: heavy-tailed manifolds]
\label{rem: QT-tail}
On heavy-tailed manifold targets the thin outer tail consists of narrow-variance components whose basins are too small for the LBFGS quench to land in: the diffusion step rarely scatters particles close enough, and any near miss is pulled back into the wide-basin body. The resulting $\hat\mu$ traces the body but misses the tail, and $\X_{\hat\mu}$ inherits the blind spot.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Importance sampling at evaluation: ESS and coverage}
\label{subsec: eval}

At evaluation time the trained flow $G$ supplies the model distribution $\nu = G^{-1}_{\#}\mu_0$ as an approximation to $\mu$, together with the exact tractable density $\nu(y) = \mu_0(G(y))|\det J_G(y)|$ that any normalizing flow makes available. Because the density is exact, the importance weight $w(y) = \mu(y)/\nu(y) = \exp(z(y))$ of \eqref{eq: weight} can be evaluated for free on any pushforward sample and used at test time to correct the residual bias of $\nu$ as an approximation to $\mu$. Drawing $\mathbf y = (y_1,\dots,y_N)$ with $y_i = G^{-1}(x_i)$ and $x_i\sim\mu_0$ i.i.d., the self-normalized importance-sampling estimator
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\mathbb E_\mu[f]}  =  \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N w(y_i) f(y_i)}{\sum_{i=1}^N w(y_i)}
    \label{eq: snis}
\end{equation}
is a consistent estimator of $\mathbb E_\mu[f]$ for any $\mu$-integrable test function $f$, regardless of how far $\nu$ is from $\mu$: the flow does not need to be exact for the estimate to converge as $N\to\infty$. To obtain approximate samples from $\mu$ rather than just expectations one resamples $\mathbf y$ by the weights and refreshes with a short Langevin chain targeting $\mu$, exactly the surrogate that drove the AIS step in Section~\ref{subsec: ais} but now applied with the trained flow held fixed. The training bias the X functionals were designed to reduce is, at this stage, traded for sampling cost: the better the flow, the smaller the residual reweighting that \eqref{eq: snis} has to do, and the lower its variance.

\begin{remark}[Coarse training, fine importance sampling]
\label{rem: coarse-fine-IS}
If the target has a natural coarse representation, the flow can be trained on the cheap low-dimensional coarse version and the resulting proposal reweighted via \eqref{eq: snis} against the fine high-dimensional target at evaluation -- the high-dimensional target then never enters a gradient step. This separation is the way \citet{lin2026jeffreys} applies the framework to path integral Monte Carlo.
\end{remark}

We assess the quality of this pipeline through two complementary metrics: the effective sample size (ESS), which measures how uniform the importance weights are on the support reached by the flow, and the coverage, which checks whether every mode of $\mu$ has been reached at all.

The first metric is the effective sample size. Let $\mathbf y = (y_1,\dots,y_N) \in \mathbb R^{N\times d}$ be i.i.d.\ samples from $\nu$, with importance weights $w(y_i)$ given by \eqref{eq: weight}. The ESS is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \ESS(\mathbf y) = \frac{\big(\sum_{i=1}^N w(y_i)\big)^2}{N \sum_{i=1}^N w(y_i)^2} \in [0,1],
\end{equation}
where the upper bound $1$ follows from the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality and is attained when all weights are equal. When $G$ is perfectly trained the weights are constant and the ESS reaches $1$. As emphasized in the introduction, however, this conclusion is only one-way: a high ESS certifies that the weights are uniform \emph{on the support of $\nu$}, but modes of $\mu$ that the pushforward never reaches contribute nothing to the sum and so cannot lower the ESS, which gives rise to the \emph{fake ESS} pitfall.

To detect such missing modes, we pair the ESS with the coverage metric of \citet{naeem2020reliable}. Let $\mathbf x = (x_1,\dots,x_P) \in \mathbb R^{P\times d}$ be the samples of $\hat\mu$ produced by the quench and temper algorithm (Algorithm~\ref{alg: QT}). For each $1 \Le i \Le P$, the $k$-nearest-neighbor radius of $x_i$ is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{NND}_k(x_i;\mathbf x) = \inf\Big\{ r \Ge 0 :
    \# \big\{1\Le j \Le P: 0 < |x_i - x_j| < r\big\} \Ge k
    \Big\},
\end{equation}
i.e., the distance from $x_i$ to its $k$-th nearest neighbor in $\mathbf x$. The coverage of $\mathbf x$ by the pushforward samples $\mathbf y$ is then
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Coverage}_k(\mathbf y;\mathbf x) =
    \frac{1}{P} \sum_{i=1}^P \mathbf 1\Bigl\{
        \exists  1\Le j\Le N : |y_j - x_i| \Le \mathrm{NND}_k(x_i;\mathbf x)
    \Bigr\},
\end{equation}
i.e., the fraction of points in $\mathbf x$ whose $k$-nearest-neighbor ball contains at least one pushforward sample. A coverage close to $1$ certifies that every region populated by $\hat\mu$ has been reached by the flow, while a coverage substantially below $1$ exposes precisely the missing modes that the ESS alone cannot see.

\section{Numerical experiments}

\subsection{2D benchmark distributions}
\label{subsec: 2d-benchmark}

We test the training scheme on five 2D targets, each chosen to isolate a different failure mode of the forward KL rather than to match the GMM/DW community suite \citep{akhound2024iterated,woo2024iefm}: Threewell (three asymmetric wells of a quartic-plus-sinusoid potential, with the narrow source far from all three), Himmelblau (four isolated modes), Annulus (a thin one-dimensional ring of minima together with an isolated central minimum), Python (a thin 1D-manifold version of the Python logo built from $1024$ stroke Gaussians traced along the skeleton of the two interlocking snake bodies, plus two larger eye Gaussians), and Chessboard (a Gaussian mixture on $26$ of the $32$ black squares of an $8 \times 8$ grid, with six cells removed to break the chessboard symmetry). The potentials are listed in Table~\ref{tab: 2d-benchmark}.

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
        \toprule
        name & potential $U(x_1, x_2)$ & minima \\
        \midrule
        Threewell  & $6\bigl[(x_1^2 - 1)^2 + (x_2^2 - 1)^2 + \sin(x_1 + 2 x_2)\bigr]$ & three asymmetric wells \\
        Himmelblau & $\bigl(x_1^2 + x_2 - 11\bigr)^2 + \bigl(x_1 + x_2^2 - 7\bigr)^2$ & four isolated points \\
        Annulus    & $20 \bigl(r^6 - 8 r^4 + 16 r^2 + 1\bigr)^{1/3}$, $r^2 = x_1^2 + x_2^2$ & $r = 0$ and ring at $r = 2$ \\
        Python     & Python-like Gaussian mixture & $1024$ stroke centers $+ 2$ eye centers \\
        Chessboard & Chessboard-like Gaussian mixture & $26$ centers \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Four 2D benchmark targets.}
    \label{tab: 2d-benchmark}
\end{table}

For all five targets the source $\mu_0$ is an isotropic Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$ with $\sigma$ between $0.25$ and $2.0$; on Threewell it is deliberately narrow and centered at the origin, far from all three wells, so the initial pushforward covers none of them. The flow is a neural spline flow with $6$ coupling transforms, $32$ bins, and hidden widths $(128, 128)$, on a square box matched to each target. Training uses Adam (learning rate $10^{-3}$ to $5\times 10^{-3}$) for $1000$ steps ($2000$ on Python, whose narrow tubular support takes longer to saturate), with batch size $500$, enlarged to $1000$--$2000$ on the multi-mode Threewell and Chessboard so a single batch can cover all modes. At each step the AIS surrogate is one importance-weighted resampling pass against the current pushforward followed by $50$ Langevin steps. The loss coefficients are the preferred default of Section~\ref{subsec: total-loss}, $\lambda = 1$ and $\alpha = \beta = 1/2$, and the wide-coverage measure $\hat\mu$ is built once before training by QT (Algorithm~\ref{alg: QT}) and recycled with one Langevin rejuvenation per step.

For each target we compare four losses obtained by progressively adding one X regularizer at a time: the bare forward KL \eqref{eq: forward KL}; $\KL{+}\X_\mu$ with $\lambda = 1$; $\KL{+}\X_\mu{+}\X_{\hat\mu}$ with $\alpha = 1$, $\beta = 0$; and the preferred mixture $\KL{+}\X_\mu{+}\X_{(\hat\mu+\bar\nu)/2}$ with $\alpha = \beta = 1/2$.

\paragraph{Threewell.} The quartic-plus-sinusoid potential has three asymmetric wells, and the narrow source $\mu_0$ (standard deviation $0.25$) sits at the origin, far from all of them, so the initial pushforward covers none. Forward KL and $\KL{+}\X_\mu$ lock onto only two of the three wells (coverage $\approx 0.75$ in both cases) and never discover the third, yet their ESS remains close to one ($0.99$ and $1.00$): a fake-ESS instance in which the flow omits an entire mode while reporting near-uniform weights on the support it does reach. Adding $\X_{\hat\mu}$ pulls mass onto the QT-discovered third well and lifts coverage to $\approx 1.00$, and the mixture variant $\X_{(\hat\mu+\bar\nu)/2}$ attains the same full coverage at the highest full-coverage ESS ($0.99$) with the cleanest wells of the four (Figure~\ref{fig: threewell}). On Threewell, $\X_{\hat\mu}$ and the mixture extension are the only two losses that recover the missed third well.

\paragraph{Himmelblau.} The source places nearly all its mass near the central saddle, so the initial pushforward is far from any of the four Himmelblau modes. Forward KL and $\KL{+}\X_\mu$ both converge to a flow that covers only two of the four modes (coverage $\approx 0.53$ and $\approx 0.48$), yet the ESS gives no warning: $\KL{+}\X_\mu$ posts the highest nominal ESS of the four ($0.97$) while missing half the modes, a fake-ESS instance. Adding $\X_{\hat\mu}$ steers mass toward the QT-discovered modes and recovers coverage $1.00$ (Figure~\ref{fig: himmelblau}); the mixture variant $\X_{(\hat\mu+\bar\nu)/2}$ also reaches coverage $1.00$, and the two full-coverage losses tie at the best full-coverage ESS ($0.95$), with the mixture pushforward the visibly cleaner of the two. This case confirms the role of $\X_{\hat\mu}$ (or its mixture extension) as the safeguard one needs precisely when $\mu_0$ does not by itself cover all modes of $\mu$, the regime in which the forward KL is silent.

On the shape, manifold, and leakage targets (Annulus, Python, Chessboard) coverage is already complete and ESS becomes the discriminator: $\X_\mu$ helps on Annulus and Python but is flat on Chessboard, whereas the mixture improves on bare forward KL by $+0.31$, $+0.22$, and $+0.07$ and attains the best ESS on all three. It absorbs the mode-discovery role of $\X_{\hat\mu}$ while suppressing the off-support leakage that $\X_{\hat\mu}$ alone can introduce when the target has holes interleaved with its modes.

\subsection{Bayesian inverse problem on a Fourier function space}
\label{subsec: fourier}

We turn next to a Bayesian benchmark in which the four losses are compared on the posterior of a finite set of Fourier coefficients of an unknown scalar field. The example is deliberately low-dimensional ($d = 9$), so that the equilibrium structure can be enumerated and verified by hand, and it is constructed so that the fake-ESS pitfall surfaces in a genuine Bayesian inverse problem rather than a hand-built mixture.

Let $u(x;\theta) = \sum_{m=1}^{9} \theta_m \varphi_m(x)$ be a real scalar field on the 2-torus $[0,1]^2$, expanded in the $d = 9$ Fourier modes with $|k|^2 \Le 2$ (half-plane convention), $\varphi_m$ either a cosine or sine of $2\pi k_m\!\cdot\! x$. Split the nine coefficients $\theta\in\mathbb R^9$ contiguously into two groups of sizes 5 and 4, and let $v_g(x;\theta)$ denote the partial field summed over the modes in group $g$. The source is the centered Gaussian smoothness prior $\mu_0 = \mathcal N(0,\Sigma_0)$ with diagonal covariance $(\Sigma_0)_{mm} = 2/(1+|k_m|^2)$.

Observations are produced once from a fixed $\theta^\star$ drawn from the prior (rescaled to amplitude $2.5$), as squared partial fields on a $3\times 3$ spatial grid $\{x_j\}_{j=1}^{9}$ with independent Gaussian noise of variance $4$,
$y_{g,j} = [v_g(x_j;\theta^\star)]^2 + \varepsilon_{g,j}$,
clamped to be non-negative. The target potential is the negative log-posterior, up to an additive constant,
\begin{equation}
    U(\theta)  =  \frac12 \theta^{\top} \Sigma_0^{-1} \theta
     +  \frac{1}{8}\!\sum_{g=1}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{9}\Bigl(\bigl[v_g(x_j;\theta)\bigr]^2 - y_{g,j}\Bigr)^{2}.
    \label{eq: fourier-potential}
\end{equation}

Squaring $v_g$ makes $U$ exactly invariant under each per-group sign flip $\theta\mapsto S_g\theta$ that negates the coordinates of $\theta$ in group $g$; the two commuting sign-flips generate a Klein four-group acting freely on a generic $\theta^\star$, producing exactly $4$ degenerate, well-separated modes (radius $\approx 6.3$, pairwise separation $\approx 5.4$ on our seed; degeneracy $U \equiv 28.66$ verified to machine precision). The centered Gaussian source sits on the common symmetry saddle at $\theta = 0$. The posterior is nontrivial to sample for two reasons that combine: the four modes are far from any point of significant prior mass (each lies several prior-standard-deviations out), and the curved high-$U$ barriers between adjacent sign-flip basins are very rarely crossed by a Langevin chain on $U$ during the short rejuvenation step that the AIS surrogate can afford — so a flow trained by bare forward KL is starved of $\mu$-samples outside whichever basin its pushforward first happens to reach, and collapses there.

The flow is a neural spline flow on $[-12, +12]^9$ with six coupling transforms, sixteen bins, and hidden widths $(128, 128)$. Training uses Adam with learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch size $500$, and $2000$ steps; the AIS surrogate is the one-step ($M=1$) resample-plus-Langevin pass of Section~\ref{subsec: 2d-benchmark}, and $\hat\mu$ is built once before training by quench and temper (Algorithm~\ref{alg: QT}) and recycled with one Langevin rejuvenation per step. Mode coverage is measured by assigning each pushforward sample to the nearest of the four known sign-flip mode centers, with a mode counted as found when it holds at least $5\%$ of the pushforward mass; this is paired with the $k=5$ Naeem coverage against the QT pool.

Table~\ref{tab: fourier-result} reports the four-method comparison. Bare forward KL attains a fresh-pool ESS of $0.926$ while concentrating its entire pushforward on a single one of the four equal-mass modes; adding $\X_\mu$ does not improve coverage and reduces the ESS to $0.859$, with the pushforward landing in a different single basin. The QT-driven $\X_{\hat\mu}$ and the mixture reach $4/4$ coverage with per-mode occupancies within $\pm 1\%$ of the uniform $1/4$ share and $k=5$ Naeem coverage close to $1$, at fresh-pool ESS $\approx 0.89$.

\subsection{High-dimensional product multi-well}
\label{subsec: highd}

To test the framework beyond two dimensions we construct a family of product multi-well targets in dimension $d = 2^k$, playing here the role that double-well and Lennard-Jones systems play in the diffusion-sampler literature \citep{akhound2024iterated,woo2024iefm}, but with an analytically known mode structure and a diagonal optimal transport map so that training failures are unambiguously training failures and not capacity failures of the flow,
\begin{equation}
    U(x) = \frac12 \sum_{i=1}^{2^k} x_i^2 + 12 \sum_{i=1}^{k} \exp(-x_i^2),
    \label{eq: highd-potential}
\end{equation}
a double well in each of the first $k$ coordinates (minima at $\pm\sqrt{\ln 24} \approx \pm 1.78$, separated by a barrier of height $\approx 9.9$) and a standard Gaussian in the remaining $2^k - k$. The source is the standard Gaussian $\mu_0 = \mathcal N(0, I_d)$, whose potential is the quadratic part of $U$. Because $U$ is separable, the target factorizes into $k$ identical double wells and $2^k - k$ Gaussians, so it has exactly $2^k = d$ modes -- the number of modes equals the dimension -- and the optimal transport map is diagonal. The source sits exactly on the barrier of every double well, so the untrained pushforward covers none of the $2^k$ mode combinations. We sweep $k = 1, \dots, 7$, i.e.\ $d = 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128$.

The flow is a neural spline flow on $[-4, 4]^d$ with $6$ coupling transforms, $16$ bins, and hidden widths $(128, 128)$ for $d \Le 16$ and $(256, 256)$ for $d \Ge 32$. Training uses Adam at learning rate $10^{-3}$ with batch size $200$ -- forward KL is highly sensitive to the batch size, so a small batch sharpens the contrast between the losses -- drawn from a source pool of $10^4 \cdot 2^k$ samples; the number of steps decreases with $d$ from $3000$ ($d \Le 4$) to $1200$ ($d \Ge 64$). The $\mu$-surrogate is the same one-step importance-resampling pass followed by $50$ Langevin steps used in the 2D experiments, and $\hat\mu$ is built once by QT from $100 \cdot 2^k$ samples. The four losses are exactly those of Section~\ref{subsec: 2d-benchmark}, with $\lambda = 1$ and $\alpha = \beta = 1/2$.

Table~\ref{tab: highd-ess} reports the final ESS of each loss across the seven dimensions. Unlike the 2D mode-discovery targets, here the flow reaches all $2^k$ modes for every loss -- each mode is filled to within a few percent of its uniform $2^{-k}$ share, and the $k = 5$ coverage against $\hat\mu$ stays $\Ge 0.997$ throughout -- so this is the calibration regime of Annulus, Python, and Chessboard rather than the fake-ESS regime, and ESS is the sole discriminator. Two trends are clear. First, the bare forward KL degrades monotonically with dimension, from $0.99$ at $d = 2$ to $0.55$ at $d = 128$, as the log-weight variance accumulates one double well at a time. Second, the X regularizers counteract this and the gap widens with dimension: negligible at $d = 2$, it reaches $+0.18$--$0.21$ at $d = 64$ and $128$. The mixture $\X_{(\hat\mu+\bar\nu)/2}$ attains the best (or tied-best) ESS at every $d \Ge 4$, and at the highest dimensions the wide-coverage $\X_{\hat\mu}$ and the mixture pull clearly ahead of $\X_\mu$ alone. Figure~\ref{fig: highd-ess} shows the ESS trajectories at $d = 128$: bare forward KL settles lowest and stays the noisiest, while the X-augmented variants climb higher and the mixture is both the highest and the steadiest -- the same ordering observed on Chessboard (Figure~\ref{fig: chessboard}), now in $128$ dimensions.
