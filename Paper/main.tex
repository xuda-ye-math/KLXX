\begin{abstract}
Normalizing flow Boltzmann generators retain a tractable proposal density, but training with forward KL depends on target samples that may be biased or omit modes. A flow can then miss target mass while its observed importance weights give a high effective sample size. We introduce log-ratio variation, a pairwise regularizer of the exact target-to-model log-density ratio, and use it to define KLXX. Its three weighting measures provide signals for target fitting, candidate mode coverage, and leakage control without changing the exact-target minimizer. We derive an exponential Fisher--Rao bound and a fixed-surrogate error bound for forward KL with the target-weighted term, then place KLXX in an adaptive-staging Boltzmann generator with exact importance reweighting and sharpening of the molecular potential. In the reported model and molecular tests, KLXX improves coverage-aware or stagewise diagnostics, and selected molecular observables agree with independent OpenMM simulations. Log-ratio variation thus supplies information that a sampled forward KL objective can omit.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec: intro}

Drawing samples from a target distribution $\mu \propto \exp(-U)$ on $\mathbb R^d$ is a recurring problem in molecular simulation, Bayesian inference, and statistical physics \citep{frenkel2002understanding,robert2004monte,landau2014guide}. When $\mu$ is high-dimensional and multimodal, its mass splits among basins of $U$ separated by barriers. Local sampling kernels, including Metropolis--Hastings \citep{metropolis1953equation,hastings1970monte}, Langevin dynamics \citep{langevin1908theorie,leimkuhler2015molecular}, Nos{\'e}--Hoover dynamics \citep{nose1984unified,hoover1985canonical}, and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo \citep{duane1987hybrid}, evolve from the current configuration and can therefore remain confined to one basin over accessible simulation times. When this occurs, finite-time estimates are biased toward the visited basin, with no direct account of the mass not reached.

Tempering and particle methods address this problem by connecting a tractable distribution to $\mu$ through intermediate distributions. Parallel tempering (PT) and replica exchange run copies of the system at several temperatures and exchange neighboring configurations, so a basin reached at high temperature can enter the low-temperature ensemble \citep{swendsen1986replica,geyer1991markov,hukushima1996exchange}. Annealed importance sampling (AIS) moves along a bridge of intermediate distributions while accumulating importance weights \citep{neal2001annealed}. Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) uses a similar bridge but resamples and rejuvenates a weighted particle set at each step \citep{doucet2001introduction,delmoral2006sequential}. For a specified chemical process, umbrella sampling biases selected reaction coordinates to cross relevant barriers and reweights the biased samples to recover a free-energy profile \citep{torrie1977umbrella,kastner2011umbrella}. Nested sampling instead rewrites the normalizing integral as a one-dimensional quadrature over a likelihood threshold \citep{skilling2006nested}.

Recent work improves PT through three distinct mechanisms. Nonreversible swap schedules and tuned temperature grids accelerate round trips \citep{syed2022nonreversible}; variational-reference methods adapt the reference endpoint and its connecting path \citep{surjanovic2022variational}; and neural transports improve communication between adjacent distributions with limited overlap \citep{zhang2025accelerated}. All three strengthen communication between neighboring distributions, but none removes the dependence on local exploration: a basin not reached by any chain cannot be passed to the target ensemble \citep{woodard2009conditions,chehab2024provable}. Improving this communication therefore does not by itself resolve missing-mode coverage. This gap motivates a learned proposal with a tractable density whose training draws information from more than one sampling measure.

Boltzmann generators provide a complementary route through a learned transport map \citep{noe2019boltzmann}. Let $\mu_0\propto\exp(-U_0)$ be an easily sampled reference distribution, usually Gaussian, and let $G:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^d$ be an invertible normalizing flow \citep{rezende2015variational,papamakarios2017masked,kingma2018glow}. We use the target-to-source convention $G_{\#}\mu\approx\mu_0$. The inverse map then sends independent source draws toward the target, and their pushforward distribution is $\nu:=(G^{-1})_{\#}\mu_0$. Its density function is
\begin{equation}
    \nu(y)=\mu_0(G(y))\left|\det J_G(y)\right|,
    \label{eq: pushforward-density}
\end{equation}
where $J_G(y)$ is the Jacobian matrix of $G$ at $y$. An importance weight proportional to $\mu/\nu$ is therefore available for every pushforward sample without fitting another density or tracing a stochastic path. These weights enable importance-reweighted estimates and define the effective sample size (ESS), a diagnostic of density agreement on the support reached by $\nu$ \citep{liu2001monte,doucet2001introduction,martino2017rethinking}. The remaining challenge is that a high ESS certifies agreement only on the reached support: a target mode absent from the pushforward samples contributes no observed weight.

The choice of training objective determines how this challenge enters training. The original Boltzmann generator \citep{noe2019boltzmann} and its variational-inference predecessor \citep{rezende2015variational} minimize the reverse Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence
\begin{equation}
    \text{(reverse)}\quad \KL(\nu\|\mu) = \int_{\mathbb R^d} \nu(x) \log \frac{\nu(x)}{\mu(x)} \D x,
    \label{eq: reverse KL}
\end{equation}
which needs samples only from $\nu$ and evaluations of $U$. Its empirical gradient is therefore accessible, but the divergence is mode-seeking \citep{wainwright2008graphical}: a mode absent from $\nu$ contributes no samples and hence no gradient. A flow can then collapse onto a subset of the target modes \citep{wu2020stochastic,felardos2023designing}. Existing approaches interleave MCMC kernels with deterministic layers, encode target symmetries in the architecture, reduce gradient variance near the optimum, or anneal from a smoother potential \citep{wu2020stochastic,kohler2020equivariant,vaitl2022gradients,schopmans2025temperature}. These changes can improve exploration, but their success still depends on whether the resulting sampler reaches each relevant mode.

The opposing objective minimizes the forward KL,
\begin{equation}
    \text{(forward)}\quad \KL(\mu\|\nu) = \int_{\mathbb R^d} \mu(x) \log \frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} \D x,
    \label{eq: forward KL}
\end{equation}
which is mass-covering for exact distributions: it diverges if $\nu$ vanishes where $\mu$ has positive mass \citep{wainwright2008graphical}. Its empirical form, however, needs samples from $\mu$, the distribution being learned. Existing work resolves this circularity in several ways. Maximum likelihood uses configurations from a reference thermodynamic state \citep{wirnsberger2020targeted}; adaptive flow samplers train on a running Markov chain and use the flow as a global proposal \citep{gabrie2022adaptive}; molecular flows can fit a molecular-dynamics trajectory \citep{klein2023equivariant}; and transferable architectures amortize coverage across related molecules \citep{klein2024transferable}. Persistent chains and coupled-target regression provide other approximations to the missing target expectation \citep{naesseth2020markovian,rehman2026regflow}. Because every empirical loss can see only the regions represented by its sample source, this exact-distribution property does not prevent empirical mode omission. This tension motivates an objective that retains forward KL while obtaining training signals beyond one target surrogate.

The sampled forward KL leaves three distinct gaps. First, a mode absent from its target surrogate contributes no training points. If the flow also misses that mode, the observed weights can be nearly uniform on the reached support, so the ESS can be close to one even though target mass is absent; we call this a misleading or \emph{fake ESS} \citep{noe2019boltzmann,midgley2022flow}. Second, a finite or biased target surrogate distorts the sampled objective, and the fitted flow can inherit that error. Third, target samples give little information where the proposal places excess mass, allowing leakage between modes or into target-poor regions. Improving one of these failures does not by itself resolve the other two.

To address these gaps, we propose KLXX, a forward KL loss augmented by log-ratio variation; the graphical abstract summarizes it, and Equation~\eqref{eq: KLXX-G-intro} gives its full default form. KLXX preserves the tractable target-to-proposal density ratio and exact-target minimizer while separating target fitting, candidate-mode coverage, and proposal-support control. Annealing supplies the target surrogate and target-weighted variation. The new third term mixes quench and temper (QT) and pushforward samples: QT contributes candidate modes missing from the surrogate, while pushforward samples expose proposal support and leakage. QT guides coverage rather than estimating physical mode weights or guaranteeing complete discovery.

The closest objective-level comparison is Flow Annealed Importance Sampling Bootstrap (FAB), which couples AIS and flow training but replaces the forward KL by the $\alpha$-divergence ($\alpha=2$) \citep{midgley2022flow}. Its integrand is proportional to $\mu^2/\nu$, and its exact minimizer also minimizes importance-weight variance. FAB targets $\mu^2/\nu$ with AIS and reuses those samples through a prioritized replay buffer, adding buffer maintenance. Related methods insert flows into SMC, anneal toward low-temperature targets, add an inference-time SMC correction, or connect disconnected regions \citep{arbel2021annealed,matthews2022craft,schopmans2025temperature,tan2025sequential,vonklitzing2026constrained}. KLXX instead retains the forward KL and adds nonnegative log-ratio variations. Its extra cost is QT and the variation evaluations, including the additional mixture batch; it uses no replay buffer. FAB's $2$-divergence gives a stronger tail penalty and may reduce the need for separate coverage construction, whereas KLXX obtains coverage information from QT. Remark~\ref{rem: fab} compares the sampling mechanisms after they are defined.

Centered log-ratio penalties appear in log-variance objectives for learned diffusion samplers \citep{richter2024improved} and in LDR for data-based Boltzmann generator objectives \citep{schopmans2026ldr}; such centered log-ratio regularization in Boltzmann generator training is therefore not new. Developed independently of LDR, our target-weighted term instead pairs the forward KL integrand at two independent target points, yielding the double integral below. The new KLXX component is the variation term weighted by a mixture of QT and pushforward samples.

A separate line trains a stochastic process rather than a bijection. Controlled stochastic differential equations, denoising diffusions, and flow-matching models learn a drift or velocity that transports the reference toward $\mu$ \citep{zhang2022path,vargas2023denoising,berner2024optimal,nusken2021solving,richter2024improved}. Energy-only variants such as iterated denoising energy matching (iDEM) alternate a sampling loop with a score- or velocity-fitting loop and a replay buffer \citep{akhound2024iterated,woo2024iefm}. Learned nonequilibrium transport augments AIS with a trainable drift, and later work extends this approach to larger many-body systems \citep{albergo2024nets,havens2025adjoint,akhoundsadegh2025progressive}.

These stochastic transports can be more expressive than the invertible architectures used here. Their terminal density, however, often requires per-sample ODE integration \citep{he2025notrick}. We retain a bijection because the exact proposal density, importance ratio, ESS, and reweighting are central to the objective and final estimator. We now turn from the principle-level comparison to the mathematical definition of KLXX. Define the tractable log-density ratio $z(x):=\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)}$. For a nonnegative density $\omega$ on $\mathbb R^d$, define the \emph{log-ratio variation}
\begin{equation}
    \X_\omega(\mu\|\nu) = \iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d} \omega(x)\,\omega(y)
    \biggl|\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} - \log\frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)}\biggr| \D x\D y,
    \label{eq: X}
\end{equation}
which measures how far $z$ is from a constant on the region weighted by $\omega$. For $\omega=\mu$, $\X_\mu$ averages $|z(x)-z(y)|$ over independent target points, whereas LDR-1 uses the centered penalty $\mathbb E_\mu|z-\mathbb E_\mu z|$. Jensen's inequality and the triangle inequality give $\mathbb E_\mu|z-\mathbb E_\mu z|\Le\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)\Le2\mathbb E_\mu|z-\mathbb E_\mu z|$. Thus, at the same coefficient, $\KL+\X_\mu$ and LDR-1 are comparable up to a factor of two. This suggests similar training performance, although the objectives are not equivalent. KLXX is also not equivalent to LDR-2: its target-weighted variation is pairwise $L^1$, and its new mixture term uses QT and pushforward samples. Since $\X_\omega$ is nonnegative and vanishes at $\nu=\mu$, adding it to the exact forward KL preserves the target as a minimizer.

The target distribution $\mu$ supplies samples for both the forward KL and $\X_\mu$. The wide-coverage distribution $\hat\mu$ supplies candidate-mode samples, while $\bar\nu$ is the pushforward distribution of the current proposal; samples from $\bar\nu$ are held fixed during differentiation. The combined loss is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}[\nu] = \KL(\mu\|\nu) + \lambda\, \X_\mu(\mu\|\nu) + \X_{\alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu}(\mu\|\nu),
    \label{eq: total-loss}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda,\alpha,\beta\Ge0$. For simplicity, the introduction assumes $\alpha+\beta=1$; Section~\ref{subsec: total-loss} allows arbitrary nonnegative mixture weights. Under this convention, the final term is the log-ratio variation under the mixture distribution $\alpha\hat\mu+\beta\bar\nu$. Its pairwise expectation includes pairs drawn within either distribution and pairs with one sample from each distribution.

Although training seeks $G_{\#}\mu\approx\mu_0$, the pushforward definition implies the exact identity $G_{\#}\nu=\mu_0$. Combining \eqref{eq: pushforward-density} with $\mu(y)=\exp(-U(y))$ and $\mu_0(x)=\exp(-U_0(x))$ yields
\begin{equation}
    z(y):=\log\frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)} = U_0(G(y)) - U(y) - \log|\det J_G(y)|.
    \label{eq: logratio-z}
\end{equation}
Hence the loss in terms of the map is
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}[G]
    &= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \mu}\bigl[z(y)\bigr]}_{\KL(\mu\|\nu)}
     + \lambda \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \mu}\bigl[|z(y) - z(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)}
     + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu}\bigl[|z(y) - z(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_{\alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu}(\mu\|\nu)} .
    \label{eq: total-loss-G-intro}
\end{align}
The default choice $(\lambda,\alpha,\beta)=(1,\frac12,\frac12)$ is
\begin{align}
    \mathrm{KLXX}[G]
    &= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \mu}\bigl[z(y)\bigr]}_{\KL(\mu\|\nu)}
     + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \mu}\bigl[|z(y) - z(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)}
     + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim (\hat\mu + \bar\nu)/2}\bigl[|z(y) - z(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_{(\hat\mu + \bar\nu)/2}(\mu\|\nu)} .
    \label{eq: KLXX-G-intro}
\end{align}
With equal target and mixture batch sizes, KLXX evaluates about twice as many points per step as forward KL and also requires the QT construction.

Our contributions are described as follows. First, we introduce the KLXX objective, whose new component mixes QT and pushforward samples. Pairwise differences remove the unknown target normalizer, and the exact-target loss retains $\nu=\mu$ as a minimizer. The target-weighted variation reuses the forward KL batch, while the mixture supplies candidate-mode and proposal-support information absent from that batch.

Second, we analyze forward KL with $\X_\mu$ in Fisher--Rao geometry. The regularizer adds a nonpositive term to the instantaneous KL dissipation. For a fixed biased target surrogate and regularization strength below the stated threshold, it also improves the stationary-error bound by a nonnegative discrepancy term. These results do not cover the full KLXX loss, the changing empirical surrogate used in training, or discrete neural-network optimization.

Third, we build an adaptive-staging Boltzmann generator that uses neither external target data nor a replay buffer. AIS and QT generate the required samples on the fly, and the MCMC transitions use only derivatives of $U$ without differentiating the flow density. Each stage compares its trained proposal with the identity on the complete validation set, retains the proposal with higher ESS, and shrinks rejected stage increments. For molecular potentials, an additional continuation sharpens a regularized interaction core toward the reported endpoint.

Fourth, we demonstrate that QT gives KLXX a mode-discovery signal unavailable to AIS-only training. QT can reach target basins outside the regions covered by the source and annealed samples. In the Himmelblau and Sparse tests, the AIS-only losses miss remote wells, while both QT-based losses recover every mode; mixing QT and pushforward samples also reduces visible intermodal leakage (Figures~\ref{fig: himmelblau} and~\ref{fig: sparse}). We therefore report ESS together with coverage or a problem-specific mode diagnostic, since uniform weights on reached modes do not imply complete coverage. The clock comparison uses shared stage schedules, whereas the molecular stage schedules are method-specific and single-seed; the results support this mechanism without establishing universal or fixed-compute dominance over forward KL.

\section{Fisher--Rao gradient flow analysis}
\label{sec: FR}

We first analyze forward KL with target-weighted log-ratio variation in Fisher--Rao geometry; Appendix~\ref{sec: appendix-FR} gives the full derivations and proofs. This continuous-time density-space idealization exposes the mechanism of the loss but does not imply a convergence rate for the discrete optimizer: we view $\nu$ as a probability density evolving along the gradient flow and ask how fast $\KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$ decays to zero.

For a tractable analysis we keep only the forward KL and the shape term $\X_\mu$, the log-ratio variation at weight $\omega = \mu$. The contributions weighted by $\hat\mu$ and $\bar\nu$ are left out: these fixed training measures do not enter the density-space idealization. The loss is then
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal L[\nu] = \KL(\mu\|\nu) + \lambda\,\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu),
    \label{eq: loss KL X}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda > 0$ fixed. Write $w=\mu/\nu$ for the importance weight and $z=\log w$ for the log-ratio. Because $\X_\mu$ contains an absolute value, we use the subgradient selection $\operatorname{sgn}(0)=0$. The statements below apply to positive solutions of the resulting Fisher--Rao subgradient flow; for $\lambda\Le\tfrac12$, its explicit birth term is nonnegative and preserves positivity.

The choice of Riemannian metric matters. In the Wasserstein-2 geometry the forward KL is not displacement convex, so the Otto--Villani/Bakry--{\'E}mery argument used for the reverse KL does not apply. We therefore work in the Fisher--Rao (FR) geometry. Along a positive FR solution, the forward KL has an exponential bound, and $\X_\mu$ adds a nonlocal birth--death term: the update at one point depends on how its importance weight ranks among weights under $\mu$. Writing the $\nu$-mean as $\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle_\nu := \int (\cdot)\,\nu\,\D x$, the FR gradient of a smooth functional $\mathcal F$ and the selected flow for $\mathcal L$ are
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal F(\nu) = \nu\Bigl(\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu} - \Bigl\langle\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu}\Bigr\rangle_\nu\Bigr),
    \qquad
    \partial_t\nu_t = -\,\mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal L(\nu_t),
    \label{eq: FR-gradient}
\end{equation}
and the mean-subtraction makes $\int \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal F = 0$, so the flow conserves probability mass.

Differentiating $\KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$ along this flow, and reducing the $\X_\mu$ term by a rank-correlation identity, gives the dissipation
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\KL(\mu\|\nu_t) = -\chi^2(\mu\|\nu_t) - \lambda\, \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\!\left[ \left|\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu_t(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu_t(y)}\right| \right],
    \label{eq: combined-rate}
\end{equation}
where $\chi^2(\mu\|\nu):=\int(\mu/\nu-1)^2\nu\,\D x$ is the chi-squared divergence. The regularizer contributes a nonpositive term to the instantaneous KL dissipation. Since $\chi^2(\mu\|\nu_t) \Ge \KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$, Gr\"onwall's lemma yields
\begin{equation}
    \KL(\mu\|\nu_t) \Le e^{-t}\,\KL(\mu\|\nu_0), \qquad t \Ge 0,
    \label{eq: KL-exponential}
\end{equation}
without assuming log-concavity, a spectral gap, or a growth condition on $\mu$. This density-space statement does not imply that forward KL or KLXX trains faster with a discrete optimizer. It also excludes the cost of producing target samples. The added term in \eqref{eq: combined-rate} describes how $\X_\mu$ changes the density flow, not a universal transient speedup.

The convergence result assumes that the outer expectation is taken under the exact target. To isolate surrogate bias, fix a smooth density $\tilde\mu$ and replace $\mu$ by $\tilde\mu$ only in the outer integrals while retaining the exact pointwise log-ratio. This defines
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\mathcal L}[\nu]
=\mathbb E_{x\sim\tilde\mu}\!\left[\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)}\right]
+\lambda\,\mathbb E_{x,y\sim\tilde\mu}\!\left[\left|\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)}-\log\frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)}\right|\right].
\label{eq: surrogate-loss-main}
\end{equation}
We assume that the densities are positive on their common domain and that the displayed KL, chi-squared, and pair integrals are finite. At $\lambda=0$, the stationary density is $\nu^\star=\tilde\mu$, so the residual error is $\KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu)$. The next result shows when $\X_\mu$ improves this stationary-error bound. It is an idealization of one detached surrogate, not a theorem for the changing empirical batches used by the trainer.
\begin{theorem}
\label{thm: accuracy}
Assume $\mu$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $\tilde\mu$ and $\lambda \in [0, \tfrac12)$. Then every stationary density $\nu^\star$ of the selected subgradient flow for the fixed-surrogate loss $\widetilde{\mathcal L}$ in \eqref{eq: surrogate-loss-main} satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \KL(\mu\|\nu^\star) \Le \KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu) - \lambda\!\iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\tilde\mu(y) \biggl|\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu^\star(y)}\biggr| \D x \D y.
    \label{eq: accuracy-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\noindent The subtracted term is nonnegative and is positive unless the displayed importance ratio is constant on the support of $\tilde\mu$. Within the theorem's assumptions, the rank feedback raises $\nu^\star$ in comparatively underweighted regions and lowers it elsewhere. The experiments use $\lambda=1$, outside the sufficient range $\lambda<\tfrac12$, so the theorem does not certify their finite-training behavior. Appendix~\ref{sec: appendix-FR} gives the variation, rank-correlation identity, and proofs.

\section{Flow training with log-ratio variation}
\label{sec: training}

This section turns the abstract loss of the introduction into a training algorithm for the flow map $G$. Let $U_0$ and $U$ be the source and target potentials on $\mathbb R^d$, so that $\mu_0 \propto \exp(-U_0)$ is the samplable reference and $\mu \propto \exp(-U)$ is the target. We parameterize $G:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^d$ so that $G_{\#}\mu \approx \mu_0$ and write $\nu := (G^{-1})_{\#}\mu_0$ for the induced approximation to $\mu$. The loss itself evaluates only the native target-to-source map $G$ and its log-Jacobian; it does not explicitly involve $G^{-1}$. The inverse map is nevertheless used to generate Monte Carlo proposals for AIS and pushforward samples for the $\bar\nu$ term. These are sampling-only evaluations: the sampled coordinates are detached from the inverse-map path, while their subsequent log-ratio evaluations remain differentiable through $G$. We first assemble the training loss and identify its sampling ingredients (Section~\ref{subsec: total-loss}), then supply them in turn: an AIS approximation of $\mu$ (Section~\ref{subsec: ais}), the quench and temper construction of the wide-coverage measure $\hat\mu$ (Section~\ref{subsec: QT}), and the ESS and coverage diagnostics used to evaluate the trained flow (Section~\ref{subsec: eval}).

\subsection{Training loss with log-ratio variation}
\label{subsec: total-loss}

By change of variables, the pushforward density is $\nu(y)=\mu_0(G(y))|\det J_G(y)|$, so every term is a functional of the scalar field $z(y)$ in \eqref{eq: logratio-z}. The forward KL is its mean under the target, $\KL(\mu\|\nu)=\mathbb E_{y\sim\mu}[z(y)]$. For the empirical distributions used in training, we extend \eqref{eq: X} to any finite nonnegative measure $\omega$ by integrating against $\omega(\D y)\omega(\D y')$. The same formulas apply on a torus or a Euclidean--torus product with the corresponding standard reference volume. Set $s:=\alpha+\beta$. For $s>0$, let $\xi:=(\alpha\hat\mu+\beta\bar\nu)/s$ be the normalized mixture, with any zero-weight component omitted. Since $\alpha\hat\mu+\beta\bar\nu=s\xi$, the double integral in \eqref{eq: X} produces the factor $s^2$ below. The map-level loss is
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}[G]
    &= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \mu}\bigl[z(y)\bigr]}_{\KL(\mu\|\nu)}
     + \lambda \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \mu}\bigl[|z(y) - z(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)}
     + \underbrace{s^2\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \xi}\bigl[|z(y) - z(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_{\alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu}(\mu\|\nu)} .
    \label{eq: total-loss-G}
\end{align}
When $s=0$, the last term is omitted. Equation~\eqref{eq: total-loss-G} is the map form of \eqref{eq: total-loss} after removing the introductory restriction $s=1$; the factor $s^2$ follows from the double integral in \eqref{eq: X} and is required when $\alpha+\beta\ne1$. The default choice $\lambda=1$ and $\alpha=\beta=\frac12$ defines KLXX. The pushforward distribution $\nu=(G^{-1})_{\#}\mu_0$ depends on the trainable parameters of $G$. The barred measure $\bar\nu$ is the same distribution frozen at the current parameters: samples are drawn through $G^{-1}$ and detached from that sampling path, but $z$ at the sampled coordinates is still differentiated through the native map $G$.

The three sampling measures entering \eqref{eq: total-loss-G} address complementary failure modes of the bare forward KL:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{$\mu$: target accuracy.} The term $\X_\mu$ penalizes pairwise log-ratio variation within the target batch. Theorem~\ref{thm: accuracy} gives its density-space bound for $\lambda<\frac12$; the finite-training effect at $\lambda=1$ is tested empirically.
    \item \emph{$\hat\mu$: candidate mode coverage.} QT provides comparisons on basins represented in its wide-coverage set, including basins absent from the annealed batch. This supplies a training signal for those represented basins but does not guarantee that QT finds every mode.
    \item \emph{$\bar\nu$: proposal support.} Pushforward samples evaluate the log-ratio where the current proposal places mass. The term is intended to reduce variation in target-poor regions; the experiments test whether this is accompanied by less visible leakage.
\end{itemize}
Adding these nonnegative terms to the exact-target forward KL preserves $\nu=\mu$ as a minimizer. The terms change the sampled training signal without changing that reference solution.

For $B\Ge2$, the forward KL and $\X_\mu$ share one target batch: evaluate $\mathbf z = (z(y_1),\dots,z(y_B))$, take $\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf z)$ for the forward KL, and $\mathrm{mean}(|\mathbf z - \mathbf z[\sigma]|)$ for a uniformly random derangement $\sigma$. This is an unbiased randomized estimate of the distinct-pair average and adds only a pairing and pairwise difference to the same differentiation graph. When $s>0$, the last term evaluates one additional batch drawn from $\xi$, applies the same randomized pairwise estimator, and multiplies it by $s^2$. At the KLXX setting, $s=1$ and the batch is split equally between $\hat\mu$ and $\bar\nu$.

The loss draws on three measures, of which at most two must be built. The $\bar\nu$ batch is the raw pushforward $\bar y_i = G^{-1}(x_i)$ of source samples $x_i \sim \mu_0$. The inverse-map sampling path is detached, while $z(\bar y_i)$ is then evaluated through the native map $G$ and differentiated normally. An approximation of the target $\mu$ for the forward KL and $\X_\mu$ terms is supplied by the annealing procedure of Section~\ref{subsec: ais}. When $\alpha>0$, a second construction supplies samples of the wide-coverage measure $\hat\mu$ by quench and temper (Section~\ref{subsec: QT}). Throughout loss differentiation, the sampled coordinates and all resampling, MALA, mixture, and derangement choices are treated as fixed; gradients pass only through evaluations of $z$ at the resulting coordinates. With equal target and mixture batch sizes, $\KL$+$\X_\mu$+$\X_{(\hat\mu+\bar\nu)/2}$ evaluates about twice as many samples per optimizer iteration as forward KL. This does not imply twice the total training time: constructing $\hat\mu$ also applies quench and temper to the complete validation set and adds a problem- and stage-dependent cost. Inference uses the same flow architecture for both methods.

Two recurring settings are the \emph{$\X_\mu$-only setting}, with $\lambda=1$ and $\alpha=\beta=0$, and KLXX, with $\lambda=1$ and $\alpha=\beta=\frac12$. The first adds no sampling measure beyond the target batch, while the second splits its mixture batch equally between $\hat\mu$ and $\bar\nu$. We report the full KLXX loss as $\KL$+$\X_\mu$+$\X_{(\hat\mu+\bar\nu)/2}$ when it must be distinguished from the other ablations.

\begin{remark}[Why forward KL is still necessary]
The \emph{$\X_\mu$-only setting} above means $\KL+\X_\mu$; it does not remove forward KL. Log-ratio variation alone is not a reliable training objective: empirical pairwise terms constrain relative log-ratios only on sampled regions and provide no global density-shaping constraint outside them. Appendix~D.2 of the LDR study \citep{schopmans2026ldr} confirms the analogous failure: without a data-based divergence, training with only LDR-1 or only LDR-2 produces qualitatively incorrect distributions. We therefore always combine $\X$-regularization with forward KL.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Approximating \texorpdfstring{$\mu$}{mu} by annealed importance sampling}
\label{subsec: ais}

Evaluating the $\mu$-expectations in \eqref{eq: total-loss-G} requires samples from $\mu$, which are not directly available. We form a surrogate by drawing Monte Carlo proposals $y_1,\dots,y_B$ from the pushforward $\nu=(G^{-1})_{\#}\mu_0$ and reweighting them. In the theory, the normalized importance ratio is $w=\mu/\nu$. Computation uses the proportional weight
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde w(y)
    := \exp\Big( U_0(G(y)) - U(y) - \log|\det J_G(y)| \Big)
    \propto \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)},
    \qquad y \in \mathbb R^d,
    \label{eq: weight}
\end{equation}
Below, $w_i$ denotes these proportional weights because resampling, normalized ESS, and log-ratio differences are scale invariant. A resampling step and a short Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm (MALA) simulation then diffuses the duplicates. In high dimensions, however, $\mu$ and $\nu$ can differ greatly, so the weights can collapse onto a few outliers. We temper the reweighting through $M+1$ intermediate distributions
\begin{equation}
    \pi_m(y) \propto \nu(y)^{1-\frac{m}{M}} \mu(y)^{\frac{m}{M}} \propto ((G^{-1})_{\#}\mu_0)(y)\, \widetilde w(y)^{\frac{m}{M}}, \qquad m = 0,1,\dots,M,
    \label{eq: pi-m}
\end{equation}
so that $\pi_0 = \nu$ and $\pi_M = \mu$. Each step reweights by only $\widetilde w(y)^{1/M}$, reducing the degeneracy of direct importance sampling.


\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\setstretch{1.2}
\caption{Annealed importance sampling with pushforward proposals toward $\mu$}
\label{alg: AIS}
\KwInput{Monte Carlo proposals $y_1,\dots,y_B$ from $(G^{-1})_{\#}\mu_0$, AIS ladder length $M$, intermediate distributions $\pi_m$ \eqref{eq: pi-m}}
\KwOutput{biased target-surrogate batch $y_1,\dots,y_B$ approximating $\mu$}

\For{$m = 1,\dots,M$}{
    Compute the incremental weights $\widetilde w(y_i)^{1/M}=\exp(z(y_i)/M)$ on $y_1,\dots,y_B$\;
    \tcp{resample}
    Resample from the weighted set, $y_1',\dots,y_B' \gets \mathrm{resample}\bigl(y_{1:B}, (\widetilde w(y_i)^{1/M})_{i=1}^B\bigr)$\;
    \tcp{rejuvenate under the target}
    Diffuse the resampled duplicates by a short MALA chain on $\mu$, $y_{1:B} \gets \mathrm{MALA}(y_{1:B}', U)$\;
}
\end{algorithm}
\mbox{} \\
An exact SMC sampler on the path \eqref{eq: pi-m} would rejuvenate at step $m$ with a Markov kernel invariant for $\pi_m$. A MALA kernel for $\pi_m$ would require $\nabla_y\log\nu(y)$ and hence positional derivatives of the flow density, including $\nabla_y\log|\det J_G(y)|$. Algorithm~\ref{alg: AIS} instead applies MALA directly under $\mu$, using only $-\nabla U$ in its proposal. MALA preserves $\mu$, but not the intermediate $\pi_m$, so the output is a biased target surrogate rather than exact samples from $\mu$. The construction trades intermediate invariance for a sampler that moves resampled particles under the final target. Section~\ref{sec: FR} studies a fixed smooth surrogate as a density-space idealization; it does not model the changing empirical output of Algorithm~\ref{alg: AIS} exactly. Evaluation therefore uses fresh importance weights and coverage diagnostics.

\begin{remark}[Choosing the SMC/AIS ladder length $M$]
\label{rem: ladder-length}
For a target close to the source, $M=1$ can suffice. When the change is distributed across many coordinates, the log-weight increment accumulates their contributions, so $M$ should grow with the effective dimension of the deformation. The choice $M=d$ is a conservative default when all $d$ coordinates change; a shorter ladder is appropriate when only a few directions differ.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Comparison with FAB]
\label{rem: fab}
FAB \citep{midgley2022flow} also couples AIS to flow training, but places the flow inside the chains. Its MCMC transitions target the surrogate $\mu^2/\nu$ and require the log-density gradient
\begin{equation*}
    \nabla_y \log \frac{\mu^2(y)}{\nu(y)} = -2\nabla U(y) - \nabla_y \log\nu(y)
\end{equation*}
which contains the positional gradient of the pushforward density at every MCMC step. Training uses a prioritized replay buffer of weighted AIS samples with a staleness correction. Reusing the buffer reduces repeated AIS generation but adds storage, prioritized sampling, and correction work. Here every MALA chain targets $\mu$ and uses only $-\nabla U$; the flow enters only through the initial $G^{-1}$ proposal and evaluations of $z$. Each gradient step starts from a new source batch, so no replay buffer is kept. Relative to forward KL, KLXX instead adds the QT construction and pairwise log-ratio variation evaluations: $\X_\mu$ reuses the target batch, while the mixture term evaluates an additional batch. The fixed $N$-sample QT set of Section~\ref{subsec: QT} supplies separate coverage information. FAB instead relies on its AIS budget for exploration. The $2$-divergence gives FAB a stronger tail-coverage incentive, and with sufficient AIS work it may need no separate coverage construction.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Constructing \texorpdfstring{$\hat\mu$}{mu-hat} by quench and temper}
\label{subsec: QT}

When $\alpha>0$, the last term needs samples from the wide-coverage measure $\hat\mu$. Algorithms~\ref{alg: QT} and~\ref{alg: boltzmann} use the complete validation set $\mathcal Y=\{x_1,\dots,x_N\}$ for each stage, and QT acts on all $N$ samples once per stage attempt. The resulting set is held fixed as a reservoir during optimization. At each training step a mini-batch of $B$ samples is drawn uniformly from that reservoir and refreshed by a short MALA chain before entering the $\X_\omega$ computation. We use $\hat\mu$ for the law of this draw-and-refresh operation; its input is the uniform empirical measure on the fixed QT reservoir. Standalone one-flow ablations may instead report a separately sized QT set; the staged algorithm uses only the validation set size $N$ and batch size $B$. Quench and temper (QT) is the map
\begin{equation*}
    \hat{\mathcal Y} = \mathrm{QT}(\mathcal Y,U)
\end{equation*}
from the validation set and target potential $U$ to a fixed wide-coverage reservoir $\hat{\mathcal Y}=\{\hat x_1,\dots,\hat x_N\}$. Its uniform empirical distribution is the input to the per-step refresh defining $\hat\mu$. QT consists of melt, quench, and temper.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\setstretch{1.2}
\caption{Quench and temper (QT): wide-coverage construction}
\label{alg: QT}
\KwInput{validation set $\mathcal Y = \{x_1,\dots,x_N\}$, target potential $U$, melt scale $m_e$}
\KwOutput{wide-coverage set $\hat{\mathcal Y} = \{\hat x_1,\dots,\hat x_N\}$ forming $\hat\mu$, written $\hat{\mathcal Y} = \mathrm{QT}(\mathcal Y,U)$}
\tcp{melt}
Scatter the samples $x_i \gets x_i + m_e\zeta_i$ with $\zeta_i \sim \mathcal N(0, I_d)$ i.i.d. and melt scale $m_e > 0$\;
\tcp{quench}
Drive each sample toward a mode center of $\mu$ by L-BFGS on $U$, updating $x_1,\dots,x_N$\;
\tcp{temper}
Spread the samples around each mode, $\hat x_{1:N} \gets \mathrm{MALA}(x_{1:N}, U)$\;
\end{algorithm}
\mbox{}\\
The quench places samples near local minima of $U$, and the Gaussian scatter broadens the set of basins that can be reached. On a torus or Euclidean--torus product, periodic coordinates are interpreted modulo their period after the melt and after each MALA proposal. If the QT set contains a basin absent from the annealed target batch, $\X_{\hat\mu}$ supplies pairwise log-ratio evaluations on that basin. QT does not certify complete mode discovery.

A large melt scale $m_e$ can bias the QT reservoir toward basins far from the input set. This is intentional: $\hat\mu$ is a coverage measure, not an estimate of target mode probabilities. Adding $\X_{\hat\mu}$ to the exact-target forward KL preserves $\nu=\mu$ as a minimizer, while empirical frequencies in the finite reservoir affect the optimization path.

\subsection{Importance sampling at evaluation: ESS and coverage}
\label{subsec: eval}

At evaluation time the trained flow $G$ supplies the pushforward distribution $\nu=(G^{-1})_{\#}\mu_0$ and its tractable density $\nu(y)=\mu_0(G(y))|\det J_G(y)|$. The proportional importance weight $\widetilde w(y)$ in \eqref{eq: weight} can therefore be evaluated on any pushforward sample without fitting another density model. Draw $\mathbf y=(y_1,\dots,y_N)$ with $y_i=G^{-1}(x_i)$ and $x_i\sim\mu_0$ independently, and write $\mathbf w=(w_1,\dots,w_N)$ for any common scaling of the corresponding positive weights. These weights diagnose agreement on reached support and form self-normalized importance estimates or a reweighted or resampled target approximation, followed when desired by a short MALA refresh under $\mu$.

We assess this pipeline through two metrics: the effective sample size, which measures how uniform the importance weights are on the support reached by the flow, and the coverage, which checks whether the regions represented in the QT reference set have been reached.

The first metric is the effective sample size, normalized to $[0,1]$ by dividing the classical ESS by the sample count $N$. For the weight vector $\mathbf w$, define
\begin{equation}
    \ESS(\mathbf w) = \frac{\big(\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i\big)^2}{N \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i^2} \in [0,1].
\end{equation}
The upper bound follows from Cauchy--Schwarz and is attained when all weights are equal. The factor $N$ distinguishes this normalized ESS from the classical count $(\sum_iw_i)^2/\sum_iw_i^2\in[1,N]$ \citep{martino2017rethinking}. Multiplying all weights by a common positive constant leaves both definitions unchanged. A high ESS indicates uniform observed weights only on support reached by $\nu$; a target mode absent from the sample cannot lower it.

To detect such missing modes, we pair the ESS with the coverage metric of \citet{naeem2020reliable}. Let $\hat{\mathbf y} = (\hat y_1,\dots,\hat y_N) \in \mathbb R^{N\times d}$ be the fixed QT reference reservoir produced by Algorithm~\ref{alg: QT}. For each $1 \Le i \Le N$, the $\kappa$-nearest-neighbor radius of $\hat y_i$ (with $\kappa = 5$) is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{NND}_\kappa(\hat y_i;\hat{\mathbf y}) = \inf\Big\{ r \Ge 0 :
    \# \big\{1\Le j \Le N: 0 < |\hat y_i - \hat y_j| < r\big\} \Ge \kappa
    \Big\},
\end{equation}
i.e., the distance from $\hat y_i$ to its $\kappa$-th nearest neighbor in $\hat{\mathbf y}$. The coverage of $\hat{\mathbf y}$ by the pushforward samples $\mathbf y$ is then
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Coverage}_\kappa(\mathbf y;\hat{\mathbf y}) =
    \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf 1\Bigl\{
        \exists  1\Le j\Le N : |y_j - \hat y_i| \Le \mathrm{NND}_\kappa(\hat y_i;\hat{\mathbf y})
    \Bigr\},
\end{equation}
i.e., the fraction of points in $\hat{\mathbf y}$ whose $\kappa$-nearest-neighbor ball contains at least one pushforward sample. A coverage close to $1$ indicates that the finite QT reference set is well covered by the flow, while a coverage well below $1$ exposes missing regions that the ESS alone cannot see. Because the reference reservoir is itself produced by QT, the diagnostic checks only modes found by that reference construction; it cannot reveal a mode absent from the reference set.

\section{Adaptive-staging Boltzmann generator}
\label{sec: boltzmann}

A single flow can be inadequate when the source-to-target change is too large, so we divide the interpolation into stages. Each stage changes the potential by a controlled amount and trains one flow with the loss in \eqref{eq: total-loss-G}, using user-chosen weights $\lambda,\alpha,\beta\Ge0$. The KLXX Boltzmann generator is the special case $(\lambda,\alpha,\beta)=(1,\frac12,\frac12)$; the construction below also permits other choices. Sampling diagnostics determine the stage schedule.

\subsection{Construction of the adaptive stage schedule}

To bridge the simple source $\mu_0 \propto \exp(-U_0)$ to the target $\mu \propto \exp(-U)$, we seek an increasing sequence
\begin{equation*}
    0 = t_0 < t_1 < \cdots < t_K = 1
\end{equation*}
defining the interpolating potential and measure
\begin{equation*}
    U_t(x)=(1-t)U_0(x)+tU(x),
\qquad \mu_t(x)\propto\exp(-U_t(x)),
    \qquad t\in[0,1].
\end{equation*}
Here $t$ is a dimensionless interpolation parameter, not a thermodynamic temperature. The discrete values $t_k$ are stage points, and stage $k$ is the transition from $t_{k-1}$ to $t_k$. At the accepted stage points, write $\mu_k:=\mu_{t_k}$, so $\mu_0$ is the source and $\mu_K$ is the target. Stage $k$ trains a target-to-source map $G_k$, whose inverse induces the proposal $\nu_k=(G_k^{-1})_{\#}\mu_{k-1}$ for $\mu_k$. The number of stages $K$ is selected during the run. Its central choice is the stage increment $\Delta t_k=t_k-t_{k-1}$: a large increment can be hard to fit, whereas a small increment adds stages.

Training $G_k$ requires the target surrogate for $\mu_k$ defined in Section~\ref{subsec: ais}. Before training, an optional classical SMC calculation can screen the proposed stage increment $\Delta t_k$. Its smallest incremental ESS measures overlap along that candidate stage for the current particle set. This is a difficulty diagnostic, not a mode-coverage guarantee. Its floor lies in $[0,1)$, and setting it to zero disables the screen.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
    \setstretch{1.2}
\caption{Adaptive selection of the stage increment $\Delta t_k$}
\label{alg: step}
\KwInput{potentials $U_0, U$, previous stage point $t_{k-1}$, validation set $\mathcal Y_{k-1} = \{y_1,\dots,y_N\}$ approximating $\mu_{k-1}$, SMC ladder length $M$, SMC ESS floor $\tau_{\mathrm{SMC}} = 0$, shrink factor $\gamma = 0.7$, initial guess $t_{\mathrm{init}}$}
\KwOutput{next stage point $t_{k}$, giving the stage increment $\Delta t_k = t_k - t_{k-1}$}

$U_{t_{k-1}} \gets (1-t_{k-1}) U_0 + t_{k-1} U$\;
\tcp{start from the supplied initial guess}
$t_k \gets t_{\mathrm{init}}$\;
\If{$\tau_{\mathrm{SMC}} = 0$}{
    \Return $t_k$\;
}
\While{true}{
    \tcp{run a classical SMC ladder across the candidate stage}
    Run an $M$-step classical SMC ladder on a working copy of all $N$ particles in $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$, using the linear potential bridge from $U_{t_{k-1}}$ to $U_{t_k}$ and a MALA kernel invariant for each intermediate distribution ($M$ as in Remark~\ref{rem: ladder-length})\;
    \tcp{evaluate the ladder by its worst per-step ESS}
    Record $\ESS_{\min}$, the smallest per-step ESS over the $M$ ladder steps\;
    \eIf{$\ESS_{\min} \Ge \tau_{\mathrm{SMC}}$}{
        \Return $t_k$\;
    }{
        \tcp{shrink the stage increment toward $t_{k-1}$}
        $t_k \gets t_{k-1} + \gamma\,(t_k - t_{k-1})$\;
    }
}
\end{algorithm}
\mbox{}\\
When enabled, these SMC ladders involve only the potentials $U_0$ and $U$, never the flow map. They use no QT samples, so their per-step ESS can overlook a mode the SMC samples never reach. We therefore use this ESS only to screen a candidate stage; it is a heuristic measure of stage difficulty, not a coverage certificate. The selected-proposal validation in step~(v) supplies the acceptance gate, while QT supplies the separate wide-coverage set when $\alpha>0$. By default $\tau_{\mathrm{SMC}} = 0$, so Algorithm~\ref{alg: step} returns $t_{\mathrm{init}}$ without running SMC. For a positive floor, it returns the first candidate stage along the shrink sequence whose estimated ESS clears that floor. The shrink factor is $\gamma = 0.7$. As $t_k$ approaches $t_{k-1}$, the stage becomes trivial and its ESS approaches $1$, so a floor below $1$ is attainable under repeated shrinking in exact arithmetic.

\subsection{The full training procedure}

Algorithm~\ref{alg: boltzmann} combines the stage-selection rule with the training procedure. It carries a fixed-size validation set $\mathcal Y_k\approx\mu_k$ across stages. Each candidate stage builds its QT set when $\alpha>0$, trains a flow with the selected loss weights, and compares that flow with the identity proposal. If neither proposal reaches the validation ESS floor $\tau_{\mathrm v}\in[0,1)$, the algorithm shrinks the stage increment and retries. The displayed loop ends when $t_K=1$. Practical implementations use an endpoint tolerance and finite caps on retries and stages; reaching a cap returns an incomplete stage schedule.

\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item \emph{Validation set.} Stage $k$ uses the \emph{validation set size} $N$ and the \emph{batch size} $B$. The validation set $\mathcal Y_{k-1}=\{y_1,\dots,y_N\}\approx\mu_{k-1}$ is carried over from the previous stage. A working copy of all $N$ samples supplies the SMC calculation in step~(ii), and, when $\alpha>0$, all $N$ samples initialize QT in step~(iii). Neither operation first draws a separately sized subset. Training batches are drawn at run time, $B$ samples per gradient step, from $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$; no separate training set is built.

\item \emph{Adaptive stage selection.} Algorithm~\ref{alg: step} selects the next stage point $t_k$. The first stage starts from $t_{\mathrm{init}}=t_{\mathrm{safe}}=0.2$. Later stages extrapolate the last accepted stage increment, $t_{\mathrm{init}}=\min\bigl(1,t_{k-1}+\Gamma(t_{k-1}-t_{k-2})\bigr)$, with enlarge factor $\Gamma=1.5$. The default $\tau_{\mathrm{SMC}}=0$ accepts this initial guess without the optional SMC screen. If a positive floor is supplied, the algorithm shrinks the stage increment until the classical SMC ESS on all $N$ particles clears that floor. The selected stage target is $\mu_k\propto\exp(-U_{t_k})$, with $U_{t_k}=(1-t_k)U_0+t_kU$.

\item \emph{Wide-coverage set.} When $\alpha>0$, quench and temper (Algorithm~\ref{alg: QT}) acts on the complete validation set $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$ under $U_{t_k}$. The resulting $N$ samples $\hat{\mathcal Y}_k=\mathrm{QT}(\mathcal Y_{k-1},U_{t_k})$ form the fixed stage reservoir. The measure $\hat\mu_k$ is the law obtained by drawing uniformly from this reservoir and applying the short MALA refresh used at each gradient step; it supplies the wide-coverage component of the last term in \eqref{eq: stage-loss}. When $\alpha=0$, that component vanishes and the QT construction is omitted.

\item \emph{Training loss.} The flow $G_k$ is trained by the general loss \eqref{eq: total-loss-G} specialized to the stage $\mu_{k-1}\to\mu_k$. With the pushforward distribution $\nu_k = (G_k^{-1})_{\#}\mu_{k-1}$ and its frozen sampling measure $\bar\nu_k$, the stage log-ratio is
\begin{equation*}
    z_k(y) = U_{t_{k-1}}(G_k(y)) - U_{t_k}(y) - \log|\det J_{G_k}(y)|.
\end{equation*}
Let $s=\alpha+\beta$. For $s>0$, define the stage specialization of the normalized mixture $\xi$, with any zero-weight component omitted,
\begin{equation*}
    \xi_k:=\frac{\alpha\hat\mu_k+\beta\bar\nu_k}{s}.
\end{equation*}
The stage loss then reads
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}[G_k]
    &= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \mu_k}\bigl[z_k(y)\bigr]}_{\KL(\mu_k\|\nu_k)}
     + \lambda\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \mu_k}\bigl[|z_k(y) - z_k(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_{\mu_k}(\mu_k\|\nu_k)}
     + \underbrace{s^2\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \xi_k}\bigl[|z_k(y) - z_k(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_{\alpha\hat\mu_k+\beta\bar\nu_k}(\mu_k\|\nu_k)} ,
    \label{eq: stage-loss}
\end{align}
When $s=0$, the last term is omitted. The $\mu_k$-expectations are estimated from the biased AIS surrogate: a batch $\mathcal X$ drawn from $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$ is pushed to the proposal batch $\bar{\mathcal X}_\nu = G_k^{-1}(\mathcal X)$, approximating $\nu_k$, and annealed toward $\mu_k$ by Algorithm~\ref{alg: AIS} with $G = G_k$. When $s>0$, a batch $\mathcal X_{\xi_k}$ from $\xi_k$ is formed by drawing from $\hat\mu_k$ with probability $\alpha/s$ when $\alpha>0$, and from $\bar\nu_k$ with probability $\beta/s$ when $\beta>0$; the randomized pairwise estimate is then multiplied by $s^2$. This factor retains the general-weight definition in \eqref{eq: X} when $\alpha+\beta\ne1$. The choices $(\lambda,\alpha,\beta)=(0,0,0)$ and $(1,0,0)$ give forward KL and the $\X_{\mu_k}$-only loss, respectively; $(1,\frac12,\frac12)$ gives the default KLXX loss.

\item \emph{Validation set update.} Once the candidate $G_k$ is trained, each $y \in \mathcal Y_{k-1}$ is pushed through its inverse to $\tilde y = G_k^{-1}(y)$. This gives the trained proposal set $\tilde{\mathcal Y}_k^{\mathrm{tr}} = G_k^{-1}(\mathcal Y_{k-1})$ from $\nu_k = (G_k^{-1})_{\#}\mu_{k-1}$, together with proportional importance weights represented by
\begin{equation*}
    w_k^{\mathrm{tr}}(\tilde y) := \exp\bigl(z_k(\tilde y)\bigr),
    \qquad \tilde y \in \tilde{\mathcal Y}_k^{\mathrm{tr}} .
\end{equation*}
The identity proposal keeps $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$ unchanged and has weights $w_k^{\mathrm{id}}(y)=\exp[U_{t_{k-1}}(y)-U_{t_k}(y)]$. We select the pair with the larger ESS over the complete validation set and denote it by $(\tilde{\mathcal Y}_k,w_k)$, taking the stage map to be $G_k$ or the identity accordingly. If $\ESS(w_k) < \tau_{\mathrm v}$, with default $\tau_{\mathrm v} = 0.6$, we discard the candidate, shrink to $t_k \gets t_{k-1} + \gamma\,(t_k - t_{k-1})$, and repeat steps (iii)--(v) at the reduced stage increment. Otherwise we advance the validation set to $\mathcal Y_k = \mathrm{MALA}\bigl(\mathrm{resample}(\tilde{\mathcal Y}_k, w_k;N),\, U_{t_k}\bigr) \approx \mu_k$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{algorithm}[p]
    \setstretch{1.1}
\caption{Adaptive-staging Boltzmann generator training}
\label{alg: boltzmann}
\small
\KwInput{potentials $U_0,U$; sizes $N,B\Ge2$; loss weights $\lambda,\alpha,\beta\Ge0$; SMC/AIS ladder length $M$; ESS floors $\tau_{\mathrm{SMC}}=0$, $\tau_{\mathrm v}=0.6$; factors $\gamma=0.7$, $\Gamma=1.5$; safe start $t_{\mathrm{safe}}=0.2$}
\KwOutput{completed stage schedule $0=t_0<\cdots<t_K=1$ and selected stage maps $\{G_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$}

\tcp{validation set, $\mathcal Y_k \approx \mu_k$}
$\mathcal Y_0 \gets N$ samples from $\mu_0$, \quad $t_0 \gets 0$, \quad $k \gets 0$\;
\While{$t_k < 1$}{
    $k \gets k + 1$\;
    Set $U_{t_{k-1}} \gets (1-t_{k-1}) U_0 + t_{k-1} U$\;
    \tcp{(i) validation set}
    Use $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$ for stage selection, validation, and QT when $\alpha>0$\;
    \tcp{(ii) adaptive stage selection}
    \eIf{$k=1$}{
        Set $t_{\mathrm{init}} = t_{\mathrm{safe}}$\;
    }
    {
        Set $t_{\mathrm{init}} = \min\big(1, t_{k-1} + \Gamma(t_{k-1} - t_{k-2})\big)$\;
    }
    Choose $t_k$ by Algorithm~\ref{alg: step} on $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$ from $t_{\mathrm{init}}$\;
    Set $U_{t_k} \gets (1-t_k) U_0 + t_k U$\;
    \Repeat{$\ESS(w_k) \Ge \tau_{\mathrm v}$}{
        Initialize $G_k$ to the identity map\;
        \tcp{(iii) optional wide-coverage set}
        If $\alpha>0$, set $\hat{\mathcal Y}_k \gets \mathrm{QT}(\mathcal Y_{k-1},U_{t_k})$ by Algorithm~\ref{alg: QT}\;
        \tcp{(iv) general log-ratio-variation loss}
        \For{each gradient step}{
            Draw $B$ samples $\mathcal X$ from $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$\;
            Set the pushforward batch $\bar{\mathcal X}_\nu \gets G_k^{-1}(\mathcal X)$ and detach its sampling path\;
            Anneal these Monte Carlo proposals toward $\mu_k$ to form the biased target batch $\mathcal X_\mu$ (Algorithm~\ref{alg: AIS})\;
            If $\alpha>0$, draw $\hat{\mathcal X}_\mu\subset\hat{\mathcal Y}_k$ and refresh it by MALA under $U_{t_k}$\;
            $s\gets\alpha+\beta$; if $s>0$, mix $\hat{\mathcal X}_\mu$ (when $\alpha>0$) and $\bar{\mathcal X}_\nu$ with probabilities $\alpha/s,\beta/s$ to form $\mathcal X_{\xi_k}$\;
            Update $G_k$ with the loss below using random derangements; omit its last term if $s=0$:
            \[
                \widehat{\mathcal L}[G_k]=\widehat{\mathbb E}_{y\in\mathcal X_\mu}\bigl[z_k(y)\bigr]+\lambda\widehat{\mathbb E}_{y,y'\in\mathcal X_\mu}\bigl[|z_k(y)-z_k(y')|\bigr]+s^2\widehat{\mathbb E}_{y,y'\in\mathcal X_{\xi_k}}\bigl[|z_k(y)-z_k(y')|\bigr].
            \]
        }
        \tcp{(v) validation set update}
        Form the trained and identity pairs as in step~(v)\;
        Keep the larger-ESS pair as $(\tilde{\mathcal Y}_k,w_k)$ and set $G_k$ to the corresponding map\;
        \eIf{$\ESS(w_k) < \tau_{\mathrm v}$}{
            \tcp{reject the stage and shrink toward $t_{k-1}$}
            $t_k \gets t_{k-1} + \gamma\,(t_k - t_{k-1})$, then update $U_{t_k} \gets (1-t_k) U_0 + t_k U$\;
        }{
            $\mathcal Y_k \gets \mathrm{MALA}\bigl(\mathrm{resample}(\tilde{\mathcal Y}_k,w_k;N),U_{t_k}\bigr)$\;
        }
    }
}
$K \gets k$\;
\Return $\{t_k\}_{k=0}^{K}$ and $\{G_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$\;
\end{algorithm}
\mbox{}\\
The accepted stage schedule defines a Boltzmann generator for $\mu$. Starting with samples from $\mu_0$, stage $k$ applies the selected inverse map or identity, computes $w_k$, resamples, and rejuvenates under $U_{t_k}$. The resulting validation set supplies stage $k+1$.

The accepted-stage weights also define the propagation factor used below:
\begin{equation}
    F
    := \prod_{k=1}^{K}\ESS(w_k)^{-1}.
    \label{eq: propagation-factor}
\end{equation}
Because normalized ESS lies in $(0,1]$, $F$ is at least one, with the ideal value one. It summarizes stagewise weight degeneracy by multiplying reciprocal incremental ESS values. It is neither the ESS of the complete composed sampler nor an estimator of endpoint Monte Carlo error. If a stage contains another accepted reweighting operation, such as molecular sharpening in Section~\ref{sec: molecular}, its reciprocal ESS enters the product.

% ===================== Section 5: model problems =====================
\section{Numerical experiments on model problems}
\label{sec: experiments}

We now test the roles assigned to the three sampling distributions. The 2D targets assess target accuracy, QT-assisted mode coverage, and visible proposal leakage. The product multi-well tests how the ESS difference changes with dimension. The tilted $\phi^4$ model shows why ESS must be paired with a collective-mode diagnostic. The clock model then compares the full objective on shared annealing schedules. Accordingly, ESS is reported with coverage, minority-phase weight, or sector occupancy whenever mode omission is possible.

\subsection{2D benchmark distributions}
\label{subsec: 2d-benchmark}

We consider four 2D targets: Two-Moon, Three-Well, Himmelblau, and Sparse. Two-Moon has two distorted crescent components and isolates finite-batch accuracy because both components lie within reach of the source. The remaining targets isolate mode collapse: Three-Well contains a low-probability basin, Himmelblau has four separated wells, and Sparse places two tight modes far into the source tails.

For every target, we initialize the same neural spline flow at the identity and compare four objectives:
\begin{center}
$\KL$,\qquad
$\KL$+$\X_\mu$,\qquad
$\KL$+$\X_\mu$+$\X_{\hat\mu}$,\qquad
$\KL$+$\X_\mu$+$\X_{(\hat\mu+\bar\nu)/2}$.
\end{center}
The flow has $32$ spline bins, $6$ transforms, and conditioner widths $(128,128)$. Each training step constructs a target surrogate by one AIS update through the current flow. The rejuvenation kernel uses $100$ MALA steps of size $10^{-3}$ for Two-Moon and $50$ MALA steps of size $2\times10^{-3}$ for the other targets. The fixed validation sets contain $5\times10^4$ points for Two-Moon and Himmelblau and $8\times10^4$ for Three-Well and Sparse. For both objectives containing the wide-coverage term $\X_{\hat\mu}$, quench and temper acts on the complete fixed validation set, with melt scale $2$ and an Armijo L-BFGS quench using initial step $0.5$ and $200$ iterations. The final ESS is computed from flow importance weights on that full validation set. Coverage, with $\kappa=5$ against an independently generated quench and temper reference set, measures the fraction of reference configurations represented by the pushforward through the nearest-neighbor test of Section~\ref{subsec: eval}. The reference sets contain $1000$, $4000$, $500$, and $4000$ points for Two-Moon, Three-Well, Himmelblau, and Sparse, respectively; their QT tempering uses $1000$ MALA steps at the target-specific step size above. Hence a collapsed flow can have ESS near one but cannot have full coverage. Each objective--target pair is one fixed-seed training realization, so the numerical differences below describe these matched runs rather than variation across independent trainings.

\subsection{High-dimensional product multi-well}
\label{subsec: highd}

We next isolate accuracy scaling over the sixteen dimensions
$d=16,32,\ldots,256$. Letting $k=\lfloor\log_2 d\rfloor$, we use
\begin{equation}
    U(\mathbf{x})=\frac12\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2
    +12\sum_{i=1}^{k}\exp(-x_i^2).
    \label{eq: highd-potential}
\end{equation}
The first $k$ coordinates are symmetric double wells with minima at $\pm\sqrt{\ln 24}$ and barrier approximately $9.9$, while the remaining $d-k$ coordinates are standard Gaussian. The target therefore has exactly $2^k$ equal-weight sign-pattern modes, where $2^k\Le d<2^{k+1}$. Multimodality occupies only a $k$-dimensional subspace, but the log-weight error accumulates across all $d$ coordinates.

All four objectives use the same identity-initialized neural spline flow on $[-4,4]^d$, with $16$ bins, $6$ transforms, and conditioner widths $(256,256)$. At every dimension, training uses batch size $250$, $2000$ Adam steps at learning rate $10^{-3}$, gradient clipping at $10^2$, and a fixed validation set of size $5000d$. The live target surrogate uses one AIS update followed by $50$ MALA steps of size $2\times10^{-3}$. This is a standalone one-flow ablation, so its separately reported QT set contains $1000d$ particles rather than the complete validation set used by the staged algorithm. QT uses melt scale $2$ and an Armijo L-BFGS quench with initial step $0.5$ and $200$ iterations. Final validation ESS is evaluated on $\max(200d,80000)$ fresh source samples. Each objective--dimension pair is one fixed-seed training realization.

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../Codes/HD_Product/results/ess.png}
    \caption{ESS on the product multi-well target \eqref{eq: highd-potential}. (a) Final validation ESS over all sixteen tested dimensions. (b) Batch ESS during the $d=256$ training run; faint curves are raw values and bold curves are $40$-step moving averages.}
    \label{fig: highd-ess}
\end{figure}

Every expected sign pattern appears in the fresh evaluation samples for every method and dimension, including all $256$ patterns at $d=256$. The ESS comparison is therefore not explained by a missing sign-pattern mode in those samples. Figure~\ref{fig: highd-ess} shows that every objective with log-ratio variation has higher validation ESS than forward KL at all sixteen dimensions. At $d=256$, the final values are $0.4322$ for forward KL, $0.5608$ for $\KL$+$\X_\mu$, $0.5979$ for $\KL$+$\X_\mu$+$\X_{\hat\mu}$, and $0.5687$ for $\KL$+$\X_\mu$+$\X_{(\hat\mu+\bar\nu)/2}$. The two objectives containing $\hat\mu$ behave similarly across dimension. During the $d=256$ training realization, all three objectives with log-ratio variation continue to improve after forward KL begins to plateau.

\subsection{Lattice \texorpdfstring{$\phi^4$}{phi4} field theory: ESS at a collective barrier}
\label{subsec: phi4}

The tilted scalar $\phi^4$ theory tests ESS at a collective barrier. On a periodic $L\times L$ lattice, with $d=L^2$ and magnetization $m(\boldsymbol\phi)=d^{-1}\sum_j\phi_j$, the action is
\begin{equation*}
U(\boldsymbol\phi)
=-0.8\sum_{\langle jl\rangle}\phi_j\phi_l
+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left[\phi_j^2+\frac12(\phi_j^2-1)^2\right]
+h\sum_{j=1}^{d}\phi_j.
\end{equation*}
We take $h=0.0257$ at $L=6$ and $h=0.0144$ at $L=8$. This size-dependent
field keeps the extensive tilt scale nearly fixed:
$hL^2=0.9252$ at $L=6$ and $hL^2=0.9216$ at $L=8$. Thus the two targets have
comparable phase imbalance despite their different volumes.
In the broken phase the field orders near two vacua $\pm v\mathbf 1$, where $v>0$ is the spontaneous field magnitude and $\mathbf 1\in\mathbb R^d$ is the all-ones vector. A collective domain-wall barrier separates the vacua. The positive linear term gives the two phases unequal probability, so the minority weight $p_+=\mathbb P(m>0)$ is not fixed by symmetry and must be learned.

Parallel tempering gives the references $p_+=0.1251\pm0.0009$ and $0.1284\pm0.0014$, statistically consistent with occupancies $0.1260$ and $0.1268$ from an independent mirror-MALA construction. That construction samples the positive vacuum of the symmetric $h=0$ action, obtains the negative vacuum by the exact reflection $\boldsymbol\phi\mapsto-\boldsymbol\phi$, reweights the pair by the field term, and resamples one million configurations. Each loss is run at seeds $0,1,2$ using an identity-initialized neural spline flow on $[-3,3]^d$, with $16$ bins, $6$ transforms, and conditioner widths $(256,256)$. The source is $\mathcal N(0,0.25I_d)$, centered on the barrier. Training uses a fixed validation set of $10^5$ source samples, batch size $500$, $2000$ Adam steps at learning rate $10^{-3}$, and gradient clipping at $10^3$. The live surrogate uses one AIS update followed by $50$ MALA steps of size $2\times10^{-3}$. For both objectives containing $\X_{\hat\mu}$, quench and temper acts on the complete validation set, with melt scale $2$ and an Armijo L-BFGS quench using initial step $0.1$ and $200$ iterations.

\subsection{Boltzmann generator on \texorpdfstring{$p$}{p}-state clock model}
\label{subsec: clock}

The preceding benchmarks use a single flow. We now test the full annealed construction on the $p=6$ clock model on a periodic $8\times8$ lattice,
\begin{equation}
U(\boldsymbol\theta)
=-\sum_{\langle jl\rangle}\cos(\theta_j-\theta_l)
-\frac12\sum_{j=1}^{d}\cos(6\theta_j),
\qquad \boldsymbol\theta\in[-\pi,\pi)^{d},\quad d=64,
\label{eq: clock-potential}
\end{equation}
where $\langle jl\rangle$ runs over nearest-neighbor pairs. The source is uniform on the torus and the bridge is $U_t=tU$. At low temperature, the target separates into six symmetry-related clock sectors.

Each stage uses an identity-initialized neural circular spline flow with $16$ bins, $6$ autoregressive transforms, and conditioner widths $(256,256)$. The validation set contains $4\times10^5$ particles, and both SMC and quench and temper act directly on this complete set. Every stage uses $3000$ Adam steps at learning rate $10^{-3}$, a four-level AIS surrogate with $100$ MALA steps of size $10^{-3}$, energy clipping at $10^3$, and gradient clipping at $10^2$. For KLXX, QT uses wrapped-Gaussian melt scale $2\pi$ and an Armijo L-BFGS quench with initial step $10^{-2}$ and $100$ iterations. The clock stage schedule starts from $t_{\mathrm{safe}}=0.25$, uses enlarge factor $\Gamma=2$, and applies the nondefault gates $\tau_{\mathrm{SMC}}=0.7$ and $\tau_{\mathrm v}=0.4$. We report batch sizes $B=2000,1000,500,250$. An additional completed run lies below this reporting cutoff and is excluded from the table and all aggregate clock claims.

The comparison is schedule-matched. Forward KL first constructs an adaptive stage schedule using SMC preselection and an ESS acceptance gate. The loss $\KL$+$\X_\mu$+$\X_{(\hat\mu+\bar\nu)/2}$ is then trained on the identical accepted values of $t$, without a second gate. Hence the two methods see the same stage increments within each batch-size pair. Every configuration is a single run, so individual stage ESS values carry single-run fluctuation.

The principal metric is the selected-proposal validation ESS of each accepted stage. The propagation factor in \eqref{eq: propagation-factor} summarizes stagewise weight degeneracy across the stage sequence; smaller is better. It is not a full-chain ESS or an endpoint error estimate. Table~\ref{tab: clock-summary} reports every accepted stage and the resulting factor.

\section{Boltzmann generators on molecular potential energies}
\label{sec: molecular}

The model problems above separate the target and QT training signals on explicit targets and probe the term built from pushforward samples. Molecular systems add a singular nonbonded core, a mixed Euclidean--periodic domain, and more conformational degrees of freedom as molecular size grows. We first define the molecular measure and its regularized potential, then add sharpening to the staged generator and report the completed alkane family. The pending targets are classified below as achiral or as chiral with one fixed stereochemical configuration.

\subsection{Molecular potential models}
\label{sec: mol-models}

For Cartesian coordinates \(x\), let \(E(x)\) denote the complete molecular-mechanics energy
\begin{equation}
E(x)
=E_{\mathrm{bond}}+E_{\mathrm{angle}}+E_{\mathrm{torsion}}
 +E_{\mathrm{LJ}}+E_{\mathrm{Coul}}
 +E_{\mathrm{GB}}^{\mathrm{OBC1}}+E_{\mathrm{ACE}} .
\label{eq: molecular-energy}
\end{equation}
The bonded and vacuum nonbonded part has the Amber form \citep{cornell1995amber}
\begin{align}
E_{\mathrm{vac}}(x)
={}&\sum_{b} k_b(r_b-r_b^0)^2
 +\sum_{a} k_a(\theta_a-\theta_a^0)^2
 +\sum_{\tau}\frac{V_\tau}{2}
       \bigl[1+\cos(n_\tau\varphi_\tau-\gamma_\tau)\bigr] \notag\\
&+\sum_{\substack{i<j\\\mathrm{nonbonded}}}
 \left\lbrace 4\epsilon_{ij}\left[
 \left(\frac{\sigma_{ij}}{r_{ij}}\right)^{12}
 -\left(\frac{\sigma_{ij}}{r_{ij}}\right)^6\right]
 +k_{\mathrm e}\frac{q_iq_j}{r_{ij}}\right\rbrace ,
\label{eq: molecular-amber-energy}
\end{align}
Here $b$, $a$, and $\tau$ index bonds, angles, and proper or improper torsions, while $i,j$ index nonbonded atom pairs after topology-defined exclusions and scaling. The symbols $r_b^0$, $\theta_a^0$, $k_b$, $k_a$, $V_\tau$, $n_\tau$, and $\gamma_\tau$ are force-field equilibrium values and coefficients; $\sigma_{ij}$, $\epsilon_{ij}$, and $q_i$ are the nonbonded parameters. OBC1 supplies the generalized-Born electrostatic solvation term \citep{onufriev2004gb}, and ACE supplies the nonpolar term \citep{schaefer1996ace}. All terms in \eqref{eq: molecular-energy} belong to the sampled potential.

A nonlinear molecule with \(A\) atoms has \(d=3A-6\) internal degrees of freedom after rigid translation and rotation are removed. A fixed internal-coordinate tree reconstructs \(x=x(q)\) from whitened bond and angle coordinates and periodic torsions. Write $d_{\mathrm E}$ and $d_{\mathrm T}$ for the numbers of nonperiodic Euclidean coordinates and periodic torsions, respectively, and let $\mathbb T=\mathbb R/(2\pi\mathbb Z)$. Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal Q=\mathbb R^{d_{\mathrm E}}\times\mathbb T^{d_{\mathrm T}},
\qquad d_{\mathrm E}+d_{\mathrm T}=3A-6,
\label{eq: mixed-molecular-domain}
\end{equation}
and the physical reduced potential at inverse temperature \(\beta_{\mathrm T}=(k_BT)^{-1}\) is
\begin{equation}
U(q)=\beta_{\mathrm T} E(x(q))-\log J(q),
\label{eq: molecular-reduced-potential}
\end{equation}
where \(J(q)\) is the Cartesian volume Jacobian of the reconstruction, including the whitening maps. The source is Gaussian on the Euclidean block and uniform on the torus. The mixed spline flow uses ordinary rational-quadratic splines on the Euclidean coordinates and circular splines on the torsions.

The alkane family uses neutral all-atom bundles with GAFF2 parameters, AM1-BCC charges, graph-symmetry and neutrality projection, and \(\mathrm{mbondi2}\) radii. GAFF and AM1-BCC are described in \citet{wang2004gaff} and \citet{jakalian2002am1bcc}; the bundles are prepared with AmberTools \citep{case2023ambertools}. The two chiral alcohols use GAFF2/AM1-BCC with OBC1/ACE. Alanine dipeptide uses the 22-atom ACE--ALA--NME Hamiltonian of the FAB benchmark \citep{midgley2022flow}: Amber ff96, OBC1 with \(\mathrm{igb}=2\), \(\mathrm{mbondi2}\) radii, solute and solvent dielectric constants \(1\) and \(78.5\), and the \(\mathrm L\)-alanine component selected by the reference trajectory.

The alkanes, glycerol, neutral diethanolamine, and NMA have no fixed configurational stereocenter under these bundle definitions. Neutral diethanolamine retains both signs of its nitrogen-pyramidal coordinate, and the NMA amide torsion remains periodic so that both cis and trans conformations belong to its target. The chiral targets retain only the named configurations: \(S\) for 2-butanol, \((2R,3R)\) for 2,3-butanediol, and \(\mathrm L\)-alanine for alanine dipeptide. All conformational basins within each selected configuration remain part of the corresponding target. These support conventions are part of the target rather than an after-the-fact filter.

The Lennard--Jones core in \eqref{eq: molecular-amber-energy} grows as \(r^{-12}\), so early flow proposals can place a few samples at energies and gradients far beyond the scale of the thermally occupied region. We therefore use a two-parameter molecular surrogate. Let \(x_\star\) be the bundle reference geometry. After flooring only the regular and exception Coulomb/Lennard--Jones pair distances at \(r\), denote the complete force-field energy by \(E_r(x)\), its reference value by \(E_r^\star:=E_r(x_\star)\), and its reference-relative value by \(\Delta E_r(x):=E_r(x)-E_r^\star\). For an energy threshold \(e\), define the soft cap
\begin{equation}
\mathcal C_e(\Delta E)=
\begin{cases}
\Delta E, & \Delta E\Le e,\\[2pt]
e\bigl[1+\log(\Delta E/e)\bigr], & \Delta E>e,
\end{cases}
\label{eq: molecular-regularizer}
\end{equation}
and collect the two regularization parameters in \(\rho=(e,r)\). The corresponding reduced potential is
\begin{equation}
U^{\{\rho\}}(q)
=\beta_{\mathrm T}\!\left[E_r^\star
+\mathcal C_e\bigl(\Delta E_r(x(q))\bigr)\right]-\log J(q).
\label{eq: regularized-molecular-potential}
\end{equation}
The threshold \(e\) is measured in \(\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), and the pair floor \(r\) is measured in \(\mathrm{nm}\). The component formulas of the bonded and OBC1/ACE terms are unchanged before the scalar cap is applied to the complete energy. The coordinate Jacobian and domain are not capped. The physical potential \(U\) in \eqref{eq: molecular-reduced-potential} is recovered in the formal limit \(e\to\infty\), \(r\to0\). Thus \(U^{\{\rho\}}\) is a sampling bridge, not optimizer clipping or a replacement for the physical energy used in the observables.

\subsection{Boltzmann generators with sharpening}
\label{sec: mol-sharpening}

A softer molecular potential can make early stages easier in a given adaptive-staging run. After each flow stage, \emph{sharpening} reweights the complete validation set toward a stricter regularization setting, resamples it, and applies MALA under the stricter potential. This is a stochastic potential update, not an additional flow-training loss. To extend the source-to-target interpolation of Section~\ref{sec: boltzmann}, let $\rho_0=(e_0,r_0)$ and $\rho_1=(e_1,r_1)$ be the soft and final settings and interpolate them by
\begin{equation}
\rho_s=(1-s)\rho_0+s\rho_1,
\qquad s\in[0,1].
\label{eq: molecular-sharpening-path}
\end{equation}
The molecular experiments use the finite endpoint \(\rho_1=(100,0.15)\) and audit its residual difference from \(U\) directly. A fixed-regularization run has \(\rho_0=\rho_1\). Extending the Section~\ref{sec: boltzmann} bridge by the single index \(s\), set
\begin{equation}
U_{t,s}(q)=(1-t)U_0(q)+tU^{\{\rho_s\}}(q),
\qquad
\mu_{t,s}\propto\exp(-U_{t,s}).
\label{eq: molecular-two-coordinate-bridge}
\end{equation}
Thus \(t\) remains the source-to-target interpolation parameter defined in Section~\ref{sec: boltzmann}, while \(s\) changes only the regularization setting. If \(s\) is fixed, suppressing the second index recovers the notation \(U_t,\mu_t\) of Section~\ref{sec: boltzmann}.

Accepted sample sets lie on the diagonal $s=t$, but one stage advances the two coordinates in sequence:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal Y_{k-1}\approx\mu_{t_{k-1},t_{k-1}}
\ \xrightarrow[
    \text{selected }(\tilde{\mathcal Y}_k,w_k)
]{\ \text{flow or identity}\ }\
\mathcal Y_k^-\approx\mu_{t_k,t_{k-1}}
\ \xrightarrow[
    w_k^{\mathrm{sharp}}
]{\ \text{sharpen}\ }\
\mathcal Y_k\approx\mu_{t_k,t_k}.
\label{eq: molecular-stage-arrows}
\end{equation}
The first arrow holds $s=t_{k-1}$ fixed and advances $t$ from $t_{k-1}$ to $t_k$. Potential-space SMC and mixed-domain MALA carry a copy of all $N$ particles along this bridge. The resulting sample set is held fixed and subsampled during flow training; its smallest incremental ESS also screens the proposed $t_k$. In KLXX, a second sample set is built by applying QT under $U_{t_k,t_{k-1}}$ to $N$ fresh source draws. Thus the molecular implementation uses a fixed SMC target set and a fresh-source QT set, rather than the per-stage target surrogate and validation-seeded QT in Section~\ref{sec: boltzmann}.

After training, the candidate flow and identity proposals are evaluated on the complete carried set $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$. The pair with larger ESS over the complete validation set is denoted by $(\tilde{\mathcal Y}_k,w_k)$; ties retain the identity. Reweighting, resampling, and MALA under $U_{t_k,t_{k-1}}$ produce $\mathcal Y_k^-$. The second arrow holds $t=t_k$ fixed and advances the regularization coordinate from $t_{k-1}$ to $t_k$. Its exact log weight is
\begin{equation}
\log w_k^{\mathrm{sharp}}(q)
=U_{t_k,t_{k-1}}(q)-U_{t_k,t_k}(q)
=t_k\!\left[U^{\{\rho_{t_{k-1}}\}}(q)
-U^{\{\rho_{t_k}\}}(q)\right].
\label{eq: molecular-sharpening-weight}
\end{equation}
Sharpening is accepted only when its ESS over the complete validation set clears the stage threshold. The samples are then resampled and rejuvenated by MALA under $U_{t_k,t_k}$. A failed proposal or sharpening gate shrinks $t_k-t_{k-1}$ and retries. The selected map advances the source-to-target bridge; the stochastic sharpening step changes the molecular potential.

We compare three generators. ID performs both stochastic arrows without training a flow. KL trains the forward KL and then selects the trained or identity proposal. KLXX uses the same selection rule with log-ratio variation. All three methods retain the same SMC selection, reweighting, resampling, and MALA steps. Consistently with \eqref{eq: propagation-factor}, the molecular propagation factor is
\begin{equation}
F
=\prod_{k=1}^{K}
\ESS(w_k)^{-1}
\ESS\!\left(w_k^{\mathrm{sharp}}\right)^{-1},
\label{eq: molecular-propagation-factor}
\end{equation}
where \(w_k^{\mathrm{sharp}}\equiv1\) on a fixed regularization path. As in \eqref{eq: propagation-factor}, $F$ summarizes incremental weight degeneracy; it is not a fresh source-to-endpoint ESS or an endpoint error estimator.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec: conclusions}

Forward KL is mass-covering for exact distributions, but its sampled loss sees only the target regions supplied to it. This gap can produce a misleading ESS, transfer surrogate error to the flow, and leave target-poor proposal regions weakly constrained. Log-ratio variation addresses these failures by comparing the exact log-density ratio at pairs of samples. The sampling weight determines which regions are compared: target samples measure density variation on reached target regions, QT samples add candidate modes, and pushforward samples expose regions occupied by the proposal. KLXX combines these comparisons while retaining the exact target as a minimizer and the flow as a tractable importance proposal.

The theory establishes a narrower statement. For the selected Fisher--Rao subgradient flow of forward KL with $\X_\mu$, the regularizer adds a nonpositive term to the instantaneous KL dissipation. For a fixed smooth surrogate and $\lambda<\frac12$, it also improves the stationary-error bound by a nonnegative discrepancy term. This does not prove finite-step behavior for full KLXX, Adam, or the experimental value $\lambda=1$.

The numerical results provide the remaining evidence. The 2D and $\phi^4$ ablations are consistent with the roles of the target, QT, and pushforward samples; the product test shows higher validation ESS for every tested objective with log-ratio variation across the tested dimensions. On the shared clock stage schedules, KLXX has higher validation ESS on all $28$ reported stages and a smaller propagation factor at every reported batch size. In the single-seed alkane runs, KLXX has the smallest factor in every matched setting, while forward KL approaches the identity baseline as molecule size grows. The sharpening paths have lower KLXX factors for the larger alkanes. Selected energy, size, and rotamer summaries agree with independent physical-potential simulations. These findings are consistent with an explanation based on additional support information; they are not a theorem of universal dominance or a fixed-compute comparison.

The clock and alkane configurations require additional training seeds to measure run-to-run variation. The finite molecular endpoint has nearly uniform endpoint-to-physical weights on the reported sample sets, but a continuation to $U$ would remove the remaining regularization. The molecular targets marked pending in Table~\ref{tab: molecular-targets} still require generator runs and matched references; the alanine study will also permit a direct comparison with FAB \citep{midgley2022flow}. Larger molecules and explicit solvent remain outside the present results.

\section{Fisher--Rao gradient flow with log-ratio variation}
\label{sec: appendix-FR}

Section~\ref{sec: FR} states the main gradient-flow results used in the paper. This appendix supplies their derivations and proofs on the space of densities. We view $\nu$ as a generic probability density evolving along the gradient flow of the loss under an appropriate Riemannian metric on the space of densities \citep{ambrosio2008gradient,villani2009optimal} and ask how fast $\nu_t$ converges to the target $\mu$ in $\KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$.

The absolute value in $\X_\mu$ is not differentiable when two log-ratios coincide. We set $\operatorname{sgn}(0)=0$, selecting the zero subgradient at a tie, and study the corresponding formal Fisher--Rao subgradient flow. The densities below are assumed positive and regular enough for the displayed calculations.

In this section we restrict to the loss with only the forward KL and the shape regularizer $\X_\mu$, the log-ratio variation at weight $\omega = \mu$,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal L[\nu] = \KL(\mu\|\nu) + \lambda  \X_\mu(\mu\|\nu) = \int_{\mathbb R^d} \mu(x) \log \frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} \D x +
    \lambda\iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}
     \mu(x)\mu(y) \biggl|\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} - \log\frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)}\biggr| \D x \D y,
    \label{eq: appendix-loss-KL-X}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda>0$ is fixed. The calculations below hold along a positive solution of the selected flow; when $\lambda\Le\tfrac12$, the birth term in \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-L-flow} is nonnegative and preserves positivity.
Throughout this section we abbreviate the importance weight by
\begin{equation*}
    w(x) := \frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)},
\end{equation*}
so that the log-ratio is $z(x) = \log w(x) = \log\mu(x) - \log\nu(x)$, the field $z$ of \eqref{eq: logratio-z}. The importance weight $w$ is distinct from the localization measure $\omega$ in $\X_\omega$.

The choice of Riemannian metric matters. In the Wasserstein-2 geometry, induced by optimal transport \citep{jordan1998variational,otto2001geometry}, the forward KL fails to be displacement convex along Wasserstein-2 geodesics, and the standard Otto--Villani/Bakry--{\'E}mery argument \citep{otto2000generalization,bakry2014analysis} used for the reverse KL does not apply. We therefore work in the Fisher--Rao (FR) geometry \citep{amari2016information,bauer2016uniqueness}. Along the selected positive flow, the forward KL has an exponential bound and $\X_\mu$ contributes a nonlocal birth--death term \citep{lu2019accelerating}: the update at $x$ depends on how its importance weight ranks among weights under $\mu$.

Denoting the $\nu$-mean by $\langle \cdot \rangle_\nu := \int( \cdot ) \nu \D x$, the Fisher--Rao gradient of a functional $\mathcal F[\nu]$ and the associated dissipation identity along the FR gradient flow $\partial_t\nu_t = -\mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal F(\nu_t)$ are
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal F(\nu) = \nu\biggl(\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu} - \Big\langle\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu}\Big\rangle_\nu\biggr),
    \qquad
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\mathcal F[\nu_t] = -\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\biggl(\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu} - \Big\langle\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu}\Big\rangle_{\nu_t}\biggr)^{2}\nu_t \D x.
    \label{eq: appendix-FR-gradient}
\end{equation}
The mean-subtraction enforces $\int\mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal F = 0$, so the FR flow conserves probability mass.

\subsection{Derivation of \texorpdfstring{$\X_\mu$}{X-mu}-regularized Fisher--Rao gradient flow}
\label{subsec: appendix-FR-grad-calc}

The first variation of the forward KL with respect to $\nu$ is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\delta \KL(\mu\|\nu)}{\delta \nu}(x) = -\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} = -w(x),
    \label{eq: appendix-var-KL}
\end{equation}
so by \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-gradient}, using $\langle w \rangle_\nu = \int_{\mathbb R^d} \mu \,\D x = 1$, the FR gradient of the forward KL is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\big[\KL(\mu\|\nu)\big](x) = \nu(x) - \mu(x).
    \label{eq: appendix-FR-grad-KL}
\end{equation}

For $\X_\mu$, the selected subvariation applies $\operatorname{sgn}(0)=0$ to the absolute value. At an evaluation point $r$, the variation of the inner log-ratio difference is
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\delta}{\delta\nu(r)}
    \bigl[\log w(x)-\log w(y)\bigr]
    =-\frac{\delta(r-x)}{\nu(x)}
    +\frac{\delta(r-y)}{\nu(y)}.
\end{equation*}
Here $\delta$ is the Dirac distribution, so integration against $\delta(r-x)$ evaluates the remaining integrand at $x=r$.
Applying the selected sign subgradient and then exchanging $x$ and $y$ in the second term gives
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\delta \X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)}{\delta \nu}(r)
    & = \iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}
    \mu(x)\mu(y)\operatorname{sgn}\bigl(w(x)-w(y)\bigr)
    \left[-\frac{\delta(r-x)}{\nu(x)}
    +\frac{\delta(r-y)}{\nu(y)}\right]\D x\D y \\
    & = -2w(r)\int_{\mathbb R^d}
    \mu(y)\operatorname{sgn}\bigl(w(r)-w(y)\bigr)\D y.
\end{align*}
Hence the selected subvariation is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\delta \X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)}{\delta \nu}(x) = - 2w(x)S(x),
    \qquad
    S(x) := \mathbb E_{y\sim\mu}\bigl[\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr].
    \label{eq: appendix-var-X}
\end{equation}
We call $S$ the \emph{rank field} of the importance weight under $\mu$: $S(x) > 0$ indicates that $w(x)$ is comparatively large within the distribution of weights, and hence that $\nu$ pointwise undercovers $\mu$ relative to that distribution; $S(x) < 0$ has the opposite interpretation, and $S \equiv 0$ at $\nu = \mu$. By antisymmetry,
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbb E_\mu[S] = \mathbb E_{x,y\sim \mu}\bigl[\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr] = 0,
\end{equation*}
and the selected FR subgradient of $\X_\mu$ with respect to $\nu$ is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\big[\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)\big](x) = -2\mu(x) S(x).
    \label{eq: appendix-FR-grad-X}
\end{equation}

The selected FR subgradient of $\mathcal L[\nu]=\KL(\mu\|\nu)+\lambda\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)$ is the sum of \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-grad-KL} and \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-grad-X}:
\begin{equation*}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}[\mathcal L](x) = \nu(x) - \mu(x) - 2\lambda \mu(x) S(x).
\end{equation*}
The selected Fisher--Rao subgradient flow therefore reads
\begin{equation}
    \partial_t\nu_t(x) = \mu(x) - \nu_t(x) + 2\lambda \mu(x) S_t(x), \quad
    \text{where~}
    S_t(x) := \mathbb E_{y\sim\mu}\biggl[\operatorname{sgn}\biggl(\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu_t(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu_t(y)}\biggr)\biggr].
    \label{eq: appendix-FR-L-flow}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq: appendix-FR-L-flow} is a \emph{nonlocal} PDE: its right-hand side at $x$ depends on $S_t(x)$, which in turn requires the entire profile of the importance weight $\mu/\nu_t$ over the support of $\mu$. Mass is conserved along the flow because
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\nu_t(x) \D x = 2\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\mu(x) S_t(x) \D x = 0,
\end{equation*}
and the flow is stationary at $\nu_t = \mu$, where $\mu(x)/\nu_t(x) \equiv 1$ and hence $S_t\equiv 0$. When $\lambda = 0$, \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-L-flow} reduces to the pure Fisher--Rao gradient flow of the forward KL.

\subsection{Convergence analysis of the \texorpdfstring{$\X_\mu$}{X-mu}-regularized flow}
\label{subsec: appendix-FR-convergence}

To analyze the convergence rate of the regularized flow \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-L-flow}, we take the forward KL $\KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$ as the error metric and differentiate it along the flow:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\KL(\mu\|\nu_t) = -\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\frac{\mu^2}{\nu_t}(1 + 2\lambda S_t) \D x + \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\mu \D x = -\chi^2(\mu\|\nu_t) - 2\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\frac{\mu^2}{\nu_t} S_t \D x,
\end{equation*}
where the chi-squared divergence is
\begin{equation*}
    \chi^2(\mu\|\nu) := \int_{\mathbb R^d}(w-1)^2\nu\D x = \int_{\mathbb R^d} w^2\nu\D x - 1.
\end{equation*}
The sign of the second integral is settled by the following rank-correlation identity.

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem: appendix-rank-correlation}
Let $\mu$ and $\nu$ be positive probability densities for which the displayed integral is finite. Then
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu(x)^2}{\nu(x)}S(x)\D x = \frac{1}{2} \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\bigl[|w(x) - w(y)|\bigr] \Ge 0,
\end{equation*}
with equality if and only if $w$ is $\mu$-almost-everywhere constant.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $\mu^2/\nu = \mu w$, the definition of $S$ gives
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu^2}{\nu}S\D x = \mathbb E_\mu\bigl[w(x)S(x)\bigr] = \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\bigl[w(x)\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr].
\end{equation*}
Symmetrizing in $x\leftrightarrow y$ gives
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu^2}{\nu}S\D x = \frac{1}{2} \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\bigl[(w(x) - w(y))\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr] = \frac{1}{2} \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\bigl[ |w(x) - w(y)| \bigr].
\end{equation*}
\end{proof}

Applying Lemma~\ref{lem: appendix-rank-correlation} with $\nu = \nu_t$ and $S = S_t$, the dissipation rewrites as
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\KL(\mu\|\nu_t) = -\chi^2(\mu\|\nu_t) - \lambda \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\!\left[ \left|\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu_t(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu_t(y)}\right| \right].
    \label{eq: appendix-combined-rate}
\end{equation}
The elementary inequality $w\log w \Le w^2 - w$ applied to $w = \mu/\nu_t$ gives $\chi^2(\mu\|\nu_t) \Ge \KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$, hence $\frac{\D}{\D t}\KL(\mu\|\nu_t) \Le -\KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$, and Gr\"onwall's lemma yields the exponential decay
\begin{equation}
    \KL(\mu\|\nu_t) \Le e^{-t} \KL(\mu\|\nu_0),\qquad t \Ge 0,
    \label{eq: appendix-KL-exponential}
\end{equation}
with no structural assumption on $\mu$: neither log-concavity, nor a spectral gap, nor any growth condition. This contrasts with the Wasserstein-2 geometry, where the forward KL is not displacement convex and an analogous exponential rate is unavailable without further assumptions.

The uniform convergence rate \eqref{eq: appendix-KL-exponential} should \emph{not} be read as a blanket statement that minimizing the forward KL is intrinsically faster or more efficient than minimizing the reverse KL. The FR flow is a continuous-time idealization on the space of densities and does not account for sampling cost. In practice, evaluating the forward KL and its gradient requires samples from $\mu$, and producing representative samples in high dimensions is itself difficult. The empirical KLXX objective therefore also evaluates the exact log-ratio on the quench and temper measure $\hat\mu$, which is intended to supply support information that may be absent from the target-sample measure. The bound above quantifies convergence given exact target expectations; it does not establish the behavior of this additional empirical term or remove the cost of constructing its samples.

A naive comparison of dissipation rates between the unregularized $\KL$ loss and $\KL + \lambda \X_\mu$ would also be misleading, since scaling the loss by a constant is mere time reparametrization with no qualitative gain. The substantive contribution of $\X_\mu$ is structural rather than rate-based: the pure forward KL term depends locally on the value of $\mu(x)/\nu_t(x)$, whereas the rank field $S_t$ compares that value with the complete distribution of importance weights under $\mu$. The regularized flow \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-L-flow} therefore couples the update at $x$ to global rank information that the local forward KL term does not contain.

Two further cautions concern the discrete realization of the flow. First, the time-reparametrization argument cuts in both directions once the optimizer enters. Training follows Adam steps, not the FR flow: the per-coordinate second-moment normalization absorbs a global rescaling of the loss, so a larger gradient buys no speedup by itself, while a regularizer that reshapes the relative magnitudes of the gradient components does change the optimizer's effective time scale. Part of any observed difference in training speed between $\KL$ and $\KL + \lambda \X_\mu$ may therefore be optimizer scaling rather than information geometry, and we claim no universal transient speedup on this basis. Second, the dissipation \eqref{eq: appendix-combined-rate} is a density-space statement, while each gradient step uses a finite, generally biased sample estimate. The staged clock experiment examines this finite-batch setting with a schedule-matched comparison: its quantitative claims use the selected-proposal ESS over the complete validation set at each stage, not a composed endpoint estimate or a diagnostic computed on a training mini-batch.

\begin{remark}[Monotone decay of $\X_\mu$]
The regularizer $\X_\mu$ also decreases monotonically along the selected regularized flow: pairing the selected subvariation $\delta\X_\mu/\delta\nu = -2wS$ \eqref{eq: appendix-var-X} against the flow \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-L-flow} gives
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu_t) = -2\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu^2}{\nu_t} S_t \D x - 4\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu^2}{\nu_t} S_t^2 \D x  \Le  0.
\end{equation*}
The first term is nonpositive by Lemma~\ref{lem: appendix-rank-correlation} and vanishes only when $\mu/\nu_t$ is $\mu$-almost-everywhere constant. Hence the inequality is strict away from that case, and $\X_\mu$ is driven to zero along the flow.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Asymptotic accuracy for a fixed biased surrogate}
\label{subsec: appendix-accuracy}

The convergence analysis of Section~\ref{subsec: appendix-FR-convergence} takes the outer expectation in $\KL(\mu\|\nu)+\lambda\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)$ under the exact target. To isolate surrogate bias, fix a smooth density $\tilde\mu$ that does not vary with $\nu$ during this analysis and define
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{\mathcal L}[\nu]
    = \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\log w(x)\D x
    + \lambda\!\iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\tilde\mu(y)\bigl|\log w(x) - \log w(y)\bigr|\D x\D y,
    \label{eq: appendix-surrogate-loss}
\end{equation}
where the pointwise ratio $w(x)=\mu(x)/\nu(x)$ is still evaluated against the exact target. Repeating the selected-subgradient calculation of Section~\ref{subsec: appendix-FR-grad-calc} with $\mu$ replaced by $\tilde\mu$ only in the outer integrals gives
\begin{equation*}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\bigl[\widetilde{\mathcal L}\bigr](x) = \nu(x) - \tilde\mu(x)\bigl(1 + 2\lambda \widetilde S(x)\bigr),
    \qquad
    \widetilde S(x) := \mathbb E_{y\sim\tilde\mu}\bigl[\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr].
\end{equation*}
A stationary density $\nu^\star$ of this selected flow therefore satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \nu^\star(x) = \tilde\mu(x)\biggl[1 + 2\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\tilde\mu(y)\operatorname{sgn}\biggl(\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu^\star(y)}\biggr)\D y\biggr].
    \label{eq: appendix-surrogate-stationary}
\end{equation}
At $\lambda=0$, the equation reduces to $\nu^\star=\tilde\mu$ and the residual error is $\KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu)$. For $\lambda>0$, the rank correction $1+2\lambda\widetilde S^\star$ raises $\nu^\star$ relative to $\tilde\mu$ where $\mu/\nu^\star$ is comparatively high and lowers it where the ratio is low. For $\lambda<\frac12$, the bracket in \eqref{eq: appendix-surrogate-stationary} is positive because $\widetilde S^\star\in[-1,1]$. Antisymmetry of the sign also gives
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\mathbb R^d} \tilde\mu(x)\, \widetilde S^\star(x)\, \D x
    = \mathbb E_{x, y \sim \tilde\mu}\biggl[\operatorname{sgn}\biggl(\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu^\star(y)}\biggr)\biggr] = 0,
\end{equation*}
hence every stationary point $\nu^\star = \tilde\mu(1 + 2\lambda \widetilde S^\star)$ is a probability density.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm: appendix-accuracy}
Assume that $\mu$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $\tilde\mu$ and that $\lambda \in [0, \frac12)$. Then every stationary density $\nu^\star$ of the selected subgradient flow for $\widetilde{\mathcal L}$ in \eqref{eq: appendix-surrogate-loss} satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \KL(\mu\|\nu^\star) \Le \KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu) - \lambda\!\iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\tilde\mu(y) \biggl|\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu^\star(y)}\biggr| \D x \D y.
    \label{eq: appendix-accuracy-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Substituting the stationary identity $\nu^\star = \tilde\mu(1+2\lambda\widetilde S^\star)$ into the difference of forward KLs and using $\mu = \nu^\star \cdot (\mu/\nu^\star) = \tilde\mu(1+2\lambda\widetilde S^\star)(\mu/\nu^\star)$,
\begin{equation*}
    \KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu) - \KL(\mu\|\nu^\star)
    = \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\mu(x)\log\bigl(1 + 2\lambda\widetilde S^\star(x)\bigr)\D x
    = \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x) \frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)}u(x)\log u(x)\D x,
\end{equation*}
where $u(x) := 1 + 2\lambda\widetilde S^\star(x) \Ge 1 - 2\lambda > 0$, since $\widetilde S^\star \in [-1, 1]$ (a $\tilde\mu$-average of signs) and $\lambda < \frac12$. The elementary inequality $u\log u \Ge u - 1$ for $u > 0$ then yields
\begin{equation*}
    \KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu) - \KL(\mu\|\nu^\star)
    \Ge 2\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x) \frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} \widetilde S^\star(x)\D x.
\end{equation*}
Applying the rank-correlation argument of Lemma~\ref{lem: appendix-rank-correlation} verbatim with $\mu$ replaced by $\tilde\mu$ in the outer measure,
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)}\widetilde S^\star(x)\D x = \frac{1}{2}\!\iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\tilde\mu(y) \biggl|\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu^\star(y)}\biggr|\D x\D y,
\end{equation*}
which gives \eqref{eq: appendix-accuracy-bound}.
\end{proof}

At $\lambda=0$, the bound is attained by $\nu^\star=\tilde\mu$. For $0<\lambda<\frac12$, the subtracted discrepancy is nonnegative and is positive unless $\mu/\nu^\star$ is constant on the support of $\tilde\mu$. Thus, within the theorem's assumptions, $\X_\mu$ improves the stationary-error bound for a fixed biased surrogate.

The threshold $\lambda<\frac12$ is a sufficient condition for strict positivity of $\nu^\star$. The experiments use $\lambda=1$ and do not extend the theorem beyond its stated range. The live surrogate also changes with the current flow and is represented by finite batches, so it is not the fixed smooth density assumed here.
