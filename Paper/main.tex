\begin{abstract}
Normalizing flow Boltzmann generators retain a tractable proposal density, but training with forward KL depends on target samples that may be biased or omit modes. A flow can then miss target mass while its observed importance weights give a high effective sample size. We introduce log-ratio variation, a pairwise regularizer of the exact target-to-model log-density ratio, and use it to define KLXX. Its three weighting measures provide signals for target fitting, candidate mode coverage, and leakage control without changing the exact-target population minimizer. We derive an exponential Fisher--Rao bound and a fixed-surrogate error bound for forward KL with the target-weighted term, then place KLXX in an adaptive Boltzmann generator with exact importance reweighting and sharpening of the molecular potential. In the reported model and molecular tests, KLXX improves coverage-aware or stagewise diagnostics, and selected molecular observables agree with independent simulations. Log-ratio variation thus supplies information that a sampled forward KL objective can omit.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec: intro}

Drawing samples from a target distribution $\mu \propto \exp(-U)$ on $\mathbb R^d$, known only up to its normalization constant, is a recurring problem in molecular simulation, Bayesian inference, and statistical physics. When $\mu$ is high-dimensional and multimodal, its mass splits among basins of $U$ separated by barriers. A local Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampler can remain in one basin over the available simulation time, leaving other basins unexplored \citep{metropolis1953equation,hastings1970monte}. Estimates from such a chain are biased toward the visited basin, with no direct account of the mass it did not reach.

Tempering and population methods address this problem by connecting a tractable distribution to $\mu$ through intermediate distributions. Parallel tempering (PT) and replica exchange run copies of the system at several temperatures and exchange neighboring configurations, so a basin reached at high temperature can enter the low-temperature population \citep{swendsen1986replica,geyer1991markov,hukushima1996exchange}. Annealed importance sampling (AIS) moves along a bridge of intermediate distributions while accumulating importance weights \citep{neal2001annealed}. Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) uses a similar bridge but resamples and rejuvenates a weighted particle population at each step \citep{doucet2001introduction,delmoral2006sequential}. Nested sampling instead rewrites the normalizing integral as a one-dimensional quadrature over a likelihood threshold \citep{skilling2006nested}.

Learned variants insert a map into a temperature ladder, tune the ladder endpoint, or use a trained flow to improve exchange between temperatures \citep{syed2022nonreversible,surjanovic2022variational,zhang2025accelerated}. These methods can improve finite-budget movement between basins, but their empirical output still depends on the regions reached by their particles; an unvisited basin cannot contribute to a weight or resampling step \citep{woodard2009conditions,chehab2024provable}. Our method uses this machinery but separates fitting from mode discovery. An annealing procedure approximates target expectations, while a separate quench-and-temper (QT) construction scatters source samples, quenches them toward low-energy regions, and rejuvenates them to provide a wider set of candidate modes. Section~\ref{sec: training} defines both components.

Boltzmann generators (BGs) provide a complementary route through a learned transport map \citep{noe2019boltzmann}. Let $\mu_0\propto\exp(-U_0)$ be an easily sampled reference distribution, usually Gaussian, and let $G:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^d$ be an invertible normalizing flow \citep{rezende2015variational,papamakarios2017masked,kingma2018glow}. We use the target-to-source convention $G_{\#}\mu\approx\mu_0$. The inverse map then sends independent source draws toward the target, and their distribution is $\nu:=(G^{-1})_{\#}\mu_0$. Invertibility gives the density of $\nu$ by change of variables. The importance ratio $\mu/\nu$, known up to a common normalizing factor, is therefore available for every generated sample without fitting another density or tracing a stochastic path. These weights enable importance-reweighted estimates and define the effective sample size (ESS), a diagnostic of density agreement on the support reached by $\nu$ \citep{liu2001monte,doucet2001introduction,martino2017rethinking}. A high ESS alone does not establish mode coverage, because a mode absent from the generated sample contributes no observed weight.

The choice of training objective then divides the field. The original BG \citep{noe2019boltzmann} and its variational-inference predecessor \citep{rezende2015variational} minimize the reverse Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence
\begin{equation}
    \text{(reverse)}\quad \KL(\nu\|\mu) = \int_{\mathbb R^d} \nu(x) \log \frac{\nu(x)}{\mu(x)} \D x,
    \label{eq: reverse KL}
\end{equation}
which needs samples only from $\nu$ and evaluations of $U$. Its empirical gradient is therefore accessible, but the divergence is mode-seeking \citep{wainwright2008graphical}: a mode absent from $\nu$ contributes no samples and hence no gradient. A flow can then collapse onto a subset of the target modes \citep{wu2020stochastic,felardos2023designing}. Existing approaches interleave MCMC kernels with deterministic layers, encode target symmetries in the architecture, reduce gradient variance near the optimum, or anneal from a smoother potential \citep{wu2020stochastic,kohler2020equivariant,vaitl2022gradients,schopmans2025temperature}. These changes can improve exploration, but their success still depends on whether the resulting sampler reaches each relevant mode.

The opposing objective minimizes the forward KL,
\begin{equation}
    \text{(forward)}\quad \KL(\mu\|\nu) = \int_{\mathbb R^d} \mu(x) \log \frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} \D x,
    \label{eq: forward KL}
\end{equation}
which is mass-covering at the population level: it diverges if $\nu$ vanishes where $\mu$ has positive mass \citep{wainwright2008graphical}. Its empirical form, however, needs samples from $\mu$, the distribution being learned. Existing work resolves this circularity in several ways. Maximum likelihood uses configurations from a reference thermodynamic state \citep{wirnsberger2020targeted}; adaptive flow samplers train on a running Markov chain and use the flow as a global proposal \citep{gabrie2022adaptive}; molecular flows can fit a molecular-dynamics trajectory \citep{klein2023equivariant}; and transferable architectures amortize coverage across related molecules \citep{klein2024transferable}. Persistent chains and coupled-target regression provide other approximations to the missing target expectation \citep{naesseth2020markovian,rehman2026regflow}. Each empirical loss can see only the regions represented by its sample source. Thus population-level mass coverage does not prevent empirical mode omission.

We keep the forward KL and address this sampling limitation directly. The annealing procedure supplies its target batch, and log-ratio variation reuses the exact target-to-model density ratio on that batch and on two additional sampling measures. One is the QT distribution introduced above; the other is a detached copy of the current model. This division lets the loss compare density ratios on reached target regions, candidate missing modes, and target-poor regions reached by the proposal. The precise functional is introduced below.

The closest line of work couples annealing and flow training so that each improves the other. Flow Annealed Importance Sampling Bootstrap (FAB) replaces the forward KL by the $\alpha$-divergence at $\alpha=2$ \citep{midgley2022flow}. Its integrand is proportional to $\mu^2/\nu$, and its population minimizer also minimizes the variance of the importance weights. FAB draws training points from AIS chains targeting this surrogate and reuses them through a prioritized replay buffer. Related methods insert learned flows into SMC stages, anneal toward a low-temperature target, add an inference-time SMC correction, or constrain geometric paths that can move mass between disconnected regions \citep{arbel2021annealed,matthews2022craft,schopmans2025temperature,tan2025sequential,vonklitzing2026constrained}.

Our construction differs in three respects. First, it retains the forward KL and adds a nonnegative regularizer that vanishes at the exact target, instead of changing the divergence. Second, its MCMC proposals use the target score $-\nabla U$ but no positional derivative of the flow density; the flow enters through its initial proposal and through log-ratio evaluations. Third, QT supplies a wide-coverage population separately from the annealing chain, and no replay buffer is used. These choices keep the terminal proposal density tractable and the sampler score-free in the flow. They also impose a separate QT cost and give up the stronger tail penalty of the order-$2$ R\'enyi objective. With enough annealing work, FAB may need no separate coverage construction. Remark~\ref{rem: fab} returns to this comparison after the sampler is defined.

The off-policy log-dispersion regularizer provides another close comparison \citep{schopmans2026ldr}. It controls a scalar spread of $\log(\mu/\nu)$ under an off-policy measure. Log-ratio variation instead uses pairwise differences; changing the weighting measure changes which regions and cross-regions are compared. This distinction produces the three training signals used by KLXX.

A separate line trains a stochastic process rather than a bijection. Controlled stochastic differential equations, denoising diffusions, and flow-matching models learn a drift or velocity that transports the reference toward $\mu$ \citep{zhang2022path,vargas2023denoising,berner2024optimal,nusken2021solving,richter2024improved}. Energy-only variants such as iterated denoising energy matching (iDEM) alternate a sampling loop with a score- or velocity-fitting loop and a replay buffer \citep{akhound2024iterated,woo2024iefm}. Learned nonequilibrium transport augments AIS with a trainable drift, and later work extends this approach to larger many-body systems \citep{albergo2024nets,havens2025adjoint,akhoundsadegh2025progressive}.

These stochastic transports can be more expressive than the invertible architectures used here. Their terminal density, however, often requires an auxiliary likelihood calculation, such as a per-sample ordinary-differential-equation solve \citep{he2025notrick}. We retain a bijection because the exact proposal density, importance ratio, ESS, and reweighting are central to both the objective and the final estimator. The two approaches make different trade-offs: stochastic dynamics offer architectural freedom, while invertibility preserves direct density evaluation. Table~\ref{tab: methods} summarizes this distinction. In that table, $\X$ denotes the log-ratio variation defined below, $\hat\mu$ is the QT coverage measure, and $\bar\nu$ is the detached current model.

\paragraph{What is new.}
The sampled forward KL has three gaps. First, a mode absent from its target surrogate contributes no training points. If the flow also misses that mode, the observed weights are conditioned on the reached support and can be nearly uniform. The ESS can then be close to one even though target mass is absent; we call this a misleading or \emph{fake ESS} \citep{noe2019boltzmann,midgley2022flow}. Second, a finite target surrogate contains sampling error, and the score-free annealing procedure used here is biased because its rejuvenation kernels preserve the final target rather than each intermediate distribution. A plain forward KL fit can inherit this error. Third, target samples provide little information in regions where the model places mass but the target density or the empirical target population is small. The proposal can therefore allocate excess mass between modes or in target-poor regions. These are distinct problems: improving density agreement on reached modes does not discover an absent mode or remove proposal leakage.

To expose all three gaps, define the log-density ratio
$z(x):=\log[\mu(x)/\nu(x)]$. The normalizing constant of $\mu$ is unknown, so $z$ is known only up to an additive constant; every difference $z(x)-z(y)$ is nevertheless exact. For a nonnegative, integrable weight $\omega$ on $\mathbb R^d$, define the \emph{log-ratio variation}
\begin{equation}
    \X_\omega(\mu\|\nu) = \iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d} \omega(x)\,\omega(y)
    \biggl|\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} - \log\frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)}\biggr| \D x \D y,
    \label{eq: X}
\end{equation}
which integrates both arguments against $\omega$. The same definition applies on a torus or a Euclidean--torus product, with the integrals taken against the corresponding standard reference volume. This functional measures how far $z$ is from a constant on the region sampled by $\omega$. It is nonnegative and vanishes when $z$ is constant there. In particular, it vanishes at $\nu=\mu$, so adding it to the exact forward KL preserves the target as a population minimizer.

We use three weights. The target weight $\omega=\mu$ makes $\X_\mu$ control variation of $z$ across target samples, whereas the forward KL controls only their mean. This term uses the same target batch as the forward KL. The wide-coverage weight $\omega=\hat\mu$ is represented by a QT population designed to include modes that the annealed target surrogate may miss. Its finite-sample frequencies determine how often regions enter the pairwise loss, so $\hat\mu$ is a coverage guide rather than an estimate of the physical mode weights. The model weight $\omega=\bar\nu$ is the current proposal distribution with its parameters held fixed while the loss is differentiated. It evaluates $z$ where the proposal places mass and is intended to limit leakage into regions poorly represented by target samples. The latter two roles are design mechanisms, not consequences of the Fisher--Rao theorem below. The combined loss is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}[\nu] = \KL(\mu\|\nu) + \lambda\, \X_\mu(\mu\|\nu) + \X_{\alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu}(\mu\|\nu),
    \label{eq: total-loss}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda\Ge 0$ controls the target-weighted term and, when the mixture is used, $\alpha,\beta\Ge0$ with $\alpha+\beta=1$. Expanding the product measure in the last term gives self-comparisons within $\hat\mu$ and $\bar\nu$ and a cross-comparison between them. The cross-comparison ties the log-ratio scale on QT configurations to that on model configurations; this information would be absent if the two variations were added separately. For the flow convention above, change of variables evaluates the same log-ratio as
\begin{equation}
    z(y) = U_0(G(y)) - U(y) - \log|\det J_G(y)| + C,
    \label{eq: logratio-z}
\end{equation}
where $J_G(y)$ is the Jacobian matrix of $G$ at $y$, and the unknown constant $C$ does not depend on $G$. Hence the loss in terms of the map is
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}[G]
    &= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \mu}\bigl[z(y)\bigr]}_{\KL(\mu\|\nu)}
     + \lambda \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \mu}\bigl[|z(y) - z(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)}
     + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu}\bigl[|z(y) - z(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_{\alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu}(\mu\|\nu)} .
    \label{eq: total-loss-G-intro}
\end{align}
The $\X_\mu$ term reuses the target batch and log-ratio evaluations of the forward KL. The mixture term evaluates one additional batch, split equally between QT and detached model samples at the default setting. We refer to the choice $\lambda=1$ and $\alpha=\beta=\frac12$ as KLXX; its full objective is $\KL$+$\X_\mu$+$\X_{(\hat\mu+\bar\nu)/2}$. Compared with a forward KL iteration at the same batch size, KLXX evaluates about twice as many samples and also pays the cost of constructing the QT population.

KLXX can improve on a sampled forward KL because it evaluates the same exact log-ratio on more informative supports. The target term measures density variation on reached target regions, QT contributes modes absent from the annealed surrogate, and the detached model term probes regions reached by the proposal. The theory below concerns only the target-weighted term, so it does not imply that KLXX must outperform forward KL in every problem. The experiments isolate the target and QT contributions; comparison with the equal mixture is consistent with, but does not isolate, the detached-model role.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
We make four contributions. First, we define log-ratio variation and combine its three weights in KLXX. Pairwise differences remove the unknown target normalizer, and the exact-target population loss retains $\nu=\mu$ as a minimizer. The target-weighted term reuses the forward KL batch, while the mixture supplies the mode and proposal supports absent from that batch.

Second, we analyze forward KL with $\X_\mu$ in Fisher--Rao geometry. The regularizer adds a nonpositive term to the instantaneous KL dissipation. For a fixed biased target surrogate and regularization strength below the stated threshold, it also improves the stationary-error bound by a nonnegative discrepancy term. These results do not cover the full KLXX loss, the changing empirical surrogate used in training, or discrete neural-network optimization.

Third, we build an adaptive Boltzmann generator whose MCMC transitions use the target score but no flow score or replay buffer. Each stage compares its trained proposal with the identity on the complete validation population, retains the proposal with higher ESS, and shrinks rejected temperature increments. For molecular potentials, a second continuation sharpens a regularized interaction core toward the reported endpoint.

Fourth, we isolate the target and QT weighting roles and probe the combined QT/detached-model term before using KLXX on staged lattice and molecular targets. ESS is paired with coverage or a problem-specific mode diagnostic, so weight uniformity is not treated as evidence of mode coverage. The clock comparisons use shared schedules, while the molecular ladders are method-specific and single-seed; the results support the proposed mechanism but do not establish universal or fixed-compute dominance over forward KL.

\section{Fisher--Rao gradient flow analysis}
\label{sec: FR}

Section~\ref{sec: intro} introduced the forward KL with log-ratio variation. Before turning it into a training algorithm in Section~\ref{sec: training}, we summarize its gradient-flow properties on the space of densities; Appendix~\ref{sec: appendix-FR} gives the full derivations and proofs. This continuous-time population idealization exposes the mechanism of the loss but does not imply a convergence rate for the discrete optimizer: we view $\nu$ as a probability density evolving along the gradient flow and ask how fast $\KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$ decays to zero.

For a tractable analysis we keep only the forward KL and the shape term $\X_\mu$, the log-ratio variation at weight $\omega = \mu$. The contributions weighted by $\hat\mu$ and $\bar\nu$ are left out: these fixed training measures do not enter the density-space idealization. The loss is then
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal L[\nu] = \KL(\mu\|\nu) + \lambda\,\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu),
    \label{eq: loss KL X}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda > 0$ fixed. Write $w=\mu/\nu$ for the importance weight and $z=\log w$ for the log-ratio. Because $\X_\mu$ contains an absolute value, we use the subgradient selection $\operatorname{sgn}(0)=0$. The statements below apply to positive solutions of the resulting Fisher--Rao subgradient flow; for $\lambda\Le\tfrac12$, its explicit birth term is nonnegative and preserves positivity.

The choice of Riemannian metric matters. In the Wasserstein-2 geometry the forward KL is not displacement convex, so the Otto--Villani/Bakry--{\'E}mery argument used for the reverse KL does not apply. We therefore work in the Fisher--Rao (FR) geometry. Along a positive FR solution, the forward KL has an exponential bound, and $\X_\mu$ adds a nonlocal birth--death term: the update at one point depends on how its importance weight ranks among weights under $\mu$. Writing the $\nu$-mean as $\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle_\nu := \int (\cdot)\,\nu\,\D x$, the FR gradient of a smooth functional $\mathcal F$ and the selected flow for $\mathcal L$ are
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal F(\nu) = \nu\Bigl(\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu} - \Bigl\langle\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu}\Bigr\rangle_\nu\Bigr),
    \qquad
    \partial_t\nu_t = -\,\mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal L(\nu_t),
    \label{eq: FR-gradient}
\end{equation}
and the mean-subtraction makes $\int \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal F = 0$, so the flow conserves probability mass.

Differentiating $\KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$ along this flow, and reducing the $\X_\mu$ term by a rank-correlation identity, gives the dissipation
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\KL(\mu\|\nu_t) = -\chi^2(\mu\|\nu_t) - \lambda\, \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\!\left[ \left|\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu_t(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu_t(y)}\right| \right],
    \label{eq: combined-rate}
\end{equation}
where $\chi^2(\mu\|\nu):=\int(\mu/\nu-1)^2\nu\,\D x$ is the chi-squared divergence. The regularizer contributes a nonpositive term to the instantaneous KL dissipation. Since $\chi^2(\mu\|\nu_t) \Ge \KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$, Gr\"onwall's lemma yields
\begin{equation}
    \KL(\mu\|\nu_t) \Le e^{-t}\,\KL(\mu\|\nu_0), \qquad t \Ge 0,
    \label{eq: KL-exponential}
\end{equation}
without assuming log-concavity, a spectral gap, or a growth condition on $\mu$. This population statement does not imply that forward KL or KLXX trains faster with a discrete optimizer. It also excludes the cost of producing target samples. The added term in \eqref{eq: combined-rate} describes how $\X_\mu$ changes the density flow, not a universal transient speedup.

The convergence result assumes that the outer expectation is taken under the exact target. To isolate surrogate bias, fix a smooth density $\tilde\mu$ and replace $\mu$ by $\tilde\mu$ only in the outer integrals while retaining the exact pointwise log-ratio. This defines
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\mathcal L}[\nu]
=\mathbb E_{x\sim\tilde\mu}\!\left[\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)}\right]
+\lambda\,\mathbb E_{x,y\sim\tilde\mu}\!\left[\left|\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)}-\log\frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)}\right|\right].
\label{eq: surrogate-loss-main}
\end{equation}
We assume that the densities are positive on their common domain and that the displayed KL, chi-squared, and pair integrals are finite. At $\lambda=0$, the stationary density is $\nu^\star=\tilde\mu$, so the residual error is $\KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu)$. The next result shows when $\X_\mu$ improves this stationary-error bound. It is an idealization of one detached surrogate, not a theorem for the changing empirical batches used by the trainer.
\begin{theorem}
\label{thm: accuracy}
Assume $\mu$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $\tilde\mu$ and $\lambda \in [0, \tfrac12)$. Then every stationary density $\nu^\star$ of the selected subgradient flow for the fixed-surrogate loss $\widetilde{\mathcal L}$ in \eqref{eq: surrogate-loss-main} satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \KL(\mu\|\nu^\star) \Le \KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu) - \lambda\!\iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\tilde\mu(y) \biggl|\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu^\star(y)}\biggr| \D x \D y.
    \label{eq: accuracy-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\noindent The subtracted term is nonnegative and is positive unless the displayed importance ratio is constant on the support of $\tilde\mu$. Within the theorem's assumptions, the rank feedback raises $\nu^\star$ in comparatively underweighted regions and lowers it elsewhere. The experiments use $\lambda=1$, outside the sufficient range $\lambda<\tfrac12$, so the theorem does not certify their finite-training behavior. Appendix~\ref{sec: appendix-FR} gives the variation, rank-correlation identity, and proofs. Section~\ref{sec: training} now supplies the target surrogate and QT population needed to evaluate the loss.

\section{Flow training with log-ratio variation}
\label{sec: training}

This section turns the abstract loss of the introduction into a training algorithm for the flow map $G$. Let $U_0$ and $U$ be the source and target potentials on $\mathbb R^d$, so that $\mu_0 \propto \exp(-U_0)$ is the samplable reference and $\mu \propto \exp(-U)$ is the target. We parameterize $G:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^d$ so that $G_{\#}\mu \approx \mu_0$ and write $\nu := (G^{-1})_{\#}\mu_0$ for the induced approximation to $\mu$. The loss itself evaluates only the native target-to-source map $G$ and its log-Jacobian; it does not explicitly involve $G^{-1}$. The inverse map is nevertheless used to initialize score-free annealing and to draw model samples. Both are inference-only evaluations performed without differentiating through $G^{-1}$. We first assemble the training loss and identify its sampling ingredients (Section~\ref{subsec: total-loss}), then supply them in turn: a score-free annealing approximation of $\mu$ (Section~\ref{subsec: ais}), the quench-and-temper construction of the wide-coverage measure $\hat\mu$ (Section~\ref{subsec: QT}), and the ESS and coverage diagnostics used to evaluate the trained flow (Section~\ref{subsec: eval}).

\subsection{Training loss with log-ratio variation}
\label{subsec: total-loss}

By change of variables, the model density is $\nu(y)=\mu_0(G(y))|\det J_G(y)|$, so every term is a functional of the scalar field $z(y)$ in \eqref{eq: logratio-z}. The forward KL is its mean under the target, $\KL(\mu\|\nu)=\mathbb E_{y\sim\mu}[z(y)]$. When $\omega$ is normalized, $\X_\omega$ is the mean absolute difference $\mathbb E_{y,y'\sim\omega}|z(y)-z(y')|$; for a general weight it is the double integral in \eqref{eq: X}. The map-level loss is
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}[G]
    &= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \mu}\bigl[z(y)\bigr]}_{\KL(\mu\|\nu)}
     + \lambda \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \mu}\bigl[|z(y) - z(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)}
     + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu}\bigl[|z(y) - z(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_{\alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu}(\mu\|\nu)} .
    \label{eq: total-loss-G}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq: total-loss-G} is the map form of \eqref{eq: total-loss}. The default $\lambda=1$ and $\alpha=\beta=\frac12$ is KLXX. The model distribution $\nu=(G^{-1})_{\#}\mu_0$ depends on the trainable parameters of $G$. The barred measure $\bar\nu$ is the same distribution frozen at the current parameters: samples are drawn through $G^{-1}$, but their sampling path is detached before the loss is differentiated.

The three sampling measures entering \eqref{eq: total-loss-G} address complementary failure modes of the bare forward KL:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{$\mu$: target accuracy.} The term $\X_\mu$ compares log-ratios within the target batch. Theorem~\ref{thm: accuracy} gives its population bound for $\lambda<\frac12$; the finite-training effect at $\lambda=1$ is tested empirically.
    \item \emph{$\hat\mu$: candidate mode coverage.} QT provides comparisons on basins represented in its wide-coverage population, including basins absent from the annealed batch. This supplies a training signal for those represented basins but does not guarantee that QT finds every mode.
    \item \emph{$\bar\nu$: proposal support.} Detached model samples evaluate the log-ratio where the current proposal places mass. The term is intended to reduce variation in target-poor regions; the experiments test whether this is accompanied by less visible leakage.
\end{itemize}
Adding these nonnegative terms to the exact-target forward KL preserves $\nu=\mu$ as a population minimizer. The terms change the sampled training signal without changing that reference solution.

\begin{remark}[Population theory and discrete training]
\label{rem: robustness-not-speed}
The exponential bound \eqref{eq: KL-exponential} concerns the idealized FR flow. It gives no speed or stability guarantee for Adam, and the biased-surrogate theorem does not cover the experimental value $\lambda=1$. The numerical sections therefore treat finite-training gains as empirical results. The clock experiment uses a schedule-matched comparison to separate the loss from the choice of bridge increments.
\end{remark}

The forward KL and $\X_\mu$ share one target batch: evaluate $\mathbf z = (z(y_1),\dots,z(y_B))$, take $\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf z)$ for the forward KL, and $\mathrm{mean}(|\mathbf z - \mathbf z[\sigma]|)$ for a random permutation $\sigma$. Thus $\X_\mu$ adds only a permutation and pairwise difference to the same differentiation graph. The equal-weight mixture evaluates one additional batch drawn from $(\hat\mu+\bar\nu)/2$ and applies the same randomized pairwise estimator.

The loss draws on three measures, of which only two must be built. The $\bar\nu$ batch is the raw pushforward $\bar y_i = G^{-1}(x_i)$ of source samples $x_i \sim \mu_0$, taken with the gradient through $G$ detached. This avoids differentiating through the sampled pushforward. The other two require construction: an approximation of the target $\mu$ for the forward KL and $\X_\mu$ terms, supplied by the annealing procedure of Section~\ref{subsec: ais}, and samples of the wide-coverage measure $\hat\mu$ for the mixture term, supplied by quench and temper (Section~\ref{subsec: QT}). With equal target and mixture batch sizes, $\KL$+$\X_\mu$+$\X_{(\hat\mu+\bar\nu)/2}$ evaluates about twice as many samples per optimizer iteration as forward KL. This does not imply twice the total training time: constructing $\hat\mu$ also applies quench and temper to the complete validation population and adds a problem- and stage-dependent cost. Inference uses the same flow architecture for both methods.

We use two fixed settings. The \emph{$\X_\mu$-only setting} has $\lambda=1$ and $\alpha=\beta=0$; it adds no sampling measure beyond the target batch. KLXX has $\lambda=1$ and $\alpha=\beta=\frac12$; its second batch is split equally between $\hat\mu$ and $\bar\nu$. We report the full loss as $\KL$+$\X_\mu$+$\X_{(\hat\mu+\bar\nu)/2}$ when it must be distinguished from the other ablations.

\subsection{Approximating \texorpdfstring{$\mu$}{mu} by score-free annealing}
\label{subsec: ais}

Evaluating the $\mu$-expectations in \eqref{eq: total-loss-G} requires samples from $\mu$, which are not directly available. We form a surrogate by drawing $y_1,\dots,y_B$ from the pushforward $\nu=(G^{-1})_{\#}\mu_0$ and reweighting them. In the theory, the normalized importance ratio is $w=\mu/\nu$. Computation uses the proportional weight
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde w(y)
    := \exp\Big( U_0(G(y)) - U(y) - \log|\det J_G(y)| \Big)
    \propto \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)},
    \qquad y \in \mathbb R^d,
    \label{eq: weight}
\end{equation}
where the common unknown factor cancels in resampling, normalized ESS, and every difference of log-ratios. Below, $w_i$ denotes either representative because these operations are scale invariant. A resampling step and a short Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm (MALA) simulation then diffuses the duplicates. In high dimensions, however, $\mu$ and $\nu$ can differ greatly, so the weights can collapse onto a few outliers. We temper the reweighting along the geometric path of $M+1$ distributions
\begin{equation}
    \pi_m(y) \propto \nu(y)^{1-\frac{m}{M}} \mu(y)^{\frac{m}{M}} \propto ((G^{-1})_{\#}\mu_0)(y)\, \widetilde w(y)^{\frac{m}{M}}, \qquad m = 0,1,\dots,M,
    \label{eq: pi-m}
\end{equation}
so that $\pi_0 = \nu$ and $\pi_M = \mu$. Each step reweights by only $\widetilde w(y)^{1/M}$, reducing the degeneracy of direct importance sampling.


\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\setstretch{1.2}
\caption{Score-free annealing from $(G^{-1})_{\#}\mu_0$ toward $\mu$}
\label{alg: AIS}
\KwInput{samples $y_1,\dots,y_B$ from $(G^{-1})_{\#}\mu_0$, ladder length $M$, intermediate distributions $\pi_m$ \eqref{eq: pi-m}}
\KwOutput{a biased sample approximation $y_1,\dots,y_B$ of $\mu$}

\For{$m = 1,\dots,M$}{
    \tcp{reweight along the geometric path}
    Compute the incremental weights on $y_1,\dots,y_B$, $\widetilde w(y_i)^{1/M}=\exp(z(y_i)/M)$ up to a common factor\;
    \tcp{resample}
    Resample from the weighted set, $y_1',\dots,y_B' \gets \mathrm{resample}\bigl(y_{1:B}, (\widetilde w(y_i)^{1/M})_{i=1}^B\bigr)$\;
    \tcp{rejuvenate under the target}
    Diffuse the resampled duplicates by a short MALA chain on $\mu$, $y_{1:B} \gets \mathrm{MALA}(y_{1:B}', U)$\;
}
\end{algorithm}
\mbox{} \\
An exact SMC sampler on the path \eqref{eq: pi-m} would rejuvenate at step $m$ with a Markov kernel invariant for $\pi_m$. Its score contains $\nabla_y\log\nu(y)$ and hence positional derivatives of the flow density, including $\nabla_y\log|\det J_G(y)|$. To keep the training sampler score-free in the flow, Algorithm~\ref{alg: AIS} instead applies MALA directly under $\mu$, using only $-\nabla U$ in its proposal. MALA preserves $\mu$, but not the intermediate $\pi_m$, so the output is a biased target surrogate rather than an exact AIS/SMC sample. The construction trades intermediate invariance for a sampler that moves resampled particles under the final target. Section~\ref{sec: FR} studies a fixed smooth surrogate as a population idealization; it does not model the changing empirical output of Algorithm~\ref{alg: AIS} exactly. Evaluation therefore uses fresh importance weights and coverage diagnostics.

\begin{remark}[Choosing the ladder length $M$]
\label{rem: ladder-length}
For a target close to the source, $M=1$ can suffice. When the change is distributed across many coordinates, the log-weight increment accumulates their contributions, so $M$ should grow with the effective dimension of the deformation. The choice $M=d$ is a conservative default when all $d$ coordinates change; a shorter ladder is appropriate when only a few directions differ.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Comparison with FAB]
\label{rem: fab}
FAB \citep{midgley2022flow} also couples AIS to flow training, but places the flow inside the chains. Its transitions target the surrogate $\mu^2/\nu$, whose score
\begin{equation*}
    \nabla_y \log \frac{\mu^2(y)}{\nu(y)} = -2\nabla U(y) - \nabla_y \log\nu(y)
\end{equation*}
carries the positional gradient of the pushforward density at every MCMC step. Training uses a replay buffer of past AIS samples with a staleness correction. Here every MALA chain targets $\mu$ with score $-\nabla U$; the flow enters only through the initial $G^{-1}$ proposal and evaluations of $z$. Each gradient step starts from a new source batch, so no replay buffer is kept. The fixed $N$-sample QT set of Section~\ref{subsec: QT} supplies separate coverage information. FAB instead relies on its AIS budget for exploration. The order-$2$ R\'enyi objective gives FAB a stronger tail-coverage incentive, and with sufficient AIS work it may need no separate coverage construction.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Constructing \texorpdfstring{$\hat\mu$}{mu-hat} by quench and temper}
\label{subsec: QT}

The mixture term needs samples from the wide-coverage measure $\hat\mu$. Algorithms~\ref{alg: QT} and~\ref{alg: boltzmann} use the staged full-population convention: $\mathcal Y=\{x_1,\dots,x_N\}$ is the complete validation population drawn from the source measure for the stage, and QT acts on all $N$ particles once per stage attempt. The resulting set is held fixed during optimization. At each training step a mini-batch of $B$ samples is drawn from it and refreshed by a short MALA chain before entering the $\X_\omega$ computation. Standalone one-flow ablations may instead report a separately sized QT population; this does not alter the two-size staged algorithm, whose only sample sizes are $N$ and $B$. Quench and temper (QT) is the map
\begin{equation*}
    \hat{\mathcal Y} = \mathrm{QT}(\mathcal Y,U)
\end{equation*}
from the complete $N$-particle validation population and the target potential $U$ to a wide-coverage set $\hat{\mathcal Y}=\{\hat x_1,\dots,\hat x_N\}$. Its empirical distribution defines $\hat\mu$. QT has three phases: diffusion (melt), optimization (quench), and MALA rejuvenation (temper).

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\setstretch{1.2}
\caption{Quench and temper (QT): wide-coverage construction}
\label{alg: QT}
\KwInput{complete validation population $\mathcal Y = \{x_1,\dots,x_N\}$, target potential $U$, melt scale $m_e$}
\KwOutput{wide-coverage set $\hat{\mathcal Y} = \{\hat x_1,\dots,\hat x_N\}$ forming $\hat\mu$, written $\hat{\mathcal Y} = \mathrm{QT}(\mathcal Y,U)$}
\tcp{diffusion (melt)}
Scatter the samples to a wider region of $\mathbb R^d$, $x_i \gets x_i + m_e\zeta_i$ with $\zeta_i \sim \mathcal N(0, I_d)$ i.i.d. and melt scale $m_e > 0$\;
\tcp{optimization (quench)}
Drive each sample toward a mode center of $\mu$ by L-BFGS on $U$, updating $x_1,\dots,x_N$\;
\tcp{rejuvenation (temper)}
Spread the samples around each mode, $\hat x_{1:N} \gets \mathrm{MALA}(x_{1:N}, U)$\;
\end{algorithm}
\mbox{}\\
The quench places samples near local minima of $U$, and the Gaussian scatter broadens the set of basins that can be reached. On a torus or Euclidean--torus product, periodic coordinates are interpreted modulo their period after the melt and after each MALA proposal. If the QT population contains a basin absent from the annealed target batch, $\X_{\hat\mu}$ supplies pairwise log-ratio evaluations on that basin. QT does not certify complete mode discovery.

A large melt scale $m_e$ can bias the QT set toward basins far from the input population. This is intentional: $\hat\mu$ is a coverage measure, not an estimate of target mode probabilities. Adding $\X_{\hat\mu}$ to the exact-target forward KL preserves $\nu=\mu$ as a population minimizer, while empirical frequencies in the finite QT set affect the optimization path.

\subsection{Importance sampling at evaluation: ESS and coverage}
\label{subsec: eval}

At evaluation time the trained flow $G$ supplies the model distribution $\nu=(G^{-1})_{\#}\mu_0$ and its tractable density $\nu(y)=\mu_0(G(y))|\det J_G(y)|$. The proportional importance weight $\widetilde w(y)$ in \eqref{eq: weight} can therefore be evaluated on any pushforward sample without fitting another density model. Draw $\mathbf y=(y_1,\dots,y_N)$ with $y_i=G^{-1}(x_i)$ and $x_i\sim\mu_0$ independently, and write $\mathbf w=(w_1,\dots,w_N)$ for any common scaling of the corresponding positive weights. These weights diagnose agreement on reached support and correct residual bias by reweighting or resampling, followed when desired by a short MALA refresh under $\mu$.

We assess this pipeline through two metrics: the effective sample size, which measures how uniform the importance weights are on the support reached by the flow, and the coverage, which checks whether the regions represented in the QT reference set have been reached.

The first metric is the effective sample size, normalized to $[0,1]$ by dividing the classical ESS by the sample count $N$. For the weight vector $\mathbf w$, define
\begin{equation}
    \ESS(\mathbf w) = \frac{\big(\sum_{i=1}^N w_i\big)^2}{N \sum_{i=1}^N w_i^2} \in [0,1].
\end{equation}
The upper bound follows from Cauchy--Schwarz and is attained when all weights are equal. The factor $N$ distinguishes this normalized ESS from the classical count $(\sum_iw_i)^2/\sum_iw_i^2\in[1,N]$ \citep{martino2017rethinking}. Multiplying all weights by a common positive constant leaves both definitions unchanged. A high ESS indicates uniform observed weights only on support reached by $\nu$; a target mode absent from the sample cannot lower it.

To detect such missing modes, we pair the ESS with the coverage metric of \citet{naeem2020reliable}. Let $\hat{\mathbf y} = (\hat y_1,\dots,\hat y_N) \in \mathbb R^{N\times d}$ be the samples of $\hat\mu$ produced by the quench and temper algorithm (Algorithm~\ref{alg: QT}). For each $1 \Le i \Le N$, the $\kappa$-nearest-neighbor radius of $\hat y_i$ (with $\kappa = 5$) is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{NND}_\kappa(\hat y_i;\hat{\mathbf y}) = \inf\Big\{ r \Ge 0 :
    \# \big\{1\Le j \Le N: 0 < |\hat y_i - \hat y_j| < r\big\} \Ge \kappa
    \Big\},
\end{equation}
i.e., the distance from $\hat y_i$ to its $\kappa$-th nearest neighbor in $\hat{\mathbf y}$. The coverage of $\hat{\mathbf y}$ by the pushforward samples $\mathbf y$ is then
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Coverage}_\kappa(\mathbf y;\hat{\mathbf y}) =
    \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf 1\Bigl\{
        \exists  1\Le j\Le N : |y_j - \hat y_i| \Le \mathrm{NND}_\kappa(\hat y_i;\hat{\mathbf y})
    \Bigr\},
\end{equation}
i.e., the fraction of points in $\hat{\mathbf y}$ whose $\kappa$-nearest-neighbor ball contains at least one pushforward sample. A coverage close to $1$ indicates that the finite QT reference set is well covered by the flow, while a coverage well below $1$ exposes missing regions that the ESS alone cannot see. Because $\hat\mu$ is the same wide-coverage measure that $\X_{\hat\mu}$ trains toward, this diagnostic checks only the modes found by QT; it cannot reveal a mode absent from the reference set.

\section{Adaptive-temperature Boltzmann generator}
\label{sec: boltzmann}

Section~\ref{sec: training} described one flow between a source and target. When that change is too large for one map, we divide it into stages along a temperature ladder. Each stage changes the potential by a controlled amount and trains one flow with the loss in \eqref{eq: total-loss-G}. Sampling diagnostics determine the ladder.

\subsection{Construction of temperature ladder}

To bridge the simple source $\mu_0 \propto \exp(-U_0)$ to the target $\mu \propto \exp(-U)$, we seek an increasing sequence
\begin{equation*}
    0 = t_0 < t_1 < \cdots < t_K = 1
\end{equation*}
defining the interpolating potential and measure
\begin{equation*}
    U_t(x)=(1-t)U_0(x)+tU(x),
    \qquad \mu_t\propto\exp(-U_t),
    \qquad t\in[0,1].
\end{equation*}
At the accepted levels, write $\mu_k:=\mu_{t_k}$, so $\mu_0$ is the source and $\mu_K$ is the target. Stage $k$ trains a map $G_k$ with $(G_k^{-1})_{\#}\mu_{k-1}\approx\mu_k$. The number of stages $K$ is selected during the run. Its central choice is the increment $\Delta t_k=t_k-t_{k-1}$: a large increment can be hard to fit, whereas a small increment adds stages.

Training $G_k$ requires the target surrogate for $\mu_k$ defined in Section~\ref{subsec: ais}. Before training, an optional classical SMC calculation can screen the proposed increment $\Delta t_k$. Its smallest incremental ESS measures overlap along that candidate bridge for the current particle population. This is a difficulty diagnostic, not a mode-coverage guarantee. Setting its threshold to zero disables the screen.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
    \setstretch{1.2}
\caption{Adaptive selection of the temperature step $\Delta t_k$}
\label{alg: step}
\KwInput{potentials $U_0, U$, previous level $t_{k-1}$, validation population $\mathcal Y_{k-1} = \{y_1,\dots,y_N\}$ approximating $\mu_{k-1}$, SMC ladder length $M$, SMC ESS floor $\tau_{\mathrm{SMC}} = 0$, shrink factor $\gamma = 0.7$, initial guess $t_{\mathrm{init}}$}
\KwOutput{next level $t_{k}$, giving the step $\Delta t_k = t_k - t_{k-1}$}

$U_{t_{k-1}} \gets (1-t_{k-1}) U_0 + t_{k-1} U$\;
\tcp{start from the supplied initial guess}
$t_k \gets t_{\mathrm{init}}$\;
\If{$\tau_{\mathrm{SMC}} = 0$}{
    \Return $t_k$\;
}
\While{true}{
    \tcp{run classical SMC across the candidate step}
    Run $M$-step classical SMC on a working copy of all $N$ particles in $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$, using the linear potential bridge from $U_{t_{k-1}}$ to $U_{t_k}$ and a MALA kernel invariant for each intermediate distribution ($M$ as in Remark~\ref{rem: ladder-length})\;
    \tcp{score the step by its worst per-step ESS}
    Record $\ESS_{\min}$, the smallest per-step ESS over the $M$ steps\;
    \eIf{$\ESS_{\min} \Ge \tau_{\mathrm{SMC}}$}{
        \Return $t_k$\;
    }{
        \tcp{shrink the step toward $t_{k-1}$}
        $t_k \gets t_{k-1} + \gamma\,(t_k - t_{k-1})$\;
    }
}
\end{algorithm}
\mbox{}\\
When enabled, these SMC runs involve only the potentials $U_0$ and $U$, never the flow map. They use no QT samples, so their per-step ESS can overlook a mode the SMC population never reaches. We therefore use this ESS only to screen an increment; it is a heuristic measure of bridge difficulty, not a coverage certificate. The selected-proposal validation in step~(v) supplies the acceptance gate, while QT supplies the independent wide-coverage set. By default $\tau_{\mathrm{SMC}} = 0$, so Algorithm~\ref{alg: step} returns $t_{\mathrm{init}}$ without running SMC. For a positive floor, it returns the first candidate along the shrink sequence whose estimated ESS clears that floor. The shrink factor is $\gamma = 0.7$. As $t_k$ approaches $t_{k-1}$, the bridge becomes trivial and its population ESS approaches $1$; in finite samples we also impose the outer loop's retry and stage limits.

\subsection{The full training procedure}

Algorithm~\ref{alg: boltzmann} combines the ladder rule with the training procedure. It carries a fixed-size validation population $\mathcal Y_k\approx\mu_k$ across stages. Each candidate stage builds its QT set, trains a flow, and compares that flow with the identity proposal. If neither proposal reaches the validation ESS floor $\tau_{\mathrm v}$, the algorithm shrinks the increment and retries. It stops at $t_K=1$.

\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item \emph{Source population.} Stage $k$ uses two sample sizes: the \emph{validation size} $N$ and the \emph{batch size} $B$. The complete population $\mathcal Y_{k-1}=\{y_1,\dots,y_N\}\approx\mu_{k-1}$ is carried over from the previous stage. A working copy of all $N$ particles supplies the SMC calculation in step~(ii), and all $N$ particles initialize QT in step~(iii). Neither operation first draws a separately sized subset. Training batches are drawn at run time, $B$ samples per gradient step, from $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$; no separate training set is built.

\item \emph{Adaptive step.} Algorithm~\ref{alg: step} selects $t_k$. The first stage starts from $t_{\mathrm{init}}=t_{\mathrm{safe}}=0.2$. Later stages extrapolate the last accepted increment, $t_{\mathrm{init}}=\min\bigl(1,t_{k-1}+\Gamma(t_{k-1}-t_{k-2})\bigr)$, with enlarge factor $\Gamma=1.5$. The default $\tau_{\mathrm{SMC}}=0$ accepts this initial guess without the optional SMC screen. If a positive floor is supplied, the algorithm shrinks the increment until the classical SMC ESS on all $N$ particles clears that floor. The selected stage target is $\mu_k\propto\exp(-U_{t_k})$, with $U_{t_k}=(1-t_k)U_0+t_kU$.

\item \emph{Wide-coverage set.} Quench and temper (Algorithm~\ref{alg: QT}) acts on the complete validation population $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$ under $U_{t_k}$. The empirical distribution of the resulting $N$ samples $\hat{\mathcal Y}_k=\mathrm{QT}(\mathcal Y_{k-1},U_{t_k})$ defines $\hat\mu_k$ and supplies the wide-coverage half of the mixture term.

\item \emph{Training loss.} The flow $G_k$ is trained by $\KL$+$\X_{\mu_k}$+$\X_{(\hat\mu_k+\bar\nu_k)/2}$, the default loss of Section~\ref{subsec: total-loss} specialized to the stage $\mu_{k-1}\to\mu_k$. With model $\nu_k = (G_k^{-1})_{\#}\mu_{k-1}$ and its stop-gradient copy $\bar\nu_k$, the stage log-ratio is
\begin{equation*}
    z_k(y) = U_{t_{k-1}}(G_k(y)) - U_{t_k}(y) - \log|\det J_{G_k}(y)| ,
\end{equation*}
and the loss reads
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}[G_k]
    &= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \mu_k}\bigl[z_k(y)\bigr]}_{\KL(\mu_k\|\nu_k)}
     + \lambda\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \mu_k}\bigl[|z_k(y) - z_k(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_{\mu_k}(\mu_k\|\nu_k)}
     + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim (\hat\mu_k + \bar\nu_k)/2}\bigl[|z_k(y) - z_k(y')|\bigr]}_{\X_{(\hat\mu_k+\bar\nu_k)/2}(\mu_k\|\nu_k)} ,
    \label{eq: stage-loss}
\end{align}
where the omitted stage normalizing constant is independent of $G_k$. Its $\mu_k$-expectations are estimated from the biased score-free annealing surrogate: the level-$(k-1)$ batch $\mathcal X$ is pushed to $\bar{\mathcal X}_\nu = G_k^{-1}(\mathcal X)$, distributed as $\nu_k$, and annealed toward $\mu_k$ by Algorithm~\ref{alg: AIS} with $G = G_k$. The wide-coverage measure $\hat\mu_k$ is realized by the set $\hat{\mathcal Y}_k$ of step~(iii).

\item \emph{Validation set update.} Once the candidate $G_k$ is trained, each $y \in \mathcal Y_{k-1}$ is pushed through its inverse to $\tilde y = G_k^{-1}(y)$. This gives the trained proposal population $\tilde{\mathcal Y}_k^{\mathrm{tr}} = G_k^{-1}(\mathcal Y_{k-1})$ from $\nu_k = (G_k^{-1})_{\#}\mu_{k-1}$, together with the importance weights
\begin{equation*}
    w_k^{\mathrm{tr}}(\tilde y) := \exp\bigl(z_k(\tilde y)\bigr),
    \qquad \tilde y \in \tilde{\mathcal Y}_k^{\mathrm{tr}} .
\end{equation*}
The identity proposal keeps $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$ unchanged and has weights $w_k^{\mathrm{id}}(y)=\exp[U_{t_{k-1}}(y)-U_{t_k}(y)]$. We select the pair with the larger ESS over the complete validation population and denote it by $(\tilde{\mathcal Y}_k,w_k)$, taking the stage map to be $G_k$ or the identity accordingly. If $\ESS(w_k) < \tau_{\mathrm v}$, with default $\tau_{\mathrm v} = 0.6$, we discard the candidate, shrink to $t_k \gets t_{k-1} + \gamma\,(t_k - t_{k-1})$, and repeat steps (iii)--(v) at the reduced step. Otherwise we advance the validation population to $\mathcal Y_k = \mathrm{MALA}\bigl(\mathrm{resample}(\tilde{\mathcal Y}_k, w_k;N),\, U_{t_k}\bigr) \approx \mu_k$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{algorithm}[p]
    \setstretch{1.15}
\caption{Adaptive-temperature Boltzmann generator training}
\label{alg: boltzmann}
\small
\KwInput{potentials $U_0, U$, validation size $N$, batch size $B$, annealing and SMC ladder length $M$, SMC ESS floor $\tau_{\mathrm{SMC}} = 0$, validation ESS floor $\tau_{\mathrm v} = 0.6$, shrink factor $\gamma = 0.7$, enlarge factor $\Gamma = 1.5$, safe start $t_{\mathrm{safe}} = 0.2$}
\KwOutput{ladder $0 = t_0 < \cdots < t_K = 1$ and flow maps $\{G_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$ with $(G_k^{-1})_{\#}\mu_{k-1} \approx \mu_k$}

\tcp{validation population, $\mathcal Y_k \approx \mu_k$}
$\mathcal Y_0 \gets N$ samples from $\mu_0$, \quad $t_0 \gets 0$, \quad $k \gets 0$\;
\While{$t_k < 1$}{
    $k \gets k + 1$\;
    Form the stage source potential, $U_{t_{k-1}} \gets (1-t_{k-1}) U_0 + t_{k-1} U$\;
    \tcp{(i) complete source population}
    Use all $N$ particles in $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$ for stage selection, QT, and validation\;
    \tcp{(ii) adaptive temperature selection}
    \eIf{$k=1$}{
        Set $t_{\mathrm{init}} = t_{\mathrm{safe}}$\;
    }
    {
        Set $t_{\mathrm{init}} = \min\big(1, t_{k-1} + \Gamma(t_{k-1} - t_{k-2})\big)$\;
    }
    Choose $t_k$ by adaptive selection (Algorithm~\ref{alg: step}) on $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$ with initial guess $t_{\mathrm{init}}$\;
    Form the target potential $U_{t_k} \gets (1-t_k) U_0 + t_k U$\;
    \Repeat{$\ESS(w_k) \Ge \tau_{\mathrm v}$}{
        Initialize $G_k$ to the identity map\;
        \tcp{(iii) wide-coverage set}
        Apply quench and temper to all $N$ validation particles, $\hat{\mathcal Y}_k \gets \mathrm{QT}(\mathcal Y_{k-1},U_{t_k})$ (Algorithm~\ref{alg: QT})\;
        \tcp{(iv) default log-ratio-variation loss}
        \For{each gradient step}{
            Draw a training batch of $B$ samples $\mathcal X$ from the validation set $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$\;
            Form the detached model batch, $\bar{\mathcal X}_\nu \gets G_k^{-1}(\mathcal X)$, distributed as $\nu_k = (G_k^{-1})_{\#}\, \mu_{k-1}$\;
            Form the biased target surrogate $\mathcal X_\mu$ by score-free annealing (Algorithm~\ref{alg: AIS}, from $\nu_k$ toward $\mu_k$)\;
            Draw a wide-coverage batch $\hat{\mathcal X}_\mu \subset \hat{\mathcal Y}_k$ and rejuvenate, $\hat{\mathcal X}_\mu \gets \mathrm{MALA}(\hat{\mathcal X}_\mu, U_{t_k})$\;
            Update $G_k$ by a gradient step on $\KL$+$\X_{\mu_k}$+$\X_{(\hat\mu_k+\bar\nu_k)/2}$ in \eqref{eq: stage-loss}, from $\mathcal X_\mu$ and the equal mixture of $\hat{\mathcal X}_\mu$ and $\bar{\mathcal X}_\nu$\;
        }
        \tcp{(v) validation set update}
        Form the trained pair $(\tilde{\mathcal Y}_k^{\mathrm{tr}},w_k^{\mathrm{tr}})$ and identity pair $(\mathcal Y_{k-1},w_k^{\mathrm{id}})$ as in step~(v)\;
        Select the pair with larger ESS and denote it by $(\tilde{\mathcal Y}_k,w_k)$; set $G_k$ to the corresponding trained or identity map\;
        \eIf{$\ESS(w_k) < \tau_{\mathrm v}$}{
            \tcp{abort the step and shrink toward $t_{k-1}$}
            $t_k \gets t_{k-1} + \gamma\,(t_k - t_{k-1})$, then re-form $U_{t_k} \gets (1-t_k) U_0 + t_k U$\;
        }{
            Retain the selected stage map $G_k$\;
            Resample $N$ particles, $\mathcal Y_k \gets \mathrm{resample}(\tilde{\mathcal Y}_k, w_k;N)$\;
            Employ MALA rejuvenation to obtain $\mathcal Y_k \gets \mathrm{MALA}(\mathcal Y_k, U_{t_k})$\;
        }
    }
}
$K \gets k$\;
\Return $\{t_k\}_{k=0}^{K}$ and $\{G_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$\;
\end{algorithm}
\mbox{}\\
The accepted ladder defines a Boltzmann generator for $\mu$. Starting with samples from $\mu_0$, stage $k$ applies the selected inverse map or identity, computes $w_k$, resamples, and rejuvenates under $U_{t_k}$. The resulting population becomes the source for stage $k+1$.

The accepted-stage weights also define the propagation factor used below:
\begin{equation}
    F
    := \prod_{k=1}^{K}\ESS(w_k)^{-1}.
    \label{eq: propagation-factor}
\end{equation}
Because normalized ESS lies in $(0,1]$, $F$ is at least one, with the ideal value one. It summarizes stagewise weight degeneracy by multiplying reciprocal incremental ESS values. It is neither the ESS of the complete composed sampler nor an estimator of endpoint Monte Carlo error. If a stage contains another accepted reweighting operation, such as molecular sharpening in Section~\ref{sec: molecular}, its reciprocal ESS enters the product.

% ===================== Section 5: model problems =====================
\section{Numerical experiments on model problems}
\label{sec: experiments}

We now test the roles assigned to the three weighting measures. The 2D targets compare target accuracy, QT-assisted mode coverage, and visible proposal leakage. The product multi-well tests how the ESS difference changes with dimension. The tilted $\phi^4$ model shows why ESS must be paired with a collective-mode diagnostic. The clock model then compares the full objective on shared annealing schedules. Accordingly, ESS is reported with coverage, minority-phase weight, or sector occupancy whenever mode omission is possible.

\subsection{2D benchmark distributions}
\label{subsec: 2d-benchmark}

We consider four 2D targets: Two-Moon, Three-Well, Himmelblau, and Sparse. Two-Moon has two distorted crescent components and isolates finite-batch accuracy because both components lie within reach of the source. The remaining targets isolate mode collapse: Three-Well contains a low-probability basin, Himmelblau has four separated wells, and Sparse places two tight modes far into the source tails.

For every target, we initialize the same neural spline flow at the identity and compare four objectives:
\begin{center}
$\KL$,\qquad
$\KL$+$\X_\mu$,\qquad
$\KL$+$\X_\mu$+$\X_{\hat\mu}$,\qquad
$\KL$+$\X_\mu$+$\X_{(\hat\mu+\bar\nu)/2}$.
\end{center}
The flow has $32$ spline bins, $6$ transforms, and conditioner widths $(128,128)$. Each training step constructs a target surrogate by one score-free annealing update through the current flow. The rejuvenation kernel uses $100$ MALA steps of size $10^{-3}$ for Two-Moon and $50$ MALA steps of size $2\times10^{-3}$ for the other targets. The fixed validation populations contain $5\times10^4$ points for Two-Moon and Himmelblau and $8\times10^4$ for Three-Well and Sparse. For both objectives containing the wide-coverage term $\X_{\hat\mu}$, quench and temper acts on the complete fixed validation population, with melt scale $2$ and an Armijo L-BFGS quench using initial step $0.5$ and $200$ iterations. The final ESS is computed from flow importance weights on that full population. Coverage, with $\kappa=5$ against an independently generated quench-and-temper reference set, measures the fraction of reference configurations represented by the pushforward through the nearest-neighbor test of Section~\ref{subsec: eval}. The reference sets contain $1000$, $4000$, $500$, and $4000$ points for Two-Moon, Three-Well, Himmelblau, and Sparse, respectively; their QT tempering uses $1000$ MALA steps at the target-specific step size above. Hence a collapsed flow can have ESS near one but cannot have full coverage. Each objective--target pair is one fixed-seed training realization, so the numerical differences below describe these matched runs rather than variation across independent trainings.

\subsection{High-dimensional product multi-well}
\label{subsec: highd}

We next isolate accuracy scaling over the sixteen dimensions
$d=16,32,\ldots,256$. Letting $k=\lfloor\log_2 d\rfloor$, we use
\begin{equation}
    U(\mathbf{x})=\frac12\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2
    +12\sum_{i=1}^{k}\exp(-x_i^2).
    \label{eq: highd-potential}
\end{equation}
The first $k$ coordinates are symmetric double wells with minima at $\pm\sqrt{\ln 24}$ and barrier approximately $9.9$, while the remaining $d-k$ coordinates are standard Gaussian. The target therefore has exactly $2^k$ equal-weight sign-pattern modes, where $2^k\Le d<2^{k+1}$. Multimodality occupies only a $k$-dimensional subspace, but the log-weight error accumulates across all $d$ coordinates.

All four objectives use the same identity-initialized neural spline flow on $[-4,4]^d$, with $16$ bins, $6$ transforms, and conditioner widths $(256,256)$. At every dimension, training uses batch size $250$, $2000$ Adam steps at learning rate $10^{-3}$, gradient clipping at $10^2$, and a fixed validation population of size $5000d$. The live target surrogate uses one score-free annealing update followed by $50$ MALA steps of size $2\times10^{-3}$. This is a standalone one-flow ablation, so its separately reported QT population contains $1000d$ particles rather than the complete validation population used by the staged algorithm. QT uses melt scale $2$ and an Armijo L-BFGS quench with initial step $0.5$ and $200$ iterations. Final validation ESS is evaluated on $\max(200d,80000)$ fresh source samples. Each objective--dimension pair is one fixed-seed training realization.

\subsection{Lattice \texorpdfstring{$\phi^4$}{phi4} field theory: ESS at a collective barrier}
\label{subsec: phi4}

The tilted scalar $\phi^4$ theory tests ESS at a collective barrier. On a periodic $L\times L$ lattice, with $d=L^2$ and magnetization $m(\boldsymbol\phi)=d^{-1}\sum_j\phi_j$, the action is
\begin{equation*}
U(\boldsymbol\phi)
=-0.8\sum_{\langle jl\rangle}\phi_j\phi_l
+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left[\phi_j^2+\frac12(\phi_j^2-1)^2\right]
+h\sum_{j=1}^{d}\phi_j.
\end{equation*}
We take $h=0.0257$ at $L=6$ and $h=0.0144$ at $L=8$. This size-dependent
field keeps the extensive tilt scale nearly fixed:
$hL^2=0.9252$ at $L=6$ and $hL^2=0.9216$ at $L=8$. Thus the two targets have
comparable phase imbalance despite their different volumes.
In the broken phase the field orders near two vacua $\pm v\mathbf 1$, where $v>0$ is the spontaneous field magnitude and $\mathbf 1\in\mathbb R^d$ is the all-ones vector. A collective domain-wall barrier separates the vacua. The positive linear term gives the two phases unequal probability, so the minority weight $p_+=\mathbb P(m>0)$ is not fixed by symmetry and must be learned.

Parallel tempering gives the references $p_+=0.1251\pm0.0009$ and $0.1284\pm0.0014$, statistically consistent with occupancies $0.1260$ and $0.1268$ from an independent mirror-MALA construction. That construction samples the positive vacuum of the symmetric $h=0$ action, obtains the negative vacuum by the exact reflection $\boldsymbol\phi\mapsto-\boldsymbol\phi$, reweights the pair by the field term, and resamples one million configurations. Each loss is run at seeds $0,1,2$ using an identity-initialized neural spline flow on $[-3,3]^d$, with $16$ bins, $6$ transforms, and conditioner widths $(256,256)$. The source is $\mathcal N(0,0.25I_d)$, centered on the barrier. Training uses a fixed validation population of $10^5$ source samples, batch size $500$, $2000$ Adam steps at learning rate $10^{-3}$, and gradient clipping at $10^3$. The live surrogate uses one score-free annealing update followed by $50$ MALA steps of size $2\times10^{-3}$. For both objectives containing $\X_{\hat\mu}$, quench and temper acts on the complete validation population, with melt scale $2$ and an Armijo L-BFGS quench using initial step $0.1$ and $200$ iterations.

\subsection{Boltzmann generator on \texorpdfstring{$p$}{p}-state clock model}
\label{subsec: clock}

The preceding benchmarks use a single flow. We now test the full annealed construction on the $p=6$ clock model on a periodic $8\times8$ lattice,
\begin{equation}
U(\boldsymbol\theta)
=-\sum_{\langle jl\rangle}\cos(\theta_j-\theta_l)
-\frac12\sum_{j=1}^{d}\cos(6\theta_j),
\qquad \boldsymbol\theta\in[-\pi,\pi)^{d},\quad d=64,
\label{eq: clock-potential}
\end{equation}
where $\langle jl\rangle$ runs over nearest-neighbor pairs. The source is uniform on the torus and the bridge is $U_t=tU$. At low temperature, the target separates into six symmetry-related clock sectors.

Each stage uses an identity-initialized neural circular spline flow with $16$ bins, $6$ autoregressive transforms, and conditioner widths $(256,256)$. The validation set contains $4\times10^5$ particles, and both SMC and quench and temper act directly on this complete population. Every stage uses $3000$ Adam steps at learning rate $10^{-3}$, a four-level score-free annealing surrogate with $100$ MALA steps of size $10^{-3}$, energy clipping at $10^3$, and gradient clipping at $10^2$. For KLXX, QT uses wrapped-Gaussian melt scale $2\pi$ and an Armijo L-BFGS quench with initial step $10^{-2}$ and $100$ iterations. The clock ladder starts from $t_{\mathrm{safe}}=0.25$, uses enlarge factor $\Gamma=2$, and applies the nondefault gates $\tau_{\mathrm{SMC}}=0.7$ and $\tau_{\mathrm v}=0.4$. We report batch sizes $B=2000,1000,500,250$. An additional completed run lies below this reporting cutoff and is excluded from the table and all aggregate clock claims.

The comparison is schedule-matched. Forward KL first constructs an adaptive ladder using SMC preselection and an ESS acceptance gate. The loss $\KL$+$\X_\mu$+$\X_{(\hat\mu+\bar\nu)/2}$ is then trained on the identical accepted values of $t$, without a second gate. Hence the two methods see the same bridge increments within each batch-size pair. Every configuration is a single run, so individual stage values carry single-run fluctuation.

The principal metric is the selected-proposal validation ESS of each accepted increment. The propagation factor in \eqref{eq: propagation-factor} summarizes stagewise weight degeneracy across the ladder; smaller is better. It is not a full-chain ESS or an endpoint error estimate. Table~\ref{tab: clock-summary} reports every accepted stage and the resulting factor.

\section{Boltzmann generators on molecular potential energies}
\label{sec: molecular}

The model problems above separate the target and QT training signals on explicit targets and probe the combined detached-model term. Molecular systems add a singular nonbonded core, a mixed Euclidean--periodic domain, and more conformational degrees of freedom as molecular size grows. We first define the molecular measure and its regularized potential, then add sharpening to the staged generator and report the completed alkane family. Glycerol, neutral diethanolamine, and alanine dipeptide remain pending.

\subsection{Molecular potential models}
\label{sec: mol-models}

For Cartesian coordinates \(x\), let \(E(x)\) denote the complete molecular-mechanics energy
\begin{equation}
E(x)
=E_{\mathrm{bond}}+E_{\mathrm{angle}}+E_{\mathrm{torsion}}
 +E_{\mathrm{LJ}}+E_{\mathrm{Coul}}
 +E_{\mathrm{GB}}^{\mathrm{OBC1}}+E_{\mathrm{ACE}} .
\label{eq: molecular-energy}
\end{equation}
The bonded and vacuum nonbonded part has the Amber form \citep{cornell1995amber}
\begin{align}
E_{\mathrm{vac}}(x)
={}&\sum_{b} k_b(r_b-r_b^0)^2
 +\sum_{a} k_a(\theta_a-\theta_a^0)^2
 +\sum_{\tau}\frac{V_\tau}{2}
       \bigl[1+\cos(n_\tau\varphi_\tau-\gamma_\tau)\bigr] \notag\\
&+\sum_{\substack{i<j\\\mathrm{nonbonded}}}
 \left\lbrace 4\epsilon_{ij}\left[
 \left(\frac{\sigma_{ij}}{r_{ij}}\right)^{12}
 -\left(\frac{\sigma_{ij}}{r_{ij}}\right)^6\right]
 +k_{\mathrm e}\frac{q_iq_j}{r_{ij}}\right\rbrace ,
\label{eq: molecular-amber-energy}
\end{align}
Here $b$, $a$, and $\tau$ index bonds, angles, and proper or improper torsions, while $i,j$ index nonbonded atom pairs after topology-defined exclusions and scaling. The symbols $r_b^0$, $\theta_a^0$, $k_b$, $k_a$, $V_\tau$, $n_\tau$, and $\gamma_\tau$ are force-field equilibrium values and coefficients; $\sigma_{ij}$, $\epsilon_{ij}$, and $q_i$ are the nonbonded parameters. OBC1 supplies the generalized-Born electrostatic solvation term \citep{onufriev2004gb}, and ACE supplies the nonpolar term \citep{schaefer1996ace}. All terms in \eqref{eq: molecular-energy} belong to the sampled potential.

A nonlinear molecule with \(A\) atoms has \(d=3A-6\) internal degrees of freedom after rigid translation and rotation are removed. A fixed internal-coordinate tree reconstructs \(x=x(q)\) from whitened bond and angle coordinates and periodic torsions. Write $d_{\mathrm E}$ and $d_{\mathrm T}$ for the numbers of nonperiodic Euclidean coordinates and periodic torsions, respectively, and let $\mathbb T=\mathbb R/(2\pi\mathbb Z)$. Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal Q=\mathbb R^{d_{\mathrm E}}\times\mathbb T^{d_{\mathrm T}},
\qquad d_{\mathrm E}+d_{\mathrm T}=3A-6,
\label{eq: mixed-molecular-domain}
\end{equation}
and the physical reduced potential at inverse temperature \(\beta=(k_BT)^{-1}\) is
\begin{equation}
U(q)=\beta E(x(q))-\log J(q),
\label{eq: molecular-reduced-potential}
\end{equation}
where \(J(q)\) is the Cartesian volume Jacobian of the reconstruction, including the whitening maps. The source is Gaussian on the Euclidean block and uniform on the torus. The mixed spline flow uses ordinary rational-quadratic splines on the Euclidean coordinates and circular splines on the torsions.

The alkane family uses neutral all-atom bundles with GAFF2 parameters, AM1-BCC charges, graph-symmetry and neutrality projection, and \(\mathrm{mbondi2}\) radii. GAFF and AM1-BCC are described in \citet{wang2004gaff} and \citet{jakalian2002am1bcc}; the bundles are prepared with AmberTools \citep{case2023ambertools}. OBC1/ACE is used across the homologous series, and the independent references are propagated with OpenMM \citep{eastman2017openmm}. Alanine dipeptide uses the 22-atom ACE--ALA--NME Hamiltonian of the FAB benchmark \citep{midgley2022flow}: Amber ff96, OBC1 with \(\mathrm{igb}=2\), \(\mathrm{mbondi2}\) radii, solute and solvent dielectric constants \(1\) and \(78.5\), and the \(\mathrm L\)-alanine component selected by the reference trajectory. Glycerol has no configurational stereocenter, while neutral diethanolamine retains both signs of its nitrogen-pyramidal coordinate. These support conventions are part of the target rather than an after-the-fact filter.

The Lennard--Jones core in \eqref{eq: molecular-amber-energy} grows as \(r^{-12}\), so early flow proposals can place a few samples at energies and gradients far beyond the scale of the thermally occupied region. We therefore use a two-parameter molecular surrogate. Let \(x_\star\) be the bundle reference geometry. After flooring only the regular and exception Coulomb/Lennard--Jones pair distances at \(r\), denote the complete force-field energy by \(E_r(x)\), its reference value by \(E_r^\star:=E_r(x_\star)\), and its reference-relative value by \(\Delta E_r(x):=E_r(x)-E_r^\star\). For an energy threshold \(e\), define the soft cap
\begin{equation}
\mathcal C_e(\Delta E)=
\begin{cases}
\Delta E, & \Delta E\Le e,\\[2pt]
e\bigl[1+\log(\Delta E/e)\bigr], & \Delta E>e,
\end{cases}
\label{eq: molecular-regularizer}
\end{equation}
and collect the two regularization parameters in \(\rho=(e,r)\). The corresponding reduced potential is
\begin{equation}
U^{\{\rho\}}(q)
=\beta\!\left[E_r^\star
+\mathcal C_e\bigl(\Delta E_r(x(q))\bigr)\right]-\log J(q).
\label{eq: regularized-molecular-potential}
\end{equation}
The threshold \(e\) is measured in \(\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), and the pair floor \(r\) is measured in \(\mathrm{nm}\). The component formulas of the bonded and OBC1/ACE terms are unchanged before the scalar cap is applied to the complete energy. The coordinate Jacobian and domain are not capped. The physical potential \(U\) in \eqref{eq: molecular-reduced-potential} is recovered in the formal limit \(e\to\infty\), \(r\to0\). Thus \(U^{\{\rho\}}\) is a sampling bridge, not optimizer clipping or a replacement for the physical energy used in the observables.

\subsection{Boltzmann generators with sharpening}
\label{sec: mol-sharpening}

A softer molecular potential can make early stages easier in a given adaptive run. After each flow stage, \emph{sharpening} reweights the complete population toward a stricter regularization setting, resamples it, and applies MALA under the stricter potential. This is a stochastic potential update, not an additional flow-training loss. To write it with the temperature bridge of Section~\ref{sec: boltzmann}, let $\rho_0=(e_0,r_0)$ and $\rho_1=(e_1,r_1)$ be the soft and final settings and interpolate them by
\begin{equation}
\rho_s=(1-s)\rho_0+s\rho_1,
\qquad s\in[0,1].
\label{eq: molecular-sharpening-path}
\end{equation}
The molecular experiments use the finite endpoint \(\rho_1=(100,0.15)\) and audit its residual difference from \(U\) directly. A fixed-regularization run has \(\rho_0=\rho_1\). Extending the Section~\ref{sec: boltzmann} bridge by the single index \(s\), set
\begin{equation}
U_{t,s}(q)=(1-t)U_0(q)+tU^{\{\rho_s\}}(q),
\qquad
\mu_{t,s}\propto\exp(-U_{t,s}).
\label{eq: molecular-two-coordinate-bridge}
\end{equation}
Thus \(t\) retains its Section~\ref{sec: boltzmann} meaning, moving from the source to the molecular target, while \(s\) changes only the regularization setting. If \(s\) is fixed, suppressing the second index recovers the notation \(U_t,\mu_t\) of Section~\ref{sec: boltzmann}.

Accepted populations lie on the diagonal $s=t$, but one stage advances the two coordinates in sequence:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal Y_{k-1}\approx\mu_{t_{k-1},t_{k-1}}
\ \xrightarrow[
    \text{selected }(\tilde{\mathcal Y}_k,w_k)
]{\ \text{flow or identity}\ }\
\mathcal Y_k^-\approx\mu_{t_k,t_{k-1}}
\ \xrightarrow[
    w_k^{\mathrm{sharp}}
]{\ \text{sharpen}\ }\
\mathcal Y_k\approx\mu_{t_k,t_k}.
\label{eq: molecular-stage-arrows}
\end{equation}
The first arrow holds $s=t_{k-1}$ fixed and advances $t$ from $t_{k-1}$ to $t_k$. Potential-space SMC and mixed-domain MALA carry a copy of all $N$ particles along this bridge. The resulting population is held fixed and subsampled during flow training; its smallest incremental ESS also screens the proposed $t_k$. In KLXX, a second population is built by applying QT under $U_{t_k,t_{k-1}}$ to $N$ fresh source draws. Thus the molecular implementation uses a fixed SMC target population and a fresh-source QT population, rather than the per-step target surrogate and validation-seeded QT in Section~\ref{sec: boltzmann}.

After training, the candidate flow and identity proposals are evaluated on the complete carried population $\mathcal Y_{k-1}$. The pair with larger ESS over the complete validation population is denoted by $(\tilde{\mathcal Y}_k,w_k)$; ties retain the identity. Reweighting, resampling, and MALA under $U_{t_k,t_{k-1}}$ produce $\mathcal Y_k^-$. The second arrow holds $t=t_k$ fixed and advances the regularization coordinate from $t_{k-1}$ to $t_k$. Its exact log weight is
\begin{equation}
\log w_k^{\mathrm{sharp}}(q)
=U_{t_k,t_{k-1}}(q)-U_{t_k,t_k}(q)
=t_k\!\left[U^{\{\rho_{t_{k-1}}\}}(q)
-U^{\{\rho_{t_k}\}}(q)\right].
\label{eq: molecular-sharpening-weight}
\end{equation}
Sharpening is accepted only when its ESS over the complete validation population clears the stage threshold. The samples are then resampled and rejuvenated by MALA under $U_{t_k,t_k}$. A failed proposal or sharpening gate shrinks $t_k-t_{k-1}$ and retries. The selected map advances the source-to-target bridge; the stochastic sharpening step changes the molecular potential.

We compare three generators. ID performs both stochastic arrows without training a flow. KL trains the forward KL and then selects the trained or identity proposal. KLXX uses the same selection rule with log-ratio variation. All three methods retain the same SMC selection, reweighting, resampling, and MALA steps. Consistently with \eqref{eq: propagation-factor}, the molecular propagation factor is
\begin{equation}
F
=\prod_{k=1}^{K}
\ESS(w_k)^{-1}
\ESS\!\left(w_k^{\mathrm{sharp}}\right)^{-1},
\label{eq: molecular-propagation-factor}
\end{equation}
where \(w_k^{\mathrm{sharp}}\equiv1\) on a fixed regularization path. As in \eqref{eq: propagation-factor}, $F$ summarizes incremental weight degeneracy; it is not a fresh source-to-endpoint ESS or an endpoint error estimator.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec: conclusions}

Forward KL is mass-covering as a population objective, but its sampled loss sees only the target regions supplied to it. This gap can produce a misleading ESS, transfer surrogate error to the flow, and leave target-poor proposal regions weakly constrained. Log-ratio variation addresses these failures by comparing the exact log-density ratio at pairs of samples. The sampling weight determines which regions are compared: target samples measure density variation on reached target regions, QT samples add candidate modes, and detached model samples expose regions occupied by the proposal. KLXX combines these comparisons while retaining the exact target as a population minimizer and the flow as a tractable importance proposal.

The theory establishes a narrower statement. For the selected Fisher--Rao subgradient flow of forward KL with $\X_\mu$, the regularizer adds a nonpositive term to the instantaneous KL dissipation. For a fixed smooth surrogate and $\lambda<\frac12$, it also improves the stationary-error bound by a nonnegative discrepancy term. This does not prove finite-step behavior for full KLXX, Adam, or the experimental value $\lambda=1$.

The numerical results provide the remaining evidence. The 2D and $\phi^4$ ablations are consistent with the target, QT, and detached-model roles; the product test shows higher validation ESS for every tested objective with log-ratio variation across the tested dimensions. On the shared clock schedules, KLXX has higher validation ESS on all $28$ reported increments and a smaller propagation factor at every reported batch size. In the single-seed alkane runs, KLXX has the smallest factor in every matched setting, while forward KL approaches the identity baseline as molecule size grows. The sharpening paths have lower KLXX factors for the larger alkanes. Selected energy, size, and rotamer summaries agree with independent physical-potential simulations. These findings are consistent with an explanation based on additional support information; they are not a theorem of universal dominance or a fixed-compute comparison.

The clock and alkane configurations require additional training seeds to measure run-to-run variation. The finite molecular endpoint has nearly uniform endpoint-to-physical weights on the reported populations, but a continuation to $U$ would remove the remaining regularization. Glycerol, neutral diethanolamine, and alanine dipeptide still require generator runs and matched references; the alanine study will also permit a direct comparison with FAB \citep{midgley2022flow}. Larger molecules and explicit solvent remain outside the present results.

\section{Fisher--Rao gradient flow with log-ratio variation}
\label{sec: appendix-FR}

Section~\ref{sec: FR} states the main gradient-flow results used in the paper. This appendix supplies their derivations and proofs on the space of densities. We view $\nu$ as a generic probability density evolving along the gradient flow of the loss under an appropriate Riemannian metric on the space of densities \citep{ambrosio2008gradient,villani2009optimal} and ask how fast $\nu_t$ converges to the target $\mu$ in $\KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$.

The absolute value in $\X_\mu$ is not differentiable when two log-ratios coincide. We set $\operatorname{sgn}(0)=0$, selecting the zero subgradient at a tie, and study the corresponding formal Fisher--Rao subgradient flow. The densities below are assumed positive and regular enough for the displayed calculations.

In this section we restrict to the loss with only the forward KL and the shape regularizer $\X_\mu$, the log-ratio variation at weight $\omega = \mu$,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal L[\nu] = \KL(\mu\|\nu) + \lambda  \X_\mu(\mu\|\nu) = \int_{\mathbb R^d} \mu(x) \log \frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} \D x +
    \lambda\iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}
     \mu(x)\mu(y) \biggl|\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} - \log\frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)}\biggr| \D x \D y,
    \label{eq: appendix-loss-KL-X}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda>0$ is fixed. The calculations below hold along a positive solution of the selected flow; when $\lambda\Le\tfrac12$, the birth term in \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-L-flow} is nonnegative and preserves positivity.
Throughout this section we abbreviate the importance weight by
\begin{equation*}
    w(x) := \frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)},
\end{equation*}
so that the log-ratio is $z(x) = \log w(x) = \log\mu(x) - \log\nu(x)$, the field $z$ of \eqref{eq: logratio-z}. The importance weight $w$ is distinct from the localization measure $\omega$ in $\X_\omega$.

The choice of Riemannian metric matters. In the Wasserstein-2 geometry, induced by optimal transport \citep{jordan1998variational,otto2001geometry}, the forward KL fails to be displacement convex along Wasserstein-2 geodesics, and the standard Otto--Villani/Bakry--{\'E}mery argument \citep{otto2000generalization,bakry2014analysis} used for the reverse KL does not apply. We therefore work in the Fisher--Rao (FR) geometry \citep{amari2016information,bauer2016uniqueness}. Along the selected positive flow, the forward KL has an exponential bound and $\X_\mu$ contributes a nonlocal birth--death term \citep{lu2019accelerating}: the update at $x$ depends on how its importance weight ranks among weights under $\mu$.

Denoting the $\nu$-mean by $\langle \cdot \rangle_\nu := \int( \cdot ) \nu \D x$, the Fisher--Rao gradient of a functional $\mathcal F[\nu]$ and the associated dissipation identity along the FR gradient flow $\partial_t\nu_t = -\mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal F(\nu_t)$ are
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal F(\nu) = \nu\biggl(\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu} - \Big\langle\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu}\Big\rangle_\nu\biggr),
    \qquad
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\mathcal F[\nu_t] = -\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\biggl(\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu} - \Big\langle\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu}\Big\rangle_{\nu_t}\biggr)^{2}\nu_t \D x.
    \label{eq: appendix-FR-gradient}
\end{equation}
The mean-subtraction enforces $\int\mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal F = 0$, so the FR flow conserves probability mass.

\subsection{Derivation of \texorpdfstring{$\X_\mu$}{X-mu}-regularized Fisher--Rao gradient flow}
\label{subsec: appendix-FR-grad-calc}

The first variation of the forward KL with respect to $\nu$ is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\delta \KL(\mu\|\nu)}{\delta \nu}(x) = -\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} = -w(x),
    \label{eq: appendix-var-KL}
\end{equation}
so by \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-gradient}, using $\langle w \rangle_\nu = \int_{\mathbb R^d} \mu \,\D x = 1$, the FR gradient of the forward KL is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\big[\KL(\mu\|\nu)\big](x) = \nu(x) - \mu(x).
    \label{eq: appendix-FR-grad-KL}
\end{equation}

For $\X_\mu$, the selected subvariation applies $\operatorname{sgn}(0)=0$ to the absolute value. At an evaluation point $r$, the variation of the inner log-ratio difference is
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\delta}{\delta\nu(r)}
    \bigl[\log w(x)-\log w(y)\bigr]
    =-\frac{\delta(r-x)}{\nu(x)}
    +\frac{\delta(r-y)}{\nu(y)}.
\end{equation*}
Here $\delta$ is the Dirac distribution, so integration against $\delta(r-x)$ evaluates the remaining integrand at $x=r$.
Applying the selected sign subgradient and then exchanging $x$ and $y$ in the second term gives
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\delta \X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)}{\delta \nu}(r)
    & = \iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}
    \mu(x)\mu(y)\operatorname{sgn}\bigl(w(x)-w(y)\bigr)
    \left[-\frac{\delta(r-x)}{\nu(x)}
    +\frac{\delta(r-y)}{\nu(y)}\right]\D x\D y \\
    & = -2w(r)\int_{\mathbb R^d}
    \mu(y)\operatorname{sgn}\bigl(w(r)-w(y)\bigr)\D y.
\end{align*}
Hence the selected subvariation is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\delta \X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)}{\delta \nu}(x) = - 2w(x)S(x),
    \qquad
    S(x) := \mathbb E_{y\sim\mu}\bigl[\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr].
    \label{eq: appendix-var-X}
\end{equation}
We call $S$ the \emph{rank field} of the importance weight under $\mu$: $S(x) > 0$ indicates that $w(x)$ is comparatively large within the population of weights, and hence that $\nu$ pointwise undercovers $\mu$ relative to that population; $S(x) < 0$ has the opposite interpretation, and $S \equiv 0$ at $\nu = \mu$. By antisymmetry,
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbb E_\mu[S] = \mathbb E_{x,y\sim \mu}\bigl[\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr] = 0,
\end{equation*}
and the selected FR subgradient of $\X_\mu$ with respect to $\nu$ is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\big[\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)\big](x) = -2\mu(x) S(x).
    \label{eq: appendix-FR-grad-X}
\end{equation}

The selected FR subgradient of $\mathcal L[\nu]=\KL(\mu\|\nu)+\lambda\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)$ is the sum of \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-grad-KL} and \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-grad-X}:
\begin{equation*}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}[\mathcal L](x) = \nu(x) - \mu(x) - 2\lambda \mu(x) S(x).
\end{equation*}
The selected Fisher--Rao subgradient flow therefore reads
\begin{equation}
    \partial_t\nu_t(x) = \mu(x) - \nu_t(x) + 2\lambda \mu(x) S_t(x), \quad
    \text{where~}
    S_t(x) := \mathbb E_{y\sim\mu}\biggl[\operatorname{sgn}\biggl(\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu_t(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu_t(y)}\biggr)\biggr].
    \label{eq: appendix-FR-L-flow}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq: appendix-FR-L-flow} is a \emph{nonlocal} PDE: its right-hand side at $x$ depends on $S_t(x)$, which in turn requires the entire profile of the importance weight $\mu/\nu_t$ over the support of $\mu$. Mass is conserved along the flow because
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\nu_t(x) \D x = 2\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\mu(x) S_t(x) \D x = 0,
\end{equation*}
and the flow is stationary at $\nu_t = \mu$, where $\mu(x)/\nu_t(x) \equiv 1$ and hence $S_t\equiv 0$. When $\lambda = 0$, \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-L-flow} reduces to the pure Fisher--Rao gradient flow of the forward KL.

\subsection{Convergence analysis of the \texorpdfstring{$\X_\mu$}{X-mu}-regularized flow}
\label{subsec: appendix-FR-convergence}

To analyze the convergence rate of the regularized flow \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-L-flow}, we take the forward KL $\KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$ as the error metric and differentiate it along the flow:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\KL(\mu\|\nu_t) = -\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\frac{\mu^2}{\nu_t}(1 + 2\lambda S_t) \D x + \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\mu \D x = -\chi^2(\mu\|\nu_t) - 2\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\frac{\mu^2}{\nu_t} S_t \D x,
\end{equation*}
where the chi-squared divergence is
\begin{equation*}
    \chi^2(\mu\|\nu) := \int_{\mathbb R^d}(w-1)^2\nu\D x = \int_{\mathbb R^d} w^2\nu\D x - 1.
\end{equation*}
The sign of the second integral is settled by the following rank-correlation identity.

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem: appendix-rank-correlation}
Let $\mu$ and $\nu$ be positive probability densities for which the displayed integral is finite. Then
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu(x)^2}{\nu(x)}S(x)\D x = \frac{1}{2} \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\bigl[|w(x) - w(y)|\bigr] \Ge 0,
\end{equation*}
with equality if and only if $w$ is $\mu$-almost-everywhere constant.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $\mu^2/\nu = \mu w$, the definition of $S$ gives
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu^2}{\nu}S\D x = \mathbb E_\mu\bigl[w(x)S(x)\bigr] = \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\bigl[w(x)\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr].
\end{equation*}
Symmetrizing in $x\leftrightarrow y$ gives
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu^2}{\nu}S\D x = \frac{1}{2} \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\bigl[(w(x) - w(y))\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr] = \frac{1}{2} \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\bigl[ |w(x) - w(y)| \bigr].
\end{equation*}
\end{proof}

Applying Lemma~\ref{lem: appendix-rank-correlation} with $\nu = \nu_t$ and $S = S_t$, the dissipation rewrites as
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\KL(\mu\|\nu_t) = -\chi^2(\mu\|\nu_t) - \lambda \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\!\left[ \left|\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu_t(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu_t(y)}\right| \right].
    \label{eq: appendix-combined-rate}
\end{equation}
The elementary inequality $w\log w \Le w^2 - w$ applied to $w = \mu/\nu_t$ gives $\chi^2(\mu\|\nu_t) \Ge \KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$, hence $\frac{\D}{\D t}\KL(\mu\|\nu_t) \Le -\KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$, and Gr\"onwall's lemma yields the exponential decay
\begin{equation}
    \KL(\mu\|\nu_t) \Le e^{-t} \KL(\mu\|\nu_0),\qquad t \Ge 0,
    \label{eq: appendix-KL-exponential}
\end{equation}
with no structural assumption on $\mu$: neither log-concavity, nor a spectral gap, nor any growth condition. This contrasts with the Wasserstein-2 geometry, where the forward KL is not displacement convex and an analogous exponential rate is unavailable without further assumptions.

The uniform convergence rate \eqref{eq: appendix-KL-exponential} should \emph{not} be read as a blanket statement that minimizing the forward KL is intrinsically faster or more efficient than minimizing the reverse KL. The FR flow is a continuous-time idealization on the space of densities and does not account for sampling cost. In practice, evaluating the forward KL and its gradient requires samples from $\mu$, and producing representative samples in high dimensions is itself difficult. The empirical KLXX objective therefore also evaluates the exact log-ratio on the quench-and-temper population $\hat\mu$, which is intended to supply support information that may be absent from the target-sample population. The bound above quantifies convergence given exact target expectations; it does not establish the behavior of this additional empirical term or remove the cost of constructing its samples.

A naive comparison of dissipation rates between the unregularized $\KL$ loss and $\KL + \lambda \X_\mu$ would also be misleading, since scaling the loss by a constant is mere time reparametrization with no qualitative gain. The substantive contribution of $\X_\mu$ is structural rather than rate-based: the pure forward KL term depends locally on the value of $\mu(x)/\nu_t(x)$, whereas the rank field $S_t$ compares that value with the complete distribution of importance weights under $\mu$. The regularized flow \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-L-flow} therefore couples the update at $x$ to global rank information that the local forward KL term does not contain.

Two further cautions concern the discrete realization of the flow. First, the time-reparametrization argument cuts in both directions once the optimizer enters. Training follows Adam steps, not the FR flow: the per-coordinate second-moment normalization absorbs a global rescaling of the loss, so a larger gradient buys no speedup by itself, while a regularizer that reshapes the relative magnitudes of the gradient components does change the optimizer's effective time scale. Part of any observed difference in training speed between $\KL$ and $\KL + \lambda \X_\mu$ may therefore be optimizer scaling rather than information geometry, and we claim no universal transient speedup on this basis. Second, the dissipation \eqref{eq: appendix-combined-rate} is a population statement, while each gradient step uses a finite, generally biased sample estimate. The staged clock experiment examines this finite-batch setting with a schedule-matched comparison: its quantitative claims use the selected-proposal ESS over the complete validation population at each level, not a composed endpoint estimate or a diagnostic computed on a training mini-batch.

\begin{remark}[Monotone decay of $\X_\mu$]
The regularizer $\X_\mu$ also decreases monotonically along the selected regularized flow: pairing the selected subvariation $\delta\X_\mu/\delta\nu = -2wS$ \eqref{eq: appendix-var-X} against the flow \eqref{eq: appendix-FR-L-flow} gives
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu_t) = -2\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu^2}{\nu_t} S_t \D x - 4\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu^2}{\nu_t} S_t^2 \D x  \Le  0.
\end{equation*}
The first term is nonpositive by Lemma~\ref{lem: appendix-rank-correlation} and vanishes only when $\mu/\nu_t$ is $\mu$-almost-everywhere constant. Hence the inequality is strict away from that case, and $\X_\mu$ is driven to zero along the flow.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Asymptotic accuracy for a fixed biased surrogate}
\label{subsec: appendix-accuracy}

The convergence analysis of Section~\ref{subsec: appendix-FR-convergence} takes the outer expectation in $\KL(\mu\|\nu)+\lambda\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)$ under the exact target. To isolate surrogate bias, fix a smooth density $\tilde\mu$ that does not vary with $\nu$ during this analysis and define
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{\mathcal L}[\nu]
    = \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\log w(x)\D x
    + \lambda\!\iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\tilde\mu(y)\bigl|\log w(x) - \log w(y)\bigr|\D x\D y,
    \label{eq: appendix-surrogate-loss}
\end{equation}
where the pointwise ratio $w(x)=\mu(x)/\nu(x)$ is still evaluated against the exact target. Repeating the selected-subgradient calculation of Section~\ref{subsec: appendix-FR-grad-calc} with $\mu$ replaced by $\tilde\mu$ only in the outer integrals gives
\begin{equation*}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\bigl[\widetilde{\mathcal L}\bigr](x) = \nu(x) - \tilde\mu(x)\bigl(1 + 2\lambda \widetilde S(x)\bigr),
    \qquad
    \widetilde S(x) := \mathbb E_{y\sim\tilde\mu}\bigl[\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr].
\end{equation*}
A stationary density $\nu^\star$ of this selected flow therefore satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \nu^\star(x) = \tilde\mu(x)\biggl[1 + 2\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\tilde\mu(y)\operatorname{sgn}\biggl(\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu^\star(y)}\biggr)\D y\biggr].
    \label{eq: appendix-surrogate-stationary}
\end{equation}
At $\lambda=0$, the equation reduces to $\nu^\star=\tilde\mu$ and the residual error is $\KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu)$. For $\lambda>0$, the rank correction $1+2\lambda\widetilde S^\star$ raises $\nu^\star$ relative to $\tilde\mu$ where $\mu/\nu^\star$ is comparatively high and lowers it where the ratio is low. For $\lambda<\frac12$, the bracket in \eqref{eq: appendix-surrogate-stationary} is positive because $\widetilde S^\star\in[-1,1]$. Antisymmetry of the sign also gives
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\mathbb R^d} \tilde\mu(x)\, \widetilde S^\star(x)\, \D x
    = \mathbb E_{x, y \sim \tilde\mu}\biggl[\operatorname{sgn}\biggl(\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu^\star(y)}\biggr)\biggr] = 0,
\end{equation*}
hence every stationary point $\nu^\star = \tilde\mu(1 + 2\lambda \widetilde S^\star)$ is a probability density.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm: appendix-accuracy}
Assume that $\mu$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $\tilde\mu$ and that $\lambda \in [0, \frac12)$. Then every stationary density $\nu^\star$ of the selected subgradient flow for $\widetilde{\mathcal L}$ in \eqref{eq: appendix-surrogate-loss} satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \KL(\mu\|\nu^\star) \Le \KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu) - \lambda\!\iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\tilde\mu(y) \biggl|\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu^\star(y)}\biggr| \D x \D y.
    \label{eq: appendix-accuracy-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Substituting the stationary identity $\nu^\star = \tilde\mu(1+2\lambda\widetilde S^\star)$ into the difference of forward KLs and using $\mu = \nu^\star \cdot (\mu/\nu^\star) = \tilde\mu(1+2\lambda\widetilde S^\star)(\mu/\nu^\star)$,
\begin{equation*}
    \KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu) - \KL(\mu\|\nu^\star)
    = \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\mu(x)\log\bigl(1 + 2\lambda\widetilde S^\star(x)\bigr)\D x
    = \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x) \frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)}u(x)\log u(x)\D x,
\end{equation*}
where $u(x) := 1 + 2\lambda\widetilde S^\star(x) \Ge 1 - 2\lambda > 0$, since $\widetilde S^\star \in [-1, 1]$ (a $\tilde\mu$-average of signs) and $\lambda < \frac12$. The elementary inequality $u\log u \Ge u - 1$ for $u > 0$ then yields
\begin{equation*}
    \KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu) - \KL(\mu\|\nu^\star)
    \Ge 2\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x) \frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} \widetilde S^\star(x)\D x.
\end{equation*}
Applying the rank-correlation argument of Lemma~\ref{lem: appendix-rank-correlation} verbatim with $\mu$ replaced by $\tilde\mu$ in the outer measure,
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)}\widetilde S^\star(x)\D x = \frac{1}{2}\!\iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\tilde\mu(y) \biggl|\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu^\star(y)}\biggr|\D x\D y,
\end{equation*}
which gives \eqref{eq: appendix-accuracy-bound}.
\end{proof}

At $\lambda=0$, the bound is attained by $\nu^\star=\tilde\mu$. For $0<\lambda<\frac12$, the subtracted discrepancy is nonnegative and is positive unless $\mu/\nu^\star$ is constant on the support of $\tilde\mu$. Thus, within the theorem's assumptions, $\X_\mu$ improves the stationary-error bound for a fixed biased surrogate.

The threshold $\lambda<\frac12$ is a sufficient condition for strict positivity of $\nu^\star$. The experiments use $\lambda=1$ and do not extend the theorem beyond its stated range. The live surrogate also changes with the current flow and is represented by finite batches, so it is not the fixed smooth density assumed here.
