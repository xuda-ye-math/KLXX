\section{Introduction}

Drawing samples from a target distribution $\mu \propto \exp(-U)$ on $\mathbb R^d$ known only up to its normalization constant arises across molecular simulation, Bayesian inference, and statistical physics, and is widely tackled with Boltzmann generators built on normalizing flows \citep{rezende2015variational,papamakarios2017masked,kingma2018glow,noe2019boltzmann,wu2020stochastic}. A flow is an invertible parametric map $G:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^d$ trained so that the pushforward $\nu := G^{-1}_{\#}\mu_0$ of an easily samplable reference $\mu_0$ (typically a Gaussian) approximates $\mu$. The trained flow returns independent samples from $\nu$ along with an exact, tractable density, and importance reweighting against $\mu$ then converts those into Monte Carlo estimates with controllable finite-sample error.

Two training objectives dominate the literature. The original Boltzmann generator \citep{noe2019boltzmann} and its variational-inference predecessor \citep{rezende2015variational} minimize the reverse KL
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{KL}(\nu\|\mu) = \int_{\mathbb R^d} \nu(x) \log \frac{\nu(x)}{\mu(x)} \D x,
    \label{eq: reverse KL}
\end{equation}
which needs only samples from $\nu$ and is therefore cheap to estimate. It is, however, mode-seeking: on multimodal $\mu$ the flow tends to collapse onto a subset of the modes from which it cannot recover \citep{noe2019boltzmann,wu2020stochastic}. The opposing family minimizes the forward KL \citep{gabrie2022adaptive,wirnsberger2020targeted},
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\nu) = \int_{\mathbb R^d} \mu(x) \log \frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} \D x,
    \label{eq: forward KL}
\end{equation}
which is mode-covering but needs samples from $\mu$. Such samples are produced by annealed importance sampling (AIS) \citep{neal2001annealed}, which transports $\nu$ toward $\mu$ through a ladder of intermediate distributions while accumulating importance weights. The flow-AIS bootstrap (FAB) of \citet{midgley2022flow} couples AIS to flow training and replaces the target by the mode-sensitive surrogate $\mu^2/\nu$ to encourage exploration.

A separate line of work drops the explicit-density requirement and trains a diffusion- or SDE-based sampler directly: the path-integral sampler \citep{zhang2022path}, denoising diffusion samplers \citep{vargas2023denoising}, and the more recent iterated denoising energy matching \citep{akhound2024iterated} learn a stochastic process whose terminal distribution matches $\mu$. These samplers are expressive and scale well, but they do not deliver an exact tractable density of their output, so importance reweighting against $\mu$ is no longer available. We commit to flow-based methods for that reason: the invertibility of $G$ gives an explicit $\nu$ and therefore an exact, finite-sample importance-sampling pipeline, in which an effective sample size (ESS) \citep{liu2001monte,doucet2001introduction} computed from the flow's own weights is the gold-standard diagnostic of training quality.

The catch is that ESS is gold-standard only when $\mu$ and $\nu$ are evaluated on the same support. If $\nu$ misses modes of $\mu$, the AIS chain initialized at $\nu$ rarely escapes the basin $\nu$ already covers \citep{noe2019boltzmann,midgley2022flow}, so the AIS samples and the importance weights are computed only against the modes that $\nu$ already reaches. The resulting ESS can be close to $1$ while a large fraction of the mass of $\mu$ is missing entirely. We call this the fake ESS pitfall: ESS alone certifies nothing about mode coverage, and any AIS-reliant scheme inherits the same blind spot.

Our remedy is to augment the forward KL with a family of cross-regularization functionals that penalize variation of the log-ratio $\log(\mu/\nu)$ directly. Let
\begin{equation}
    \X_\omega(\mu\|\nu) = \iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d} \omega(x,y)
    \biggl|\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} - \log\frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)}\biggr| \D x \D y,
    \label{eq: X}
\end{equation}
where $\omega$ is a non-negative measure on $\mathbb R^{2d}$. The motivation is direct: matching $\nu$ to $\mu$ is the same as flattening $\log(\mu/\nu)$, and the pairwise difference cancels the unknown normalization of $\mu$. As a loss ingredient $\X_\omega$ has three properties that we will use throughout:
\begin{enumerate}
    \setzero
    \item \textbf{Pointwise control.} The forward KL constrains $\nu \approx \mu$ only in a global Jensen-averaged sense, whereas $\X_\omega$ penalizes log-ratio variation wherever $\omega$ places mass, letting us localize the constraint.
    \item \textbf{Sample robustness.} $\X_\omega$ vanishes whenever $\log(\mu/\nu)$ is constant on the support of $\omega$, so its minimizer is insensitive to the precise sampling of $\omega$; even hand-injected mode locations can be put into $\omega$ without corrupting it.
    \item \textbf{Forward-KL compatibility.} For $\omega = \mu \otimes \mu$ the integrand of $\X_\omega$ coincides with that of the forward KL up to a sign, so the same autograd graph and the same $\mu$-samples are reused at negligible extra cost; the scales also match and no extra hyperparameter is needed.
\end{enumerate}

Each choice of $\omega$ localizes the constraint to a region of $\mathbb R^d$; we use three choices with complementary roles. With $\omega = \mu \otimes \mu$,
\begin{equation}
    \X_\mu(\mu\|\nu) = \iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}
    \mu(x) \mu(y) \biggl|\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} - \log\frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)}\biggr| \D x \D y,
    \label{eq: X-mu}
\end{equation}
which is essentially free by the forward-KL compatibility above. The forward KL controls the $\mu$-mean of $\log(\mu/\nu)$ while $\X_\mu$ controls its $\mu$-spread, tightening the in-mode density match once $\nu$ has reached the modes of $\mu$ and reducing the residual bias inherited from imperfect AIS samples.

With $\omega = \hat\mu \otimes \hat\mu$,
\begin{equation}
    \X_{\hat\mu}(\mu\|\nu) = \iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}
    \hat\mu(x) \hat\mu(y) \biggl|\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} - \log\frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)}\biggr| \D x \D y,
    \label{eq: X-hat-mu}
\end{equation}
where $\hat\mu$ is a coarse, deliberately over-spread approximation of $\mu$. By sample robustness the minimizer of $\X_{\hat\mu}$ is insensitive to the precise shape of $\hat\mu$, so the sole purpose of $\hat\mu$ is to place mass on modes and tails that the current $\nu$ has not yet covered; the gradient then pulls the flow toward those regions. We build $\hat\mu$ once, before training, with a Quench and Temper (QT) algorithm that diffuses an initial cloud through Brownian noise to bridge basins, runs a deterministic mode-finder to collapse it onto mode centers, and rejuvenates with a short Langevin chain. QT errs on the side of broad coverage and applies uniformly to disconnected modes, long-tailed distributions, and targets supported on low-dimensional manifolds.

With $\omega = \bar\nu \otimes \bar\nu$,
\begin{equation}
    \X_{\bar\nu}(\mu\|\nu) = \iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}
    \bar\nu(x) \bar\nu(y) \biggl|\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} - \log\frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)}\biggr| \D x \D y,
    \label{eq: X-nu}
\end{equation}
where $\bar\nu$ denotes a stop-gradient copy of the current pushforward $\nu$. Sampling from $\bar\nu$ is free, since the training loop already pushes a batch from $\mu_0$ through $G^{-1}$ at every step; the detached gradient keeps the loss numerically stable on sharp targets where a true reverse-KL gradient explodes. $\X_{\bar\nu}$ plays the reverse-KL role without its pathologies: it penalizes mass that $\nu$ places outside the support of $\mu$, suppressing the mode-overfitting leakage that $\X_\mu$ and $\X_{\hat\mu}$ alone cannot see.

The three weights are complementary: $\X_\mu$ refines what AIS already reaches, $\X_{\hat\mu}$ pulls $\nu$ toward modes it has missed, and $\X_{\bar\nu}$ pushes $\nu$ away from regions outside the support of $\mu$. Our preferred training loss combines all three with the forward KL,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}[\nu] = \KL(\mu\|\nu) + \lambda\,\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu) + \X_{(\alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu)\otimes(\alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu)}(\mu\|\nu),
    \label{eq: total-loss}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda,\alpha,\beta \Ge 0$ are tunable weights and the third term is $\X_\omega$ with mixture weight $\omega = \alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu$. This mixture form is strictly stronger than the sum $\alpha^2 \X_{\hat\mu} + \beta^2 \X_{\bar\nu}$: the pairwise integral also generates cross contributions $\bigl|\log(\mu/\nu)(x) - \log(\mu/\nu)(y)\bigr|$ with $x \sim \hat\mu$ and $y \sim \bar\nu$, which anchor the in-support and out-of-support log-ratios to each other. We confirm this empirically: the mixture variant attains both the highest ESS and the cleanest coverage on all of our 2D benchmarks.

We pair ESS with the coverage metric of \citet{naeem2020reliable} for evaluation, which counts the fraction of QT-discovered $\hat\mu$ samples whose $k$-nearest-neighbor ball is reached by the trained $\nu$. Coverage exposes the modes that a fake ESS hides, so the two metrics together certify both density calibration and mode coverage.

\section{Fisher--Rao gradient flow with X functionals}
\label{sec: FR}

Before turning to the training algorithm, we study the gradient-flow properties of the loss on the space of densities; the convergence rate of this continuous-time idealization is a clean proxy for how trainable the corresponding discrete loss is in practice. We view $\nu$ as a generic probability density evolving along the gradient flow of the loss under an appropriate Riemannian metric on the space of densities \citep{ambrosio2008gradient,villani2009optimal} and ask how fast $\nu_t$ converges to the target $\mu$ in $\KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$.

In this section we restrict to the loss with only the forward KL and the raw-target regularizer $\X_\mu$,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal L[\nu] = \KL(\mu\|\nu) + \lambda\, \X_\mu(\mu\|\nu) = \int_{\mathbb R^d} \mu(x) \log \frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} \D x +
    \lambda\iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}
     \mu(x)\mu(y) \biggl|\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} - \log\frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)}\biggr| \D x \D y,
    \label{eq: loss KL X}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda>0$ is a fixed constant.
Throughout this section we abbreviate the importance weight by
\begin{equation*}
    w(x) := \frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)},
\end{equation*}
so that the log-ratio is $\log w(x) = \log\mu(x) - \log\nu(x)$.

The choice of Riemannian metric matters. In the Wasserstein-2 geometry, induced by optimal transport \citep{jordan1998variational,otto2001geometry}, the forward KL fails to be displacement convex along Wasserstein-2 geodesics, and the standard Otto--Villani / Bakry--{\'E}mery machinery \citep{otto2000generalization,bakry2014analysis} that yields exponential convergence for the reverse KL does not apply. We therefore work in the Fisher--Rao geometry \citep{amari2016information,bauer2016uniqueness}, in which the forward KL admits an unconditional exponential decay rate and the regularizer $\X_\mu$ contributes a qualitatively new, nonlocal birth--death modulation \citep{lu2019accelerating} in which the local update at $x$ is driven by how the importance weight at $x$ ranks against the population of weights under $\mu$.

Denoting the $\nu$-mean by $\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle_\nu := \int(\,\cdot\,)\,\nu\,\D x$, the Fisher--Rao gradient of a functional $\mathcal F[\nu]$ and the associated dissipation identity along the FR gradient flow $\partial_t\nu_t = -\mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal F(\nu_t)$ are
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal F(\nu) = \nu\biggl(\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu} - \Big\langle\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu}\Big\rangle_\nu\biggr),
    \qquad
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\mathcal F[\nu_t] = -\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\biggl(\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu} - \Big\langle\frac{\delta\mathcal F}{\delta\nu}\Big\rangle_{\nu_t}\biggr)^{2}\nu_t\,\D x.
    \label{eq: FR-gradient}
\end{equation}
The mean-subtraction enforces $\int\mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\mathcal F = 0$, so the FR flow conserves probability mass automatically.

\subsection{Derivation of $\X_\mu$-augmented Fisher--Rao gradient flow}
\label{subsec: FR-grad-calc}

The first variation of the forward KL with respect to $\nu$ admits the simple form
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\delta\,\KL(\mu\|\nu)}{\delta \nu}(x) = -\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)} = -w(x),
    \label{eq: var-KL}
\end{equation}
so by \eqref{eq: FR-gradient} the FR gradient of the forward KL is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\big[\KL(\mu\|\nu)\big](x) = \nu(x) - \mu(x).
    \label{eq: FR-grad-KL}
\end{equation}

For the $\X_\mu$ functional, the inner log-ratio difference has first variation
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\delta\bigl(\log w(x) - \log w(y)\bigr)}{\delta\nu(x)} = \frac{1}{\nu(y)}\delta(x-y) - \frac{1}{\nu(x)}.
\end{equation*}
By the symmetry of $\X_\mu$ in $x$ and $y$,
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\delta\,\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)}{\delta \nu}(x) & =
    2 \int_{\mathbb R^d} \mu(x) \mu(y) \operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y)) \frac{\delta (\log w(x) - \log w(y))}{\delta\nu(x)} \D y \\
    & = 2 \int_{\mathbb R^d} \mu(x) \mu(y) \operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))
    \bigg(\frac1{\nu(y)}\delta(x-y) - \frac1{\nu(x)}\bigg) \D y \\
    & = -2\int_{\mathbb R^d} w(x) \operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))  \D y,
\end{align*}
where the contribution of $\delta(x-y)$ vanishes because $\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y)) = 0$ at $y = x$. Hence
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\delta\,\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)}{\delta \nu}(x) = - 2w(x)S(x),
    \qquad
    S(x) := \mathbb E_{y\sim\mu}\bigl[\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr].
    \label{eq: var-X}
\end{equation}
We call $S$ the \emph{rank field} of the importance weight under $\mu$: $S(x) > 0$ where $w(x)$ exceeds the $\mu$-median of $w$, i.e.\ where $\nu$ undercovers a mode of $\mu$; $S(x) < 0$ where $\nu$ overcovers; and $S \equiv 0$ at $\nu = \mu$. By antisymmetry,
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbb E_\mu[S] = \mathbb E_{x,y\sim \mu}\bigl[\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr] = 0,
\end{equation*}
and the FR gradient of $\X_\mu$ with respect to $\nu$ is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\big[\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)\big](x) = -2\mu(x)\,S(x).
    \label{eq: FR-grad-X}
\end{equation}

Since the FR gradient is linear in the functional, the FR gradient of $\mathcal L[\nu] = \KL(\mu\|\nu) + \lambda\,\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)$ is the direct sum of \eqref{eq: FR-grad-KL} and \eqref{eq: FR-grad-X}:
\begin{equation*}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}[\mathcal L](x) = \mu(x) - \nu(x) + 2\lambda \mu(x) S(x).
\end{equation*}
The augmented Fisher--Rao gradient flow $\partial_t\nu_t = -\mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}[\mathcal L]$ therefore reads
\begin{equation}
    \partial_t\nu_t(x) = \mu(x) - \nu(x) + 2\lambda \mu(x) S_t(x), \quad 
    \text{where~}
    S_t(x) := \mathbb E_{y\sim\mu}\biggl[\operatorname{sgn}\biggl(\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu_t(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu_t(y)}\biggr)\biggr].
    \label{eq: FR-L-flow}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq: FR-L-flow} is a \emph{nonlocal} PDE: its right-hand side at $x$ depends on $S_t(x)$, which in turn requires the entire profile of the importance weight $\mu/\nu_t$ over the support of $\mu$. Mass is conserved along the flow because
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\nu_t(x)\,\D x = 2\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\mu(x)\,S_t(x)\,\D x = 0,
\end{equation*}
and the flow is stationary at $\nu_t = \mu$, where $\mu(x)/\nu_t(x) \equiv 1$ and hence $S_t\equiv 0$. When $\lambda = 0$, \eqref{eq: FR-L-flow} reduces to the pure Fisher--Rao gradient flow of the forward KL.

\subsection{Convergence analysis of the $\X_\mu$-augmented flow}
\label{subsec: FR-convergence}

To analyze the convergence rate of the augmented flow \eqref{eq: FR-L-flow}, we take the forward KL $\KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$ as the error metric and differentiate it along the flow:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\KL(\mu\|\nu_t) = -\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\frac{\mu^2}{\nu_t}(1 + 2\lambda S_t)\,\D x + \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\mu\,\D x = -\chi^2(\mu\|\nu_t) - 2\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\frac{\mu^2}{\nu_t}\,S_t\,\D x,
\end{equation*}
where the chi-squared divergence is
\begin{equation*}
    \chi^2(\mu\|\nu) := \int_{\mathbb R^d}(w-1)^2\nu\D x = \int_{\mathbb R^d} w^2\nu\D x - 1.
\end{equation*}
The sign of the second integral is settled by the following rank-correlation identity.

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem: rank-correlation}
For any probability density $\nu$ on $\mathbb R^d$,
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu(x)^2}{\nu(x)}S(x)\D x = \frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\bigl[|w(x) - w(y)|\bigr] \Ge 0,
\end{equation*}
with equality if and only if $w$ is $\mu$-almost-everywhere constant, i.e.\ $\nu = \mu$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By the definition of $S$,
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu^2}{\nu}S\D x = \mathbb E_\mu\bigl[w(x)S(x)\bigr] = \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\bigl[w(x)\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr].
\end{equation*}
Symmetrizing in $x\leftrightarrow y$ and averaging the two equal copies gives
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu^2}{\nu}S\D x = \frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\bigl[(w(x) - w(y))\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr] = \frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\bigl[\,|w(x) - w(y)|\,\bigr].
\end{equation*}
\end{proof}

Applying Lemma~\ref{lem: rank-correlation} with $\nu = \nu_t$ and $S = S_t$, the dissipation rewrites as
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\KL(\mu\|\nu_t) = -\chi^2(\mu\|\nu_t) - \lambda\,\mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mu}\!\left[\,\left|\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu_t(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu_t(y)}\right|\,\right].
    \label{eq: combined-rate}
\end{equation}
The elementary inequality $w\log w \Le w^2 - w$ applied to $w = \mu/\nu_t$ gives $\chi^2(\mu\|\nu_t) \Ge \KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$, hence $\frac{\D}{\D t}\KL(\mu\|\nu_t) \Le -\KL(\mu\|\nu_t)$, and Gr\"onwall's lemma yields the exponential decay
\begin{equation}
    \KL(\mu\|\nu_t) \Le e^{-t}\,\KL(\mu\|\nu_0),\qquad t \Ge 0,
    \label{eq: KL-exponential}
\end{equation}
with no structural assumption on $\mu$ --- neither log-concavity, nor a spectral gap, nor any growth condition. This contrasts sharply with the Wasserstein-2 geometry, where the forward KL is not displacement convex and an analogous exponential rate is unavailable without further assumptions.

The uniform convergence rate \eqref{eq: KL-exponential} should \emph{not} be read as a blanket statement that minimizing the forward KL is intrinsically faster or more efficient than minimizing the reverse KL. The FR flow is a continuous-time idealization on the space of densities and is agnostic to the sampling cost. In practice, the difficulty of training has been partially transported to the AIS step: evaluating the forward KL and its gradient requires accurate samples of $\mu$, and producing such samples in high dimensions is itself a hard problem --- it is the very reason that AIS is needed, and that the $\X_{\hat\mu}$ regularizer must compensate for modes that AIS alone fails to reach. The exponential rate \eqref{eq: KL-exponential} quantifies how fast the flow \emph{can} converge given accurate target samples; it does not eliminate the burden of producing them.

A naive comparison of dissipation rates between $\KL$-only and $\KL + \lambda\,\X_\mu$ would also be misleading, since scaling the loss by a constant is mere time reparametrization with no qualitative gain. The substantive contribution of $\X_\mu$ is structural rather than rate-based: while the pure-KL FR gradient is local in $x$, the rank field $S_t$ is global, so the augmented flow \eqref{eq: FR-L-flow} couples the update at $x$ to the entire distribution of importance weights under $\mu$. This nonlocal coupling amplifies the birth rate on undercovered regions of $\mu$ and suppresses it on overcovered ones, a global comparison that pure forward KL --- which knows only whether $\mu(x)/\nu_t(x)$ exceeds or falls below $1$ pointwise --- cannot produce.

\begin{remark}
The regularizer $\X_\mu$ also decreases monotonically along the augmented flow: a direct computation along \eqref{eq: FR-L-flow} gives
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\D}{\D t}\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu_t) = -2\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu^2}{\nu_t}\,S_t\,\D x - 4\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{\mu^2}{\nu_t}\,S_t^2\,\D x \;\Le\; 0,
\end{equation*}
with strict inequality whenever $\nu_t \ne \mu$. Thus $\X_\mu$ is itself driven to zero in the long-time limit.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Asymptotic accuracy of biased forward KL training}
\label{subsec: accuracy}

The convergence analysis of Section~\ref{subsec: FR-convergence} assumes that the outer expectation in $\KL(\mu\|\nu) + \lambda\,\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)$ is taken under the exact target $\mu$. In practice, the algorithm only has access to an approximation $\tilde\mu$ of $\mu$ supplied by AIS, and the realized loss reads
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{\mathcal L}[\nu]
    = \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\log w(x)\D x
    + \lambda\!\iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\tilde\mu(y)\bigl|\log w(x) - \log w(y)\bigr|\D x\D y,
    \label{eq: biased-loss}
\end{equation}
where the pointwise ratio $w(x) = \mu(x)/\nu(x)$ is still evaluated against the exact target since it is computed from the target potential $U$ and the flow density per sample. Repeating the derivation of Section~\ref{subsec: FR-grad-calc} with $\mu$ replaced by $\tilde\mu$ in the outer integrands gives the Fisher--Rao gradient
\begin{equation*}
    \mathrm{grad}^{\mathrm{FR}}\bigl[\widetilde{\mathcal L}\bigr](x) = \nu(x) - \tilde\mu(x)\bigl(1 + 2\lambda\,\widetilde S(x)\bigr),
    \qquad
    \widetilde S(x) := \mathbb E_{y\sim\tilde\mu}\bigl[\operatorname{sgn}(w(x) - w(y))\bigr].
\end{equation*}
A stationary point $\nu^\star$ of \eqref{eq: biased-loss} therefore satisfies the self-consistent equation
\begin{equation}
    \nu^\star(x) = \tilde\mu(x)\biggl[1 + 2\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\tilde\mu(y)\operatorname{sgn}\biggl(\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu^\star(y)}\biggr)\D y\biggr].
    \label{eq: biased-stationary}
\end{equation}
At $\lambda = 0$ the equation collapses to $\nu^\star = \tilde\mu$: pure forward KL training inherits the bias of the AIS distribution verbatim, and the residual accuracy is exactly $\KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu)$. For $\lambda > 0$, however, the rank-based correction $1 + 2\lambda\widetilde S^\star$ inflates $\nu^\star$ above $\tilde\mu$ where the importance weight $\mu/\nu^\star$ is high (i.e.\ where $\nu^\star$ still undercovers $\mu$) and deflates it where the weight is low, providing a global feedback that pulls $\nu^\star$ from $\tilde\mu$ back toward $\mu$. This is quantified in the following theorem.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm: accuracy}
Assume $\mu$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $\tilde\mu$ and $\lambda \in [0, 1/2]$, so that the bracket in \eqref{eq: biased-stationary} is non-negative and $\nu^\star$ is a probability density. Any stationary point $\nu^\star$ of the biased loss \eqref{eq: biased-loss} satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \KL(\mu\|\nu^\star) \Le \KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu) - \lambda\!\iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\tilde\mu(y)\,\biggl|\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu^\star(y)}\biggr|\,\D x\,\D y.
    \label{eq: accuracy-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Substituting the stationary identity $\nu^\star = \tilde\mu(1+2\lambda\widetilde S^\star)$ into the difference of forward KLs and using $\mu = \nu^\star \cdot (\mu/\nu^\star) = \tilde\mu(1+2\lambda\widetilde S^\star)(\mu/\nu^\star)$,
\begin{equation*}
    \KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu) - \KL(\mu\|\nu^\star)
    = \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\mu(x)\log\bigl(1 + 2\lambda\widetilde S^\star(x)\bigr)\D x
    = \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\,\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)}u(x)\log u(x)\D x,
\end{equation*}
where $u(x) := 1 + 2\lambda\widetilde S^\star(x) \Ge 1 - 2\lambda \Ge 0$ since $\lambda \Le 1/2$. The elementary inequality $u\log u \Ge u - 1$ for $u \Ge 0$ then yields
\begin{equation*}
    \KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu) - \KL(\mu\|\nu^\star)
    \Ge 2\lambda\!\int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\,\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)}\,\widetilde S^\star(x)\D x.
\end{equation*}
Applying the rank-correlation argument of Lemma~\ref{lem: rank-correlation} verbatim with $\mu$ replaced by $\tilde\mu$ in the outer measure,
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)}\widetilde S^\star(x)\D x = \frac{1}{2}\!\iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}\!\tilde\mu(x)\tilde\mu(y)\,\biggl|\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu^\star(x)} - \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu^\star(y)}\biggr|\D x\D y,
\end{equation*}
which gives \eqref{eq: accuracy-bound}.
\end{proof}

The right-hand side of \eqref{eq: accuracy-bound} contrasts the two roles of $\X_\mu$ training. At $\lambda = 0$ the bound is tight at $\nu^\star = \tilde\mu$ and the residual KL is fixed at $\KL(\mu\|\tilde\mu)$ --- the AIS bias is a hard floor. Switching on the regularizer with $\lambda \Le 1/2$ strictly lowers this floor by an amount controlled by the discrepancy of the importance weights $\mu/\nu^\star$ across the support of $\tilde\mu$: the larger this discrepancy, the more the rank-based feedback pulls $\nu^\star$ toward $\mu$. The $\X_\mu$ regularizer is therefore not merely a numerical stabilizer for the forward KL flow: it directly improves the asymptotic accuracy of the trained density when the target samples are biased, which is the practically relevant regime.

The threshold $\lambda \Le 1/2$ is a worst-case condition enforcing positivity of $\nu^\star$ uniformly in $x$; in practice the accuracy improvement persists well beyond it. The practical takeaway is that even without the wide-coverage regularizer $\X_{\hat\mu}$, adding $\X_\mu$ alone to the forward KL loss already reduces the bias floor inherited from inaccurate AIS samples.

\section{Flow training with X functionals}

This section turns the abstract loss of the introduction into a concrete training algorithm for the flow map $G$. Let $U_0$ and $U$ be the source and target potentials on $\mathbb R^d$, so that $\mu_0 \propto \exp(-U_0)$ is the easily samplable reference and $\mu \propto \exp(-U)$ is the target. The flow $G:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^d$ is parameterized so that $G_{\#}\mu \approx \mu_0$, and target samples are drawn from $\nu := G^{-1}_{\#}\mu_0$ by importance sampling. The remainder of the section proceeds in four steps: (i) the forward KL together with the AIS pipeline that supplies approximate samples from $\mu$; (ii) the single-pass computation of $\X_\mu$ \eqref{eq: X-mu} and of the analogously cheap $\X_{\bar\nu}$ \eqref{eq: X-nu}; (iii) the Quench and Temper construction of the wide-coverage measure $\hat\mu$ used in $\X_{\hat\mu}$ \eqref{eq: X-hat-mu}; and (iv) the assembly of all three terms into the total training loss in terms of $G$. We then close with the ESS and coverage diagnostics used to evaluate the trained flow.

\subsection{Forward KL and annealed importance sampling}

By the change-of-variables formula, the pullback density of $\mu_0$ under $G$ is
\begin{equation}
    \nu(y) = \mu_0(G(y)) \, |\det J_{G}(y)|,
    \qquad y \in \mathbb R^d,
\end{equation}
where $J_G(y) \in \mathbb R^{d\times d}$ is the Jacobian of $G$ at $y$. The forward KL loss then reads
\begin{align}
    \KL\big(\mu\|\nu\big)
    & = -\int_{\mathbb R^d} \mu(y) \log \nu(y) \D y + \mathrm{const} \notag \\
    & = \int_{\mathbb R^d} \mu(y) \Big(U_0(G(y)) - U(y) - \log|\det J_G(y)|\Big) \D y + \mathrm{const}.
    \label{eq: forward KL full}
\end{align}
We deliberately parameterize the loss in terms of $G$ (mapping target to source) rather than $G^{-1}$, so that no inversion is required during training.

Evaluating \eqref{eq: forward KL} requires samples from $\mu$, which are not directly available. The natural surrogate is to draw $y_1,\dots,y_B$ from the pushforward $\nu$ and reweight by
\begin{equation}
    w(y) = \frac{\mu(y)}{\nu(y)} = \exp\Big(
        U_0(G(y)) - U(y) - \log|\det J_G(y)|
    \Big), \qquad y \in \mathbb R^d,
    \label{eq: weight}
\end{equation}
followed by a resampling step and a short Langevin simulation to diffuse the duplicates. In high dimensions, however, $\mu$ and $\nu$ typically differ greatly, so the weights $w(y_i)$ collapse onto a few outliers and the resulting samples are of very low quality.

Annealed importance sampling (AIS) bridges this gap by inserting $M+1$ intermediate distributions
\begin{equation}
    \pi_k = \nu^{1-\frac{k}{M}} (\mu)^{\frac{k}{M}} = \nu \cdot w(y)^{\frac{k}{M}},
    \qquad k = 0,1,\dots,M,
    \label{eq: pi-k}
\end{equation}
so that $\pi_0 = \nu$ and $\pi_M = \mu$. Each rung of the ladder reweights by only $w(y)^{\frac{1}{M}}$, keeping the weight ratio close to $1$ and avoiding the degeneracy of direct importance sampling:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\setstretch{1.2}
\caption{Annealed importance sampling from $\nu$ to $\mu$}
\KwInput{samples $y_1,\dots,y_B$ from $\nu$, ladder length $M$}
\KwOutput{samples $y_1,\dots,y_B$ approximately distributed as $\mu$}

\For{$k = 1,\dots,M$}{
    Compute the per-step weights $w(y_i)^{\frac{1}{M}}$ on $y_1,\dots,y_B$\;
    Resample $y_1',\dots,y_B'$ from the weighted empirical distribution\;
    \tcp{The resampled set has duplicates; Langevin diffuses them}
    Apply a short Langevin simulation to $y_1',\dots,y_B'$, returning fresh samples $y_1,\dots,y_B \sim \pi_k$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Single-pass computation of $\X_\mu$ and $\X_{\bar\nu}$}
\label{subsec: single-pass}

Substituting the explicit log-ratio under the flow map $G$ into the definition \eqref{eq: X-mu} of $\X_\mu$, the functional becomes
\begin{align}
    \X_\mu\big(\mu \| \nu\big)
    &= \iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}
    \mu(y) \mu(y') \biggl|
    \Big(U_0(G(y)) - U(y) - \log|\det J_G(y)|\Big) \notag \\
    &\qquad{} - \Big(U_0(G(y')) - U(y') - \log|\det J_G(y')|\Big)
    \biggr| \D y \D y'.
    \label{eq: X-mu full}
\end{align}
The integrand inside the absolute value is exactly the log-ratio $\log(\mu/\nu)$ that appears in the forward KL \eqref{eq: forward KL}, so a single forward pass through $G$ on a $\mu$-batch produces both losses:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\setstretch{1.2}
\caption{Single-pass computation of forward KL and $\X_\mu$}
\KwInput{batch $\mathbf y = (y_1,\dots,y_B) \in \mathbb R^{B\times d}$ of samples from $\mu$}
\KwOutput{forward KL term and $\X_\mu$ term}
\tcp{$\mathbf z$ carries the autograd graph through $G$}
Compute $\mathbf z = U_0(G(\mathbf y)) - U(\mathbf y) - \log|\det J_G(\mathbf y)|$\;
\tcp{$\mathbf z'$ inherits the graph of $\mathbf z$ at zero cost}
Sample a random permutation $\sigma$ of $\{1,\dots,B\}$ and set $\mathbf z' = \mathbf z[\sigma]$\;
Return $\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf z)$ as the forward KL term and $\mathrm{mean}\bigl(|\mathbf z - \mathbf z'|\bigr)$ as the $\X_\mu$ term\;
\end{algorithm}
\mbox{}\\
Backpropagation goes through $\mathbf z$ exactly once, identical to a vanilla forward-KL step, so $\X_\mu$ adds essentially no extra training cost.

The same machinery computes $\X_{\bar\nu}$. Replacing the input batch $\mathbf y$ above by a batch $\bar{\mathbf y} = (\bar y_1,\dots,\bar y_B)$ drawn from $\bar\nu$ produces
\begin{align}
    \X_{\bar\nu}\big(\mu \| \nu\big)
    &= \iint_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}
    \bar\nu(\bar y) \bar\nu(\bar y') \biggl|
    \Big(U_0(G(\bar y)) - U(\bar y) - \log|\det J_G(\bar y)|\Big) \notag \\
    &\qquad{} - \Big(U_0(G(\bar y')) - U(\bar y') - \log|\det J_G(\bar y')|\Big)
    \biggr| \D \bar y \D \bar y',
    \label{eq: X-nu full}
\end{align}
which is just the second line of the algorithm above with the sample source changed. The batch $\bar{\mathbf y}$ is the cheapest available: it is the raw pushforward $\bar y_i = G^{-1}(x_i)$ of source samples $x_i \sim \mu_0$ taken under \texttt{torch.no\_grad}, so $\bar\nu$ is treated as a fixed snapshot of the current pushforward for the purposes of gradient computation. The detached sampler keeps the loss numerically stable on sharp targets, where differentiating through the sampling chain---as a true reverse-KL gradient does---would explode.

\subsection{Initiative mode discovery in $\X_{\hat\mu}$ with the QT algorithm}
\label{subsec: QT}

Computing $\X_{\hat\mu}$ requires samples from the wide-coverage measure $\hat\mu$. The samples are prepared once, before training, as a fixed pool of $P$ points $x_1,\dots,x_P$ distributed as $\hat\mu$; at each training step a mini-batch of size $B$ is drawn from the pool and refreshed by a short Langevin chain before being fed into the single-pass algorithm above with $\mu$ replaced by $\hat\mu$. The pool is produced by Quench and Temper (QT), which proceeds in three stages: diffusion (melt), optimization (quench), and rejuvenation (temper).

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\label{alg: QT}
\setstretch{1.2}
\caption{QT: initiative mode discovery}
\KwInput{samples $x_1,\dots,x_P$ from $\mu_0$}
\KwOutput{samples $x_1,\dots,x_P$ forming $\hat\mu$}
\tcp{Diffusion (melt): scatter the samples to a wider region of $\mathbb R^d$}
Choose a large $\sigma > 0$ and update $x_i \leftarrow x_i + \sigma\xi_i$ with $\xi_i \sim \mathcal N(0, I_d)$ i.i.d.\;
\tcp{Optimization (quench): drive each sample toward a mode center of $\mu$}
Run LBFGS on $U$ for a fixed number of steps to update $x_1,\dots,x_P$\;
\tcp{Rejuvenation (temper): spread the samples around each mode}
Run a short Langevin simulation targeting $\mu$ on $x_1,\dots,x_P$\;
\end{algorithm}
\mbox{}\\
The output samples concentrate near the mode centers of $\mu$, and the initial Brownian diffusion ensures that even modes far from the support of the input are reachable. The $\X_{\hat\mu}$ regularizer therefore penalizes any mode of $\mu$ on which the current pushforward $\nu$ places too little mass, driving the flow to discover modes that AIS alone could never reach.

A large diffusion $\sigma$ biases the QT output toward modes that lie far from the bulk of $\mu_0$. This is desirable rather than problematic: the modes the current pushforward fails to cover are precisely those far from $\mu_0$, and the bias is also harmless because sample robustness (Property~2 in the introduction) implies the minimizer of $\X_{\hat\mu}$ is insensitive to the precise shape of $\hat\mu$.

\subsection{Total training loss}
\label{subsec: total-loss}

Putting the three terms together, the total loss in terms of the flow map $G$ is
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}(G)
    &= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \mu}\bigl[\, U_0(G(y)) - U(y) - \log|\det J_G(y)|\, \bigr]}_{\KL(\mu\|\nu)} \notag \\
    &\quad{} + \lambda\,
    \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \mu}\Bigl[\bigl|\bigl(U_0(G(y))-U(y)-\log|\det J_G(y)|\bigr) - \bigl(U_0(G(y'))-U(y')-\log|\det J_G(y')|\bigr)\bigr|\Bigr]}_{\X_\mu(\mu\|\nu)} \notag \\
    &\quad{} +
    \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu}\Bigl[\bigl|\bigl(U_0(G(y))-U(y)-\log|\det J_G(y)|\bigr) - \bigl(U_0(G(y'))-U(y')-\log|\det J_G(y')|\bigr)\bigr|\Bigr]}_{\X_{\alpha\hat\mu + \beta\bar\nu}(\mu\|\nu)},
    \label{eq: total-loss-G}
\end{align}
where the three expectations are estimated on the per-step batches described above: AIS-transported samples for the $\mu$-integrals (forward KL and $\X_\mu$), and a mixture batch of $\alpha B$ QT samples from $\hat\mu$ together with $\beta B$ stop-gradient pushforward samples from $\bar\nu$ for the third term.

Although $(\lambda, \alpha, \beta)$ are three free parameters in principle, the X functional is by construction on the same scale as the forward KL, so any choice of order $1$ works in practice. Two regimes of the loss \eqref{eq: total-loss-G} are worth highlighting:
\begin{enumerate}
    \setzero
    \item \textbf{Simple regime, $\lambda = 1$ and $\alpha = \beta = 0$.} Only $\X_\mu$ is added on top of the forward KL. This term is free (it reuses the AIS samples and autograd graph of the forward KL) and is enough whenever the source $\mu_0$ already overlaps every mode of $\mu$: the result is a direct accuracy and stability boost over plain forward-KL training, with no extra hyperparameter and no extra sample source.
    \item \textbf{Mode-discovery regime, $\lambda = 1$ and $\alpha = \beta = 1/2$.} The mixture term is turned on with a balanced half--half draw from $\hat\mu$ and $\bar\nu$. The $\hat\mu$ half enhances mode discovery by pulling $\nu$ toward QT-discovered modes that AIS alone cannot reach, while the $\bar\nu$ half restrains mode overfitting by penalizing mass that the flow places outside the support of $\mu$. We recommend this as the default whenever $\mu_0$ may not cover all modes of $\mu$, and use it throughout the experiments.
\end{enumerate}
We have not found re-tuning $(\lambda, \alpha, \beta)$ away from these defaults to improve results on any of our benchmarks.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics: ESS and coverage}

The trained flow $G$ induces the distribution $\nu$ as an approximation to the target $\mu$. We assess its quality through two complementary metrics: the effective sample size (ESS), which measures how uniform the importance weights are on the support reached by the flow, and the coverage, which checks whether every mode of $\mu$ has been reached at all.

The first metric is the effective sample size. Let $\mathbf y = (y_1,\dots,y_N) \in \mathbb R^{N\times d}$ be i.i.d.\ samples from $\nu$, with importance weights $w(y_i)$ given by \eqref{eq: weight}. The ESS is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \ESS(\mathbf y) = \frac{\big(\sum_{i=1}^N w(y_i)\big)^2}{N \sum_{i=1}^N w(y_i)^2} \in [0,1],
\end{equation}
where the upper bound $1$ follows from the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality and is attained when all weights are equal. When $G$ is perfectly trained the weights are constant and the ESS reaches $1$. As emphasized in the introduction, however, this conclusion is only one-way: a high ESS certifies that the weights are uniform \emph{on the support of $\nu$}, but modes of $\mu$ that the pushforward never reaches contribute nothing to the sum and so cannot lower the ESS---hence the \emph{fake ESS} pitfall.

To detect such missing modes, we pair the ESS with the coverage metric of \citet{naeem2020reliable}. Let $\mathbf x = (x_1,\dots,x_P) \in \mathbb R^{P\times d}$ be the samples of $\hat\mu$ produced by the Quench and Temper algorithm (Algorithm~\ref{alg: QT}). For each $1 \Le i \Le P$, the $k$-nearest-neighbor radius of $x_i$ is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{NND}_k(x_i;\mathbf x) = \inf\Big\{ r \Ge 0 :
    \# \big\{1\Le j \Le P: 0 < |x_i - x_j| < r\big\} \Ge k
    \Big\},
\end{equation}
i.e., the distance from $x_i$ to its $k$-th nearest neighbor in $\mathbf x$. The coverage of $\mathbf x$ by the pushforward samples $\mathbf y$ is then
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Coverage}_k(\mathbf y;\mathbf x) =
    \frac{1}{P} \sum_{i=1}^P \mathbf 1\Bigl\{
        \exists\, 1\Le j\Le N : |y_j - x_i| \Le \mathrm{NND}_k(x_i;\mathbf x)
    \Bigr\},
\end{equation}
i.e., the fraction of points in $\mathbf x$ whose $k$-nearest-neighbor ball contains at least one pushforward sample. A coverage close to $1$ certifies that every region populated by $\hat\mu$ has been reached by the flow, while a coverage substantially below $1$ exposes precisely the missing modes that the ESS alone cannot see.

\section{Construction of adaptive Boltzmann generator}

We construct an adaptive Boltzmann generator that determines the AIS temperature ladder on the fly and yields a numerically stable training scheme. As a rule of thumb, for a $d$-dimensional system an empirical choice of the ladder length $M$ in AIS (Algorithm~\ref{alg: QT}) is $M = d$, which we motivate below with a Gaussian example.

The flow is a neural spline flow with $6$ coupling transforms, $32$ bins, and hidden widths $(128, 128)$, defined on the square box $[-L, L]^2$ with $L = 6, 3.6, 5$ for Himmelblau, Annulus, and Chessboard respectively. Training uses Adam with learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch size $500$, for $1000$ steps; at each step the AIS surrogate is built by one importance-weighted resampling pass against the current pushforward, followed by $50$ Langevin steps of size $2\times 10^{-3}$. The loss coefficients are fixed at the preferred default of Section~\ref{subsec: total-loss}, $\lambda = 1$ and $\alpha = \beta = 1/2$. The wide-coverage measure $\hat\mu$ is built once before training by applying QT (Algorithm~\ref{alg: QT}) to a fresh batch of $500$ source samples, with diffusion standard deviation $2$, optimization step $0.5$ for $200$ LBFGS iterations, and rejuvenation step $2\times 10^{-3}$ for $1000$ Langevin iterations; the QT pool is then recycled across training steps with one Langevin rejuvenation per step.

For each target we compare four losses obtained by progressively adding one X regularizer at a time:
\begin{enumerate}
    \setzero
    \item \textbf{KL}: the bare forward KL \eqref{eq: forward KL};
    \item \textbf{KL $+\,\X_\mu$}: adding the raw-target regularizer with $\lambda = 1$;
    \item \textbf{KL $+\,\X_\mu+\,\X_{\hat\mu}$}: also adding the wide-coverage regularizer ($\alpha = 1$, $\beta = 0$);
    \item \textbf{KL $+\,\X_\mu+\,\X_{(\hat\mu + \bar\nu)/2}$}: the preferred mixture, with $\alpha = \beta = 1/2$.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Himmelblau (mode-coverage regime).} The source places nearly all its mass near the central saddle, so the initial pushforward is far from any of the four Himmelblau modes. Forward KL and forward KL $+\,\X_\mu$ both converge to a flow that covers only two of the four modes (coverage $\approx 0.53$ in both cases), and the ESS alone gives no warning that the other two modes are missing. Adding $\X_{\hat\mu}$ steers mass toward the QT-discovered modes and recovers coverage $1.00$ (Figure~\ref{fig: himmelblau}). The mixture variant $\X_{(\hat\mu+\bar\nu)/2}$ also reaches coverage $1.00$ and attains the highest ESS of the four. This case confirms the role of $\X_{\hat\mu}$ (or its mixture extension) as the safeguard one needs precisely when $\mu_0$ does not by itself cover all modes of $\mu$ --- the regime in which the forward KL is silent.
