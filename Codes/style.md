# Reading the X-regularization results — the story across the `Codes/` benchmarks

The four benchmarks in `Codes/` tell one story about the X-regularized forward KL, and every results write-up should present its numbers in service of that story. The forward KL objective trains a flow's pushforward to match a target, and three ingredients repair its three characteristic failures: $\mathrm{X}_\mu$ sharpens accuracy, $\mathrm{X}_{\hat\mu}$ finds the modes the source never reaches, and $\bar\nu$ — the anti-leakage half of the discovery mixture — keeps the flow from leaking mass between them. The three single-step benchmarks isolate and verify each mechanism; the annealed clock model then stress-tests the whole objective on a multimodal target reached through an annealing ladder. There the X-regularized advantage extends to large batches, with one exception — the last rungs at the smallest batch, where the real difficulty is transporting mass between separated modes and the X-regularized loss can cede to the bare forward KL.

## The three ingredients

$\mathrm{X}_\mu$ is the accuracy term. The bare forward KL matches only the mean of the log-ratio; $\mathrm{X}_\mu$ adds a pairwise term that constrains its spread, and the gain is largest exactly where the forward KL gradient is noisiest — at small batch and high dimension. Its minimizer is still the same target, so it never changes what the flow converges to, only how tightly it fits.

$\mathrm{X}_{\hat\mu}$ is the discovery term. A forward KL flow trained on AIS batches initialized at its own pushforward only ever sees the modes it already covers, so a mode the source cannot reach stays invisible while the flow reports a high ESS on the support it does reach. Quench and temper melts the target and runs an optimizer over its geometry, landing $\hat\mu$ particles in every basin regardless of that basin's probability; feeding those particles into the loss forces the flow to cover the missed modes.

$\bar\nu$ is the anti-leakage term. Aggressive discovery carries a calibration cost: forced to bridge to a far or tight mode, the flow threads a thin trail of mass through the low-probability region between modes, and that leaked mass depresses the ESS. The $\bar\nu$ half samples the flow's own pushforward and penalizes exactly that leaked mass. The equal-weight mixture $(\hat\mu + \bar\nu)/2$ combines discovery and anti-leakage, and is the strongest overall setting.

## What the single-step benchmarks verify

Across the three single-step benchmarks — the 2D targets, the high-dimensional product multi-well, and the tilted $\phi^4$ lattice — the batch is kept small, smallest in Two-Moon, because that is where the bare forward KL gradient is noisiest and so where the accuracy gap to its X-regularized versions is widest. Discovery and anti-leakage, by contrast, surface wherever the source cannot reach a mode, at any batch size.

The 2D targets separate the mechanisms one at a time. Two-Moon, at a batch of $200$, isolates accuracy: no mode is missed, and $\mathrm{X}_\mu$ lifts the ESS from $0.80$ to $0.92$ at no extra flow evaluations. Three-Well, Himmelblau, and Sparse are collapse benchmarks of rising severity, and each carries the same lesson — the ESS alone cannot see a missing mode. The bare forward KL reports ESS near $0.96$–$0.98$ while covering only half to three-quarters of the target; the paired coverage metric exposes the miss, and the $\hat\mu$ mixtures restore essentially full coverage. Where discovery costs calibration (Himmelblau, Sparse), the $\bar\nu$ half buys most of it back, closing the ESS gap the $\hat\mu$-only run opens.

The high-dimensional product multi-well isolates the accuracy scaling: every loss finds all $2^k$ modes, so the story is the decay of the ESS with dimension. The forward KL falls from $0.97$ at $d = 2$ to $0.42$ at $d = 256$ as the per-coordinate fit error compounds in the log-weights; $\mathrm{X}_\mu$ lifts every column, and the $(\hat\mu + \bar\nu)/2$ mixture is the best loss at every dimension, reaching $0.60$ at $d = 256$ — a $1.4\times$ uprise over the bare forward KL.

The tilted $\phi^4$ lattice is the sharpest fake-ESS case: two $\mathbb Z_2$ vacua split by a collective barrier. The bare forward KL and $\mathrm{X}_\mu$ collapse onto one vacuum, with the minority weight $p_+$ pinned at $0$ or $1$; because sharpening the single captured vacuum is exactly what raises the ESS, $\mathrm{X}_\mu$ reports the *highest* ESS of the four losses — best precisely where the collapse is most severe. The quench-and-temper mixtures recover both vacua, landing $p_+$ within $0.004$ of the reference at both sizes, at a visible ESS cost, since covering both vacua forces a low-weight bridge across the barrier; the $\bar\nu$ half buys back part of that cost.

Together these establish the design: $\mathrm{X}_\mu$ for accuracy, $\hat\mu$ for discovery, $\bar\nu$ against leakage, each isolated by the benchmark that stresses it and best combined in the equal-weight mixture.

## What the clock model changes

The clock model is the harder test: a $p = 6$ clock field on the $8 \times 8$ lattice, cooled along an annealing ladder from the uniform torus to a cold measure that splits into six symmetry-related sectors. Two features set it apart from the single-step benchmarks.

First, the advantage of the X-regularized loss no longer needs a small batch to appear. The error-propagation factor $F = \prod_k \mathrm{ESS}_k^{-1}$ — the product of the per-rung inverse ESS, the estimated factor by which the staged reweighting enlarges the Monte Carlo error — is $2.6$ to $3.6\times$ smaller for KL+$\mathrm{X}_\mu$+$\mathrm{X}_{(\hat\mu + \bar\nu)/2}$ than for the bare forward KL at every batch size, and still $2.8\times$ smaller at the largest $B = 2000$. On this annealed, multimodal target the X-regularization keeps its advantage at every batch size, the largest included.

Second, the clock exposes the one regime where the X-regularized loss can fall behind the bare forward KL: the last rungs of the ladder ($t \to 1$) at the smallest batch. The core difficulty has changed there. Early and midway along the ladder the deformation is large but smooth, and accuracy is the binding constraint — where the X-regularization helps. As $t \to 1$ the sectors separate, and the remaining task is no longer to fit a shape but to move probability mass between them. A trained flow is a diffeomorphism: it preserves the connectedness of its input, so it cannot shift population between separated sectors except by threading a near-zero-weight bridge through the low-density gap. That redistribution is instead accomplished by reweighting. Because reweighting is common to both losses, the forward KL and KL+$\mathrm{X}_\mu$+$\mathrm{X}_{(\hat\mu + \bar\nu)/2}$ behave alike on these late rungs.

In this regime the discovery mechanism can even mislead. Quench and temper detects the missed modes but does not assign them their correct weights; when the true difficulty is inter-mode weight transport, those wrongly-weighted discovery samples steer the X-regularized loss below the bare forward KL. The effect is confined to the extreme corner — $t \to 1$ and very small batch: the reversal appears only at $B = 125$, and by $B \geq 1000$ the X-regularized loss again leads on every rung. Its geometric-mean per-rung gap and its $F$ advantage survive at every batch size we ran.

## Presenting the numbers

The story dictates how the numbers are shown. ESS is never reported alone on a multimodal target, because a collapsed flow reports a high ESS on the support it reaches; it is always paired with the diagnostic that sees the miss — coverage on the 2D and product targets, the reweighted $p_+$ on $\phi^4$, sector occupancy on the clock. On the annealed clock the per-rung ESS and their product $F$ are the trustworthy summaries; a single composed end-to-end ESS is not. On the clock every configuration is run once, so per-rung values carry single-run fluctuation, and cross-run trends are read from aggregates — geometric means and $F$ — rather than from individual cells.
